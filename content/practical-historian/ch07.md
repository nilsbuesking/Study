# Chapter 7 — Forms of Output: Genre, Constraint, and Assessment

## 7.1 The output genre as a constraint structure

Chapter 2 opened with the principle that the genre of a *source* is information you possess before you read a word of it: knowing what kind of document you hold tells you, in advance, how it was produced and where to aim your suspicion. This chapter opens with the mirror of that principle, at the other end of the work. The genre of an *output* is a constraint structure you must read before you write a word of it: knowing what kind of thing you are producing — a writing sample, a dissertation, an article, a proposal, a popular book — tells you, in advance, what the piece is *for*, what limits it operates under, what will count as success, and therefore what "good" even means for this particular deliverable. The two principles bracket the whole enterprise. You read the genre of your evidence before you interpret it; you read the genre of your output before you build it; and in both cases the genre is not a container into which fixed content is poured but a structure that determines what the content can and must be.

The mistake this chapter exists to forestall is the assumption that good history is a single substance that one simply produces in larger or smaller quantities depending on the occasion — that a writing sample is a small dissertation, a dissertation a long article, an article a short book, and a popular book a watered-down monograph. It is not so. Each genre answers a different question for a different reader, and the success condition that follows from that question reshapes everything: how broad the topic may be, how original the argument must be, how much apparatus is shown, how the work is judged and by whom. A piece that would be excellent as one genre can be a failure as another without a single fact changing, exactly as a source that is poor evidence for one question is decisive for another (1.1). The governing questions for any output are therefore three, and they should be answered explicitly before drafting begins: who reads this, why do they read it, and what must it achieve for them? The answers fix the constraint structure, and the constraint structure dictates the work.

Two cross-cutting disciplines follow from those questions and apply across every genre, so it is worth treating them before the genres themselves.

## 7.2 Two cross-cutting disciplines: scope, and the competence–contribution distinction

The first cross-cutting discipline concerns **scope**, and the single most common practical failure across every genre of historical output is over-scoping: attempting more than the word limit, the timeframe, the available sources, and — for work produced in training — the writer's current command can sustain. Over-scoping is seductive because the broad topic feels more important and more impressive than the narrow one, and it is fatal because importance gestured at is not importance achieved. A question wide enough to be impressive in the abstract cannot, within real constraints, be answered with the depth that makes history good; the result is thin, superficial, a survey of a territory rather than an argument about a problem, and it does justice neither to the topic nor to the writer. The discipline is the one 4.2 named for the thesis and 4.7 named for selection, applied now to the whole project: scope to the constraints, ruthlessly, and prefer a narrow question answered fully to a broad one merely indicated. The narrowing is not a retreat from ambition; it is the precondition of achieving anything at all, because depth is where the quality of historical work lives, and depth is purchased only by limiting breadth. This is the abstract principle behind the concrete calls that recur in practice — the decision to focus a writing sample tightly, or to narrow a dissertation's question so that it cannot wander off course. In each case the move is the same: fit the scope to the resources so that the work can actually be done to a standard, rather than reaching for a scope the resources cannot honour.

The second cross-cutting discipline is the master distinction that organises the whole chapter: the difference between outputs that **demonstrate competence** and outputs that **advance knowledge**. The apprentice genres — the writing sample, the undergraduate essay, the examination, the MA dissertation — are assessed against a standard of demonstrated competence; the question they answer for their reader is *can this person do history well?*, and originality, while always welcome, is not their success condition. The professional genres — the journal article, the scholarly monograph — are judged by whether they advance knowledge; the question they answer is *does this contribute something the field did not have?*, and competence is assumed as the price of entry rather than counted as the achievement. The distinction matters because confusing the two registers is a category error in both directions, and a costly one. To attempt, in a competence genre, the original contribution proper to a professional one is to over-scope in the most fundamental way — to chase a kind of success the genre does not require and the constraints cannot support, at the expense of the execution the genre does require and reward. To treat a professional genre as a mere demonstration of competence is the opposite failure, under-ambition, producing technically sound work that contributes nothing and is rejected for precisely that. Reading an output's genre correctly means, above all, knowing which register it belongs to, because that determines whether you are being asked to show that you can do the work or to do work that has not been done before. The doctoral dissertation, as we shall see, is the genre defined by sitting exactly on the boundary between the two, and much of what makes it difficult is that it must satisfy both registers at once.

With those two disciplines in hand, the genres can be taken in turn. For each, the questions are the same: what is it for, what constrains it, what makes it qualitatively good, and — for the assessed genres — what the assessor is durably looking for. The assessment material stays at the level of what examiners are fundamentally evaluating rather than reproducing any particular marking scheme, because the schemes vary by institution and date quickly, while the underlying thing being assessed does not: it is, in every case, the operations of this handbook, executed with judgement and scoped appropriately to the genre's success condition.

## 7.3 The writing sample

The writing sample exists to demonstrate, to an assessor evaluating potential rather than a peer evaluating a contribution, that the writer can do history: that they can criticise a source, build an argument with warrants, engage a historiographical conversation, and write controlled prose. It is the competence genre in its purest form, because its entire purpose is evidentiary — it is offered as evidence of capacity, usually in support of an application, and read by people deciding whether the writer is worth admitting or funding. The reader is not asking whether the writer has discovered something new; they are asking whether the writer can be trusted to do the work that the place or the funding would enable.

The characteristic constraint follows directly: the sample is short relative to the research genres, and its success condition is the visible, full execution of the operations, not the originality of the conclusion or the breadth of the coverage. This is what makes the strategically correct choice counter-intuitive to many applicants, who imagine they must dazzle with a bold and wide-ranging thesis. The opposite is true, and the reasoning is exact. Because the sample is assessed on demonstrated competence, and because competence is shown by executing the operations in full, the sample should take a problem narrow enough that full execution is achievable within the length — narrow enough that the source criticism can actually be done, the historiographical positioning actually established, the argument actually warranted, all to completion and on the page. A sample that reaches for a grand original argument across a broad field cannot execute the operations in full within its length; it will show rushed source work, hand-waved historiography, and assertions without warrants, and it will therefore demonstrate the very incompetence it was meant to disprove. A sample that takes a contained, even modest, problem and executes every operation cleanly demonstrates exactly what the genre asks. The focus on mechanics — on showing the source criticism and the argument-construction and the positioning, rather than on the novelty of the result — is not a limitation reluctantly accepted; it is the optimal strategy for the genre's success condition, because you are being assessed on whether you can do the work, and so the right move is to show the work, in full, on something small enough that "in full" is possible.

What an assessor of a writing sample is looking for, durably, is therefore evidence of the trainable capacities: the critical handling of sources rather than their naive use; a real argument, however bounded, with its warrants supplied (4.3); an awareness that the writer is operating within a historiographical conversation rather than in a vacuum (6.7); control of analytical prose (Chapter 5); and the judgement to scope the piece so that all of this can be shown. An original finding is a welcome bonus and never the criterion, and an applicant who sacrifices clean execution in pursuit of originality has misread the genre and will be marked down for the rushed execution far more than rewarded for the ambition. The sample is a demonstration; the thing to demonstrate is that you can already do, on a small scale and under your own steam, what the training will teach you to do on a larger one.

## 7.4 The MA dissertation

The taught-master's dissertation is the capstone of postgraduate training and demonstrates a capacity the writing sample does not test: the ability to conceive and execute an independent research project, of limited scope, from question to conclusion. It remains a competence genre — it is assessed against a standard, and the question it answers for its examiners is *can this person do independent research well?* — but the competence it demonstrates is of a higher order than the sample's, because it requires the writer to generate the project rather than to analyse given material, and to sustain the operations across a longer and more demanding piece.

The characteristic constraint is a fixed and fairly tight word limit set against a fixed and usually short timeframe — frequently a matter of months — and it is against this constraint that the over-scoping temptation of 7.2 becomes acute and, for the master's dissertation specifically, the most common cause of failure. The discipline that answers it is the narrowing of the question to the point where it can actually be answered within the constraints, with genuine primary research, to a real depth. This is the abstract principle behind the practical instinct to keep a dissertation's question tight enough that the project cannot wander off course: a narrow, answerable question, scoped to the word count and the timeframe and the sources within reach, produces a dissertation that can do justice to itself — that can criticise its sources properly, mount a sustained argument, and reach a defensible conclusion. A question broad enough to be interesting in the abstract produces, within the same constraints, a thin survey that gestures at depth it has no room to achieve, and examiners read the thinness as a failure of research judgement, which is precisely the capacity the genre is testing. Better a small question answered fully than a large one indicated; the narrowing is the research judgement, not a substitute for it.

What examiners of a master's dissertation look for, durably, is a clear and answerable and well-scoped question; genuine engagement with primary sources rather than a synthesis of secondary literature wearing the costume of research; a sustained argument across the length (the 4.4 paragraph-as-move discipline maintained over many pages); competent historiographical positioning that locates the contribution the dissertation makes, however modest; and, threaded through all of it, the scoping judgement that fitted the project to the constraints. The single failure they most reliably penalise is over-scoping, because it is both the most common and the most diagnostic of poor research judgement. And here the maturity that 7.8 will identify in the proposal genre applies equally: stating the limits of what the dissertation has established, and the outcomes that remain open, reads as scholarly self-awareness rather than as weakness — an examiner trusts a writer who knows the boundaries of their own findings more than one who claims to have settled more than the evidence and the scope allow.

## 7.5 The PhD dissertation

The doctoral dissertation is the genre defined by the boundary of 7.2: it is at once the final apprentice piece and the first professional contribution, and it must satisfy both registers simultaneously. It must demonstrate that the candidate has become a capable independent researcher, fit to join the profession — the competence register, raised to its highest level — and it must also clear the bar that the apprentice genres do not impose, the bar of original contribution. In the British system the formal criterion is precisely this: an original contribution to knowledge. The dissertation must offer the field something it did not previously have.

The shift in register changes what the central hazard is. For the master's dissertation, the governing danger was over-scoping against a tight word limit; the doctoral dissertation, written at far greater length over years, faces a different and deeper version of the problem, which is whether the contribution is genuine, defensible, and sustained as the candidate's own across the whole. This is where the contribution-versus-redescription test of 6.6 bites hardest, because it is the test the genre is built around. The doctoral candidate must be able to say what the dissertation makes newly visible or newly decidable — what is true, or answerable, or readable in the evidence under their argument that was not so before — and not merely what new vocabulary it introduces. A "contribution" that dissolves, under examination, into the redescription of what was already known in more congenial terms is the characteristic way a doctoral thesis fails to clear its bar while appearing, to its author, to have done so. The contribution must be cashable into a different handling of the evidence, and the candidate must be able to demonstrate the cashing.

Scope remains a discipline even here, though inverted from the master's case: the doctoral dissertation succeeds by establishing a focused contribution thoroughly, not by attempting a sprawling one thinly, and the candidates who struggle are often those whose ambition outran the years available, leaving a contribution gestured at rather than demonstrated. What makes a doctoral dissertation good is therefore a genuine and defensible original contribution, scoped so that it can be fully established; resting on substantial primary research sufficient to support it; situated with precision in the historiography, so that the contribution's relation to existing work is exact rather than vague; and sustained as a single coherent argument across the length, so that the whole is one thesis rather than a set of loosely related studies. The assessment, in the viva, interrogates exactly these things: whether the contribution is original and significant, whether it is genuinely the candidate's own, whether the research is sound and sufficient to bear it, whether the candidate can defend it against objection and locate it accurately in the field, and whether, in producing it, the candidate has demonstrably become an independent researcher. The question "what is new here?" is asked directly and answered well only by a candidate who has held themselves to the 6.6 test throughout — who knows, and can articulate crisply, what their thesis contributes that redescription would not.

## 7.6 The journal article

The journal article is the primary unit of professional knowledge-advancement: the genre in which a single, focused, original contribution is made to the literature and addressed to specialist peers. It is a professional genre without ambiguity — competence is assumed, contribution is the point — and it is gatekept by peer review, which enforces the standards of the field on every piece that enters it. Its reader is the specialist, and increasingly the abstract-reader and the searcher, but its first and decisive reader is the reviewer, whose verdict determines whether the article appears at all.

The characteristic constraint is a tight word limit that forces the article to make *one* contribution and make it sharply. This is the genre's discipline and its difficulty: an article that tries to make three contributions makes none of them well, because the length will not allow three to be established to the standard the field requires, and the reviewers will find each of the three underdeveloped. Focus is therefore not merely a virtue but the structural requirement of the form — one article, one argument, one contribution, fully positioned and fully evidenced. The positioning is done by the literature-review-as-argument of 6.7, which in an article is rarely a separate section but is woven through the framing, doing the work of showing exactly the gap or tension the article fills, so that by the time the contribution is stated the reader sees precisely why it is needed. And the contribution must be cashable in the 6.6 sense; an article whose novelty is purely terminological will be seen through by reviewers whose business is to know the field well enough to recognise redescription.

What makes a journal article good is a single clear contribution, precisely located against the literature, economically argued, fully supported by evidence, and claimed at no more than the strength the evidence warrants — the calibration of claim to evidence applies with particular force, because reviewers are alert to over-claiming and an article that promises more than it delivers is rejected or sent back for the gap between claim and evidence to be closed. What peer review assesses, durably, is the originality and significance of the contribution (is this worth adding to the field?), the soundness of the method and the sufficiency of the evidence (is it true and shown?), the command of the relevant literature (does the author know the conversation they are joining?), and the proportion between claim and warrant (is what is asserted what is supported?). The verdict turns on a single composite question: is this a real, sound, and sufficiently significant contribution to this field? An article that can answer yes to all three — real, sound, significant — clears the bar; one that fails any of them does not, however well executed in the others.

## 7.7 The scholarly monograph

The monograph is the extended professional contribution: a sustained, large-scale argument or a comprehensive reinterpretation, the genre in which the major moves of a field are often made, and frequently the form into which a doctoral dissertation is eventually transformed. Its length permits — and therefore demands — what the article cannot attempt: a single thesis developed cumulatively across many chapters, each advancing the whole. The reader is the field and the educated specialist, and the standard is the highest the discipline imposes outside the article's gatekeeping, because a book makes a larger claim on the field's attention and is held to it.

The characteristic constraint is, paradoxically, the freedom that the length provides, because scale introduces a problem the shorter genres escape: the problem of coherence across length. A monograph must be one argument, not a collection of loosely related studies bound between the same covers, and the central architectural challenge is to keep a single thesis coherent across a hundred thousand words and many sub-arguments, so that each chapter is a move in one cumulative case rather than a free-standing essay. This is the 4.4 principle — the paragraph as a move in the argument — scaled up to the chapter as a move in the book: just as one should be able to state what each paragraph does to an essay's argument, one should be able to state what each chapter does to the book's, and a monograph in which the chapters cannot be so described has reverted, at the largest scale, to the container of 4.1. The risk that scale introduces is precisely the loosely-bound collection that presents itself as a unified argument, and the discipline that answers it is the maintenance of the cumulative thesis as the spine to which every chapter attaches.

What makes a monograph good is a large, original, and sustained argument that genuinely coheres across its length, where each chapter advances the cumulative thesis; deep research sufficient to support a claim of that scale; comprehensive positioning within the field; and the architectural control that holds the whole together as one case. It is assessed — by scholarly reviews after publication, and for academic presses by peer review of the manuscript before it — on the originality and significance of the overall contribution, the soundness and depth of the research, the coherence of the argument across the whole, and the author's command of the field. The monograph's distinctive test, beyond those it shares with the article, is that last one of coherence at scale: a book can be original, sound, and learned and still fail as a book if it does not cohere, if the parts do not add to a single argument greater than their sum.

## 7.8 The research proposal

The research proposal is unlike every other genre in this chapter, because it argues about work that has not yet been done. It is a bid for resources — a place, funding, supervision — addressed to assessors deciding whether to invest in a project, and judged not on results, which do not exist, but on promise and feasibility. This single fact reshapes its entire constraint structure: a proposal cannot be assessed on what it has found, so it is assessed on a distinctive triad of whether the question is worth answering, whether the project can actually be done, and whether this person can do it.

The three criteria deserve naming, because proposals fail by neglecting any one of them. **Significance**: is the question worth answering — does it promise a genuine contribution, in the 6.6 sense, rather than the confirmation of the already-known? **Feasibility**: can the project actually be done, with the time, the sources, the access, and the skills available — is the archive reachable, the language readable, the scope achievable in the period proposed? Feasibility is the most underrated of the three and a frequent ground of rejection, because assessors who have watched projects founder know that a brilliant question attached to an undoable plan is a brilliant way to fail, and they decline to fund the failure however attractive the question. **Capacity**: does the proposer demonstrate, through their command of the field and the sharpness of their framing, that they are the person who can carry the project out? The proposal must show enough command of the literature and the sources to be credible while leaving the outcome genuinely open, and balancing these is the genre's central difficulty.

That balance is where the maturity principle lives, and it inverts a novice instinct. The temptation is to promise a result — to write as though the research will certainly vindicate a predetermined thesis — on the assumption that confidence persuades. It does the reverse. A proposal that promises a foregone conclusion reads as either naive about what research is (if you already know the answer, why is the research needed?) or as intending to confirm rather than to inquire, which is not research at all. The mature proposal states its outcome space honestly: here is the question, here is what I expect I may find, here are the findings that would complicate or refute my hypothesis, and here is why the question is worth answering whichever way the evidence falls. Stating the open outcomes, including the ones uncongenial to the proposer's hypothesis, reads as scholarly self-awareness and genuine inquiry — exactly the discipline of holding the congenial explanation under extra suspicion, performed in advance and in public — and it persuades assessors precisely because it demonstrates that the proposer understands research to be a question pursued rather than a conclusion illustrated. What makes a proposal good, then, is a sharp and significant and answerable question; a feasible plan with the sources, methods, timeline, and access specified credibly; evidence of command of the field; appropriate scoping (an over-scoped proposal signals poor judgement about what research involves, the 7.2 failure projected forward); and an honestly stated open outcome space. Assessment weighs significance, feasibility, and capacity together, and a proposal strong on the question but weak on feasibility fails as surely as one feasible but insignificant.

## 7.9 Popular history

Popular history conveys historical understanding to a general, non-specialist audience, and it is judged by accessibility, narrative power, and engagement alongside accuracy. Its reader lacks specialist background and reads by choice rather than obligation, which means the writing must earn and hold attention that a captive scholarly reader would grant regardless. The characteristic constraint is the relaxation or removal of the scholarly apparatus: footnotes minimised or banished, historiographical debate pushed into the background or omitted, narrative brought to the foreground, technical vocabulary translated or avoided.

The crucial point about this genre — and the one most worth stating at the level of principle, because it is so often got exactly backwards — is that the relaxation of the apparatus does not relax the underlying honesty, and that in one crucial respect popular history is therefore harder, not easier, than scholarly history. It is harder in that respect precisely because nothing external enforces the discipline once the apparatus is gone. The scholarly genres are policed from outside: peer review, the footnote that exposes every claim to checking (5.8), the examiner, the reviewer who knows the field. Strip those away and the integrity of the work depends entirely on the internal discipline of the writer, who could, with no external check, simplify into falsehood, manufacture a certainty the evidence does not support because qualification reads as tedious to a general reader, impose a clean and satisfying narrative on a reality that was messy and contingent, or let a good story override the evidence that complicates it — and no footnote will betray the lapse, because there are no footnotes. The temptations are specific and strong: the teleology that 2.5 identified in memoir operates here as the pull toward the tidy arc, the smoothing of contingency into inevitability because inevitability tells better; the false precision warned against earlier operates as the suppression of the qualifier because the qualified claim is less gripping than the bald one. The good popular historian resists all of this while showing none of the resistance: they perform the full source criticism of Chapters 1 through 6 invisibly, behind the prose, and present the verified result accessibly, without displaying the working, while never claiming more than the hidden working supports.

What makes popular history good, then, is accuracy and honesty fully preserved beneath an accessible and engaging surface; narrative skill that serves understanding rather than distorting it; the difficult judgement of what may be simplified and what must remain complex — the distinction between simplification that clarifies and simplification that falsifies, which is among the hardest judgements in the genre; and the integrity to qualify where the evidence demands even at the cost of narrative momentum. The genre has no formal examination, but it is judged in reception — by general critics on its readability and reach, and frequently by specialists on its accuracy — and the reputational cost of getting the history wrong in public is real and lasting. The proper test of good popular history is exacting: that a specialist could read it and find nothing dishonest in it, while a general reader finds it gripping. Meeting both halves of that test at once is why the genre is difficult, and why the contempt in which popular history is sometimes held by scholars is misplaced — done well, it requires the full scholarly discipline plus a craft of presentation the scholarly genres never demand.

## 7.10 The abstract

The abstract deserves separate treatment, because it is a genre-component in its own right, with conventions distinct from the work it accompanies, and because it is the single most-read and least-carefully-written part of most scholarly output. It is governed by an asymmetry that defines it: it is written last and read first. It is written last because you cannot summarise an argument you have not finished making — the abstract is the distillation of the completed work, and an abstract drafted before the work is done is a description of intentions, not a summary of findings. And it is read first because it is the reader's entry point and triage device: the thing by which a reader, often searching or browsing, decides whether to read the full piece at all — and for many readers, the abstract is the *only* part read, which means it must stand entirely on its own and convey the essential content without reference to the body it summarises.

The cardinal distinction in writing an abstract, and the source of the most common failure, is between the **descriptive** and the **informative** abstract. A descriptive abstract states what the piece is *about* — what it examines, addresses, considers, explores: "This article examines the role of the press in the politics of the period." It describes the territory and the approach while withholding the findings, and it is almost always the wrong choice for scholarly work, because it tells the reader the subject without telling them what the piece actually *says* about it. An informative abstract states what the piece *argues and finds*: "This article argues that the press functioned less as a reporter of events than as an instrument of proprietorial politics, and shows, through X, that Y." It gives the actual content — the thesis, the basis, the finding — and it is what a scholarly abstract should be. The characteristic novice error is to write a descriptive abstract under the impression that it is informative: to state the question, the sources, and the method, and then to stop, omitting the very thing the reader most needs, which is the argument and the findings. The reader of such an abstract learns what the piece is about and not what it concludes, and so cannot use the abstract for its purpose. The corrective is blunt: state your thesis and your findings in the abstract, not merely your topic and your method. The finding is the part to be sure you have included, because it is the part most often left out.

A good informative abstract contains, compressed: the problem or question (what gap or tension the work addresses); the approach (the sources and method, briefly, enough to establish credibility but no more); the argument or thesis (what you claim); the findings or contribution (what you show — again, the part novices omit); and, often, the significance (why it matters to the field). It is self-contained, free of citations, and free of any jargon that does not stand on its own without the body to explain it. And its content varies by the genre it summarises, mirroring this chapter's whole theme: an article abstract states the single contribution; a dissertation abstract summarises the cumulative argument and its overall contribution across the length; and a proposal's summary, because there are no findings to report, is necessarily forward-looking — it states the question, its significance, the approach, and the open outcome space (7.8), and it cannot and must not pretend to findings it does not yet have. The abstract's content is thus dictated by the genre's success condition exactly as the body's is: where the genre's success is a contribution, the abstract states the contribution; where the genre's success is a promising and feasible plan, the abstract states the plan and its promise.

One discipline binds all of this: the abstract must accurately represent the piece. The calibration discipline applies with full force here, because the abstract is a promise the body must keep. An abstract that over-claims — that promises a finding the piece only gestures at, or a certainty the evidence does not reach — commits a small dishonesty that irritates every reader who reads on and finds the promise unmet, and it is a particularly self-defeating dishonesty because the reader is, by definition, about to check it. An abstract that under-sells, burying or omitting the real contribution out of misplaced modesty, fails differently, by deterring the readers the work deserves. The abstract should say exactly what the piece does, at exactly the strength the piece supports, in a form that stands alone — and writing it well, last, is the final act of the calibration that the whole handbook has been teaching: claiming neither more nor less than the work has earned.

## 7.11 Main conclusions

-   **The genre of an output is a constraint structure to read before you write,** as the genre of a source (2.1) is information to read before you interpret. Who reads this, why, and what it must achieve for them fixes the constraints, and the constraints dictate what "good" means for the piece.

-   **Over-scoping is the cardinal cross-cutting failure.** Depth, where the quality of history lives, is bought only by limiting breadth; scope to the constraints ruthlessly, and prefer a narrow question answered fully to a broad one indicated. The narrowing is research judgement, not a retreat from it.

-   **Distinguish competence genres from contribution genres,** and never confuse the registers. The writing sample, the MA dissertation, and the like are assessed on demonstrated competence (*can this person do history well?*); the article and the monograph are judged by contribution (*does this advance the field?*). Chasing contribution in a competence genre is over-scoping; treating a contribution genre as mere competence is under-ambition.

-   **The writing sample is optimised by full execution on a narrow problem,** not by a bold original thesis: assessed on demonstrated competence, it should show every operation in full on something small enough that "in full" is achievable. Mechanics-focus is the genre's optimal strategy, not a limitation.

-   **The MA dissertation lives or dies by the answerable, well-scoped question;** over-scoping is the failure examiners most reliably penalise, because it is the most diagnostic of poor research judgement. Stating limits and open outcomes reads as maturity.

-   **The PhD sits on the competence–contribution boundary and must satisfy both,** clearing the original-contribution bar where the 6.6 redescription test bites hardest: the candidate must articulate what the thesis makes newly visible or decidable, not merely what new vocabulary it deploys.

-   **The article makes one sharp contribution, fully positioned and evidenced;** the monograph makes a sustained one that must cohere across its length (chapter-as-move, the 4.4 principle at scale). Both are judged on originality, soundness, and command of the field, with claims calibrated to evidence.

-   **The proposal argues about undone work and is judged on significance, feasibility, and capacity.** Feasibility is the underrated criterion; honestly stating the open outcome space (including uncongenial findings) reads as maturity and genuine inquiry, not weakness.

-   **Popular history is, in one crucial respect, harder rather than easier than scholarly history,** because removing the apparatus removes the external enforcement of honesty, leaving the discipline entirely internal. The good popular historian does the full source criticism invisibly and claims no more than the hidden working supports: a specialist should find nothing dishonest, a general reader should find it gripping.

-   **Write the abstract informatively — state the argument and findings, not just the topic and method** — last, because it distils completed work, and self-containedly, because it is read first and often alone. Match its content to the genre's success condition, and calibrate it to claim exactly what the piece delivers.

*Germano, William (2013) —* From Dissertation to Book*.* The clearest treatment of the difference between two genres treated here — the doctoral dissertation and the scholarly monograph — and of what transforming one into the other actually requires, which is precisely a change of constraint structure and audience rather than mere expansion. The best single illustration of this chapter's governing claim that genre reshapes content.

*Phillips, Estelle M., and Derek S. Pugh —* How to Get a PhD: A Handbook for Students and Their Supervisors*. The standard guide to the doctoral process and, more usefully for 7.5, to what the doctorate is actually* for and what examiners are assessing — the competence-and-contribution double standard made concrete, and the realities of the viva. Read it for the demystification of the genre's expectations.

*Belcher, Wendy Laura (2019) —* Writing Your Journal Article in Twelve Weeks*.* Practical and widely used, and good beyond its scheduling conceit on what the article genre demands: the single focused contribution, the positioning against the literature, the calibration of claim to evidence, and the realities of peer review. The companion to 7.6.

*Swales, John M., and Christine B. Feak —* Academic Writing for Graduate Students*.* Strong on the academic genres as genres, and notably on the abstract — including the descriptive-versus-informative distinction of 7.10 and the components of an effective summary. The reference for the abstract material, and useful more broadly on the conventions that govern scholarly forms.
