# Chapter 4 — Essay and Argument Construction

## 4.1 The essay is an argument, not a container

The first three chapters were about evidence: what a source can bear, how to read a genre, what numbers do and do not establish. This chapter is about what you do with the evidence once you have criticised it, and the whole of it follows from a single proposition: **a historical essay is an argument, and an argument is the written form of an inference to the best explanation.** 1.5 set out the structure of that inference — you assemble evidence, propose the explanation that accounts for it better than the available rivals, and hold the conclusion comparatively and provisionally. An essay is that inference made explicit and defended on the page. Everything about its construction — what a thesis is, how a paragraph works, how counterevidence is handled, when a source is quoted — derives from the demands of mounting that inference persuasively.

The commonest failure in undergraduate and even graduate writing is to mistake the essay for a container: a vessel into which everything known about a topic is poured, organised by chronology, or by source, or by sub-topic, with the connective reasoning left for the reader to supply. The container essay surveys; it does not argue. It can be learned, well-researched, and entirely competent at the level of fact, and still fail completely as history, because it never proposes an explanation and never shows that explanation to be better than the alternatives. The distinction between the container and the argument is the distinction this chapter exists to teach, and it is worth stating the test up front: **an argument advances; a survey accumulates.** In an argument, each paragraph changes what has been established, so that the reader is in a different position at its end than at its beginning. In a survey, each paragraph adds another fact to a pile, and the reader at the end knows more but has been taken nowhere. If you cannot say how your essay leaves the reader in a different position from where they started — not better informed, but argumentatively further along — you have written a container.

## 4.2 Topic versus thesis

The move from container to argument begins with the distinction between a topic and a thesis, and it is the single most important distinction in this chapter.

A topic is a subject area: "the failure of the 1848 revolutions," "the causes of the Irish Famine," "Bismarck's foreign policy." A thesis is a contestable claim about that subject: a proposition that an intelligent, informed person could deny, and that you intend to defend against the denial. The decisive test is deniability. If no informed person could disagree with your central statement, it is not a thesis but a description, and an essay organised around it cannot argue, because there is nothing to argue. "The 1848 revolutions failed for a variety of reasons" is undeniable and therefore worthless as a thesis; "the 1848 revolutions failed primarily because the liberal and radical wings of the movement fractured once their common monarchical enemy was checked, and this internal solvent mattered more than the much-discussed loyalty of the conservative armies" is contestable, specific, and arguable — a thesis, because a competent historian could resist it and you would have to make a case.

A good thesis has three properties beyond deniability. It is *specific*: it names what it claims rather than gesturing at complexity (the retreat into "a variety of factors" is the characteristic evasion of a writer who has not decided what they think). It is *answerable with the available evidence*: a thesis that the evidence cannot test — because the sources do not exist, or because it asks for a precision the evidence cannot bear — is a thesis you cannot defend, however interesting. And it is *proportionate*: it claims neither more than the evidence supports (the overreach of 1.1's leaching) nor so little that it asserts nothing (the hedge that abandons the claim, treated in 4.7).

The bridge from topic to thesis is the question, and this is where 1.1 — the question is prior to the evidence — becomes a practical instruction rather than an epistemological observation. A sharply posed question generates a thesis as its answer. "Why did the 1848 revolutions fail?" is a better starting point than "the 1848 revolutions," because it demands an answer, and the answer, once you have decided on it and qualified it properly, is your thesis. The discipline at the outset of any essay is therefore to convert your topic into a question precise enough that answering it commits you to a contestable claim. If your question can be answered "it's complicated," sharpen it until it cannot.

## 4.3 The anatomy of a claim: evidence, and the missing warrant

Once you have a thesis, the essay is built from claims — the thesis itself and the subsidiary claims that support it — and it is worth being precise about what a claim, fully made, actually contains, because the place where historical arguments most often fail is a component that inexperienced writers do not know is missing.

Adapting Stephen Toulmin's analysis of argument to historical use, a fully made claim has three parts. There is the **claim** itself: the proposition you are asserting. There is the **evidence** (Toulmin's "grounds"): the criticised source material that bears on it. And there is the **warrant**: the statement of *why* this evidence supports this claim — the connective reasoning that licenses the move from grounds to conclusion. The warrant is the part that goes missing. The characteristic undergraduate paragraph juxtaposes a claim and a piece of evidence — a statement and a quotation, say — and assumes that the connection between them is self-evident, that the reader will see why the quotation supports the statement. Usually they will not, because the connection runs through reasoning the writer has performed silently and failed to put on the page. The evidence does not speak for itself; it never does; the warrant is you speaking for it.

In history the warrant is very often an act of source criticism — which is why Chapters 1 and 2 are the foundation of this one. To warrant the move from a source to a claim, you typically have to say what kind of source it is and what it can bear: *this* document supports *this* conclusion *because* of what it is (a contemporaneous record innocent of hindsight, an unwitting disclosure rather than a witting performance, a genuinely independent corroboration rather than a shared-origin echo). The warrant is where the apparatus of the first three chapters is cashed out in argument. When you write "this minute shows that the official privately doubted the policy," the warrant — usually needing to be stated — is that an internal minute is the candid, low-performance register of the diplomatic genre (2.2), so that what it discloses about private doubt is more reliable than a formal dispatch would be. Leave the warrant implicit and you have asserted; supply it and you have argued.

Two further components mark the mature claim, both inherited directly from 1.5. The **qualifier** states the strength of the claim — "almost certainly," "the balance of the evidence suggests," "tentatively" — and matches it to the evidence, neither overclaiming nor underclaiming; the calibrated "almost certainly a forgery" of 1.6 was a qualifier doing its job. And the **rebuttal** acknowledges the conditions under which the claim would fail — what evidence would defeat it, what the strongest objection is — which is not a weakness but the very thing that, under inference to the best explanation, makes the claim strong: an explanation earns its standing by surviving the strongest objection, and you cannot show it has survived an objection you have not stated.

## 4.4 The paragraph as the unit of argument

The paragraph is where the argument is actually built, and the governing principle is that **each paragraph should make one move in the argument — advance one claim — not contain one topic or describe one source.** The unit of composition is the unit of reasoning. A paragraph is not a place to put everything you know about a sub-topic; it is a single step in the inference, and it should do one identifiable thing to the argument.

The diagnostic is simple and worth applying ruthlessly to your own drafts: for each paragraph, state in one sentence what it is *doing* to the argument — not what it is *about*, but what work it performs. "This paragraph establishes that the liberal and radical wings had divergent aims even before the fracture" is a move; "this paragraph is about the working classes in 1848" is a topic, and a paragraph that can only be described by its topic is a survey paragraph that has wandered in from a container essay. If you cannot state the move, the paragraph is not pulling its weight, and the fix is either to find the move it should be making or to cut it.

Structurally, the argument paragraph has a recognisable shape that follows from 4.3: it opens with its claim (the topic sentence, which should state the paragraph's move, not its subject); it supplies evidence and — crucially — the warrant connecting evidence to claim; and it closes by linking forward, so that the move just made sets up the next. The linking is what produces advance rather than accumulation: each paragraph should leave the argument somewhere it can build from, so that the paragraphs form a chain of reasoning rather than a list of observations. When you revise, read only the first sentence of each paragraph in sequence; if those sentences alone trace the spine of an argument — claim leading to claim leading to conclusion — the essay advances. If they read as a list of topics, it accumulates, and no amount of good material within the paragraphs will rescue the structure.

## 4.5 Handling counterevidence

Every thesis worth defending faces counterevidence — sources that point the other way, facts the rival explanation accommodates more easily, the strongest version of the case against you. How you handle it is the clearest single marker dividing mature argument from amateur, and the amateur move is to hide it: to omit the inconvenient source, to argue only against the weakest form of the opposition, to write as though the evidence ran all one way. This is not only dishonest; it is self-defeating, because under inference to the best explanation an explanation earns its standing precisely by surviving the strongest objection, so an argument that never confronts the strongest objection has not earned anything. The counterevidence you suppress is the counterevidence your examiner supplies, with the added observation that you appear not to have noticed it.

The mature practice is the reverse: seek out the strongest version of the case against your thesis — steelman it, do not strawman it — and confront it directly. There are three honest moves available, and part of the skill is knowing which the evidence permits. You may **concede and limit**: grant that the counterevidence is real but show that it bounds your claim rather than defeating it, qualifying the thesis without surrendering it (the loyal armies of 1849 mattered, but they were the instrument of restoration, not its cause, and they could be deployed only because the revolutionary coalition had already fractured). You may **reinterpret**: show that the counterevidence, read with proper source criticism, does not establish what it appears to — that the witting testimony has been taken at face value, or a shared-origin echo mistaken for independent confirmation, so that the apparent counter-fact dissolves under the apparatus of Chapters 1 and 2. Or you may **absorb**: show that your thesis actually explains the counterevidence better than the rival does, turning the apparent objection into further support. What you may not do is ignore it, and what you should never do is defeat a version of the opposition weaker than the one a competent opponent would mount.

There is a discipline here that pulls against the writer's strongest instinct: be at least as sceptical of the evidence that supports you as of the evidence that does not. The natural tendency is to scrutinise hostile evidence and wave through congenial evidence, and that asymmetry is exactly the mechanism by which congeniality, rather than evidential force, comes to drive a conclusion. The corrective is to subject your own best evidence to the source criticism you would apply if it cut the other way — to ask of the source that supports you the same questions about witting performance, independence, and survival you would ask of the source that embarrasses you. An argument that has done this reads as trustworthy; one that has not reads as advocacy, and an experienced examiner can tell the difference within a page.

## 4.6 Quotation: uses and misuses

Quotation is the point at which evidence enters the prose directly, and it is badly misused more often than any other technique, because students are taught that quotation demonstrates engagement with sources and conclude, wrongly, that more is better.

Quote when, and only when, the exact wording is itself the evidence. There are a few cases where it is. When the precise formulation carries the meaning — where the source's own phrasing is the thing under analysis, so that paraphrase would destroy what you are examining — the words must be reproduced, because they are the evidence, not merely a report of it; this is the witting testimony of 1.2 at the points where its exact construction matters. When a formulation is contested, and the dispute turns on what was actually said, the wording is the fact in question. And occasionally, when a source's phrasing is so vivid that paraphrase would flatten evidence of tone, register, or attitude that bears on your argument, the quotation preserves evidence the paraphrase would lose. Outside these cases — which is to say, in the great majority of cases — paraphrase. Paraphrase is not a lesser substitute for quotation; it is the normal mode of using a source, because it forces you to state, in your own analytical voice, what the source establishes, which is the work of argument. A paragraph thick with quotation is usually a paragraph in which the writer has let the sources do the arguing instead of arguing themselves.

The misuses are worth naming so you can catch them in your own drafts. The **decorative quotation** ornaments rather than works — the famous line dropped in for colour, doing no evidential job; the worked example below opens with one. The **quotation that argues for you** lets a source assert your claim instead of your defending it, as though a historian's having said something made it so — which forgets that the secondary source is itself to be criticised, not deferred to (the business of Chapter 6). The **block quotation dropped and abandoned** — quote-and-run — sets down a long extract and moves on without analysis, leaving the reader to do the work of connecting it to the argument; every quotation must be introduced, deployed, and then *worked*, which means you must do something to it — analyse it, read it against the grain, situate it — so that the quotation is the occasion for your reasoning, not a substitute for it. And the most insidious: the **paraphrase so close it smuggles in the source's framing**, reproducing the source's witting structure under the appearance of your own analysis, so that you have unknowingly adopted the source's argument as your finding. True paraphrase is a full restatement in your own analytical terms, organised by your argument, not a light reshuffling of the source's words organised by the source's. The test is whether your paraphrase reflects your claim or the source's framing; if it follows the source's order and emphasis, you have not paraphrased, you have ventriloquised.

## 4.7 Common failure modes, with diagnosis

It is useful to have a catalogue of the characteristic ways essays fail, because most failures are recurring types, and naming the type tells you the fix. Each entry below is a symptom, its diagnosis in the terms of this handbook, and the remedy.

The **survey** has no thesis: it is organised by topic or chronology and never proposes a contestable claim. Diagnosed by the deniability test of 4.2 — nothing in it could be denied — its only remedy is to find a thesis and rebuild around it, which usually means rewriting rather than patching. The **chronicle** narrates where it should analyse: a sequence of "and then… and then…" that recounts what happened without explaining or arguing. Narration is not analysis, and the fix is to convert each "what happened" into a "why it matters to the claim." The **assertion without warrant** juxtaposes claim and evidence and leaves the connection to the reader — the missing-warrant failure of 4.3 — and is fixed by supplying the connective reasoning, usually an act of source criticism. The **kitchen sink** includes everything the writer knows, on the theory that more demonstrates more, when in fact **selection is itself argument**: choosing what to include and what to leave out is one of the primary ways an argument is made, and an essay that selects nothing has argued nothing. The remedy is ruthless: cut everything that does not advance the thesis, however hard-won.

The **buried thesis** is the subtlest and the most common in able writers: the real argument exists, but it appears only in the conclusion, discovered by the writer in the act of writing and never moved to the front. The diagnosis is that the essay was a process of figuring out what was thought, which is exactly what drafting is for — but the finished essay must present the conclusion as a thesis from the start, not re-enact the writer's journey to it. The fix is mechanical and reliable: write the essay, find your real thesis in the last paragraph, move it to the first, and rebuild the argument as a defence of it. The **hedge that abandons the claim** is the opposite of overreach: a thesis so qualified, so surrounded by "perhaps" and "to some extent" and "it could be argued," that it finally asserts nothing and cannot be wrong because it has not said anything. This is the failure that the qualifier of 4.3 must avoid — a qualifier matches the claim's strength to the evidence; a hedge dissolves the claim entirely — and the remedy is to decide what you actually think and claim it at the strength the evidence supports. Finally, the **strawman** defeats a version of the opposition weaker than any competent opponent would hold, and is diagnosed and cured by 4.5: steelman the opposition, confront the strongest case, or the apparent victory is worthless.

## 4.8 Worked example: revising a weak paragraph

The principles of this chapter are best seen operating on a single paragraph. Here is a weak one, on a topic chosen to sit away from any specialist field — the failure of the 1848 revolutions:

> There were many reasons why the revolutions of 1848 failed. The revolutions broke out in France in February 1848 and spread across Europe to the German and Italian states and the Habsburg Empire. The revolutionaries wanted liberal constitutions and, in some places, national unification. However, by 1849 they had failed almost everywhere. As one historian put it, 1848 was "the turning-point at which modern history failed to turn." The middle classes and the workers fell out, especially in France during the June Days. The conservative forces, particularly the armies, stayed loyal to the monarchs. The national movements also did not cooperate with one another. By 1849 the old order had been restored almost everywhere, though some reforms survived.

Diagnose it against the chapter. It has no thesis: "there were many reasons" is the undeniable non-claim of 4.2, and the kitchen-sink list that follows (the bourgeois–worker split, the loyal armies, the failure of national cooperation) names four or five causes and argues for none, because selection has not been used to make a claim (4.7). It chronicles rather than analyses — the "broke out… spread… failed" sequence is narration where the question demands explanation. It contains a decorative quotation (a much-quoted line usually attributed to G.M. Trevelyan) dropped in for colour and abandoned without being worked, the quote-and-run of 4.6. And every causal claim is an assertion without warrant (4.3): we are told the classes "fell out" and the armies "stayed loyal," but never why these facts support any particular account of the failure, nor how they relate to one another. The paragraph is a container in miniature.

Now a revision that makes a single argumentative move, on the thesis proposed in 4.2 — that the internal fracture of the revolutionary coalition mattered more than the loyalty of the conservative armies:

> The decisive solvent of the 1848 revolutions was internal, not external: the revolutionary coalitions dissolved from within once their common monarchical enemy was checked, and this fracture, rather than the much-emphasised loyalty of the conservative armies, is what made restoration possible. The French case is the clearest demonstration, because there the split can be dated. The liberal-bourgeois and radical-popular wings had cooperated only so long as the July Monarchy stood; with it gone, their aims diverged — constitutional order and the protection of property on one side, the social republic and the right to work on the other — and in the June Days of 1848 the bourgeois republic turned its own forces on the Parisian workers it had briefly allied with. The significance of this is easy to mistake if the armies' later loyalty is read as the cause of the revolutions' defeat. The armies mattered, and the qualification must be granted: it was soldiers who restored the monarchical order across the German and Italian states in 1849. But the armies were the instrument of restoration, not its precondition. They could be turned on the revolutions only because the revolutionary side had already ceased to be a coalition and become competing factions, each of which would rather see order restored than see its rivals prevail — a point the June Days establish before any army had marched against a German or Italian revolution at all. The chronology is the warrant: the internal fracture precedes, and makes possible, the external suppression, which is why it is the deeper cause.

The revision makes one move — it establishes a single claim and defends it — rather than listing causes. It opens with its claim as the topic sentence (4.4). It selects: the German and Italian cases, the failure of national cooperation, and the rest of the kitchen-sink list are dropped, because the argument does not need them, and the dropping is itself the argument's work (4.7). It supplies warrants: the chronology connects evidence to claim, stated explicitly at the close ("the chronology is the warrant"). It handles the strongest counter-case — the loyal armies — by concede-and-limit (4.5), granting their reality and bounding their significance rather than ignoring them. And it has dispensed with the decorative quotation entirely, because the wording of that much-quoted line was never the evidence; the events were. The paragraph is shorter on coverage and longer on argument, which is the trade every revision toward argument makes.

## 4.9 Main conclusions

-   **An essay is an argument, and an argument is inference to the best explanation made explicit.** Its job is to propose an explanation and show it better than the rivals; the container that surveys a topic without proposing one has not done history, however well-informed. The test: an argument advances, a survey accumulates.

-   **Distinguish topic from thesis by the deniability test.** A thesis is a contestable, specific, evidence-answerable, proportionate claim; convert your topic into a sharp question, and let its answer be your thesis.

-   **A fully made claim has a warrant, and the warrant is what goes missing.** Juxtaposing claim and evidence is not arguing; the connective reasoning — in history, usually an act of source criticism — must be on the page. Add the qualifier and the rebuttal that a mature claim requires.

-   **The paragraph is the unit of argument: one move, not one topic.** State what each paragraph *does* to the argument in a sentence; if you can only state what it is *about*, it is a survey paragraph. Read your topic sentences in sequence to check the spine advances.

-   **Confront the strongest counterevidence, never the weakest.** Steelman the opposition and concede-and-limit, reinterpret, or absorb it; suppressing it is both dishonest and self-defeating, since an explanation earns its standing only by surviving the strongest objection. Be as sceptical of your congenial evidence as of the hostile.

-   **Quote only when the wording is the evidence; otherwise paraphrase.** Work every quotation rather than dropping and abandoning it, and beware the close paraphrase that smuggles in the source's framing as your finding.

-   **Most failures are recognisable types** — the survey, the chronicle, the unwarranted assertion, the kitchen sink, the buried thesis, the dissolving hedge, the strawman — and naming the type gives the fix. Selection is argument; cut what does not advance the thesis.

## 4.10 Further reading

*Toulmin, Stephen (1958) —* The Uses of Argument*.* The origin of the claim–grounds–warrant analysis adapted in 4.3, and still the clearest account of why the warrant is the part of an argument most likely to be missing and most important to supply. Not about history, but the structure it describes is the one historical argument runs on.

*Booth, Wayne C., Gregory G. Colomb, and Joseph M. Williams —* The Craft of Research*.* The best book on the move from a topic to a question to an arguable problem — the work of 4.2 — and on the discipline of asking "so what?" of every claim. Cross-disciplinary, but its account of how a research argument is actually built is the most useful in print for a writer at your stage.

*Rampolla, Mary Lynn —* A Pocket Guide to Writing in History*.* The standard student-facing manual: practical, brief, and sound on the mechanics this chapter treats at the level of principle — thesis statements, the use and citation of evidence, the common errors. Modest in ambition and American in convention, but genuinely useful as a desk reference for the operations rather than the theory.

*Hexter, J.H. —* Doing History *(and the essay "The Rhetoric of History").* The high-register reflection on how historical argument is actually constructed and conveyed — the role of the example, the work that selection and arrangement do, the difference between the storage and the communication of historical knowledge. A bridge to Chapter 5, where the prose itself, rather than the argument's skeleton, becomes the subject.
