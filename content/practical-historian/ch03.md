# Chapter 3 — Quantitative Methods and Their Limits

## 3.1 The data is a source, not a measurement

This chapter assumes you can already handle a regression, read a distribution, and distinguish a Laspeyres index from a Paasche one. It is not an introduction to statistics; it is an account of what happens when statistical competence meets historical data, and why the competence, applied without adjustment, will mislead you. The single idea that organises everything below is this: **a historical dataset is a source, and must be subjected to exactly the source criticism of Chapter 2 before it is subjected to any statistical procedure whatever.** A census table, a price series, an electoral return, a set of trade figures — each is a document before it is a dataset, produced by an apparatus with its own purposes, categories, and incentives, and the numbers measure that apparatus at least as much as they measure the world. The quantitative historian's first task is not estimation but interrogation of the data-generating process: who counted, under what categories, for what purpose, with what incentive to misreport, and with what survival history since. Skip that step and you will compute, with great precision, the properties of an artefact you have mistaken for a measurement.

The reason this needs saying to a quantitatively trained reader specifically is that the instincts formed on modern data — financial data above all — are precisely the wrong ones, and they are wrong in ways that feel like rigour. The discipline of this chapter is therefore largely a discipline of *unlearning*, and 3.2 sets out the four assumptions that have to go.

## 3.2 What the quantitative reader must unlearn

Four assumptions are so deeply built into the handling of modern data that they operate below the level of conscious method. Each is approximately true of the data a financial analyst works with, and each is false of historical data in ways that matter.

**The first assumption is that the data was generated by a process aimed at accuracy.** A market price, a reported return, an audited balance — these are produced by processes that, whatever their imperfections, are oriented toward recording a real magnitude, and are policed by incentives (arbitrage, audit, regulation, reputation) that punish gross misreporting. Almost no historical data was generated this way. Censuses, tax rolls, trade returns, conscription registers, parish records — these were produced to administer, to tax, to conscript, to discipline, or to display, and the people supplying the underlying information frequently had strong incentives to misreport. A population assessed for taxation has an interest in being undercounted; a state advertising its power has an interest in overcounting its army or its output; a parish recording communicants is recording religious conformity, not belief. The number is the output of an incentive structure, and the first analytical question is always which way the incentives pushed. This is not a marginal correction to be bolted on after estimation; it determines the sign and often the order of magnitude of the bias, and it cannot be recovered from the data alone — it requires knowledge of the institution that produced the data, which is to say, ordinary historical research.

**The second assumption is that the data is a sample from a stable population, drawn by a process you can model.** This is the assumption that underwrites the entire apparatus of statistical inference — standard errors, confidence intervals, significance tests — because that apparatus is, at bottom, a theory of how a sample relates to the population it was drawn from and how it would vary across repeated draws. Historical data almost never has this structure. It is not a random sample from a defined population by a known mechanism; it is *what survived*, constructed under categories that shift, with no sampling frame and no repeatable data-generating process behind it. Computing a standard error on a historical aggregate and reporting a confidence interval is, in most cases, a category error dressed as rigour: the interval describes sampling variability that the data does not possess, while saying nothing about the far larger uncertainties — selection, misreporting, category drift — that actually dominate. The apparatus smuggles in, besides, a set of distributional assumptions — independence and identical distribution of the observations, a particular functional form, stationarity of the underlying process — that are rarely examined and still more rarely defensible for data thrown up by a non-repeating historical process; the confident interval is produced by assuming precisely the regularities the historical setting denies, and it reads as rigour exactly because the assumptions that purchased it are invisible in the result. The honest treatment of uncertainty in historical data is usually a stated range built from explicit assumptions about those dominating errors, not a confidence interval computed from a residual. The machinery you are most fluent in is the machinery least applicable, and the temptation to deploy it anyway — because it produces a clean number — is exactly the false precision 1.5 warned against, now in its most seductive form.

**The third assumption is that the categories are stable across the series.** A modern financial time series has continuity problems, but nothing like the discontinuities of historical categories. "Occupation," "unemployment," "the nation," "literate," "white-collar," "urban," "criminal" — these are not natural kinds with fixed extensions; they are constructions whose definitions changed, sometimes radically, across the very periods over which you want to compare. A series of "the unemployed" that spans a redefinition of who counts as unemployed is two different series spliced at the join and relabelled as one, and the break in definition will masquerade as a change in the world. Splicing across a category change is the historical equivalent of chaining two differently constructed indices and reporting the result as a single continuous measure — except that in history the categories often change without announcement, so that the join is invisible unless you have read the administrative history that produced the figures. 2.3 made this point about the census as a document; here it returns as the central hazard of any historical time series. Before you compare two numbers across time, you must establish that they are counting the same thing under the same definition, and the default assumption should be that they are not.

**The fourth assumption is that more precision in the figure means more information.** A figure carried to four significant figures in your spreadsheet implies a measurement accurate to four significant figures. A great deal of historical data is accurate, at best, to its leading digit — a nineteenth-century return "to the nearest thousand" may carry a real error of twenty per cent or more, and an eighteenth-century population estimate may be a contemporary's educated guess. Carrying such figures through a calculation at full machine precision and reporting the result to several decimals manufactures information that was never in the source. The discipline is to track the precision of the input and refuse to report output beyond it: if the data supports an order of magnitude, report an order of magnitude; if it supports two significant figures, stop at two. This is not pedantry. The reader who sees "37.2 per cent" assumes the third figure means something, and if it does not, you have lied to them with a decimal point.

These four together amount to a single instruction: do the source criticism of the data-generating process *first*, treat the statistical apparatus as applicable only where its assumptions actually hold, and let the precision of your conclusions be governed by the quality of the data rather than the resolution of your software.

## 3.3 Selection and survivorship

1.4 distinguished two registers of absence and promised that the second — the surviving record as a non-random sample — would return here as survivorship bias in data. It does, and it is the most pervasive single threat to quantitative history, because it operates silently: the data you have looks complete, and nothing in it announces what is missing.

The general form is that the units which generated the records you possess are systematically unlike the units which did not. Estate and probate records describe the propertied, not the population; business archives survive disproportionately for firms that succeeded and endured, so that a quantitative study of "firms" built from surviving archives is silently a study of survivors, with the failures — the very cases a study of, say, business risk most needs — absent by construction; criminal records describe those the apparatus caught and processed, not those who offended. In each case the sample is filtered by exactly the variable under study, which is the worst case: the selection is not random noise to be averaged away but a systematic distortion correlated with the outcome.

A single compact example shows several of these problems interacting. The standard historical measure of literacy for much of the modern period is the ability to sign one's name, typically in a marriage register. As a dataset it is attractive: near-universal, serial, comparable across regions. As a measure it is treacherous on at least three counts at once. It is a *proxy of contested validity*: signing one's name is neither necessary nor sufficient for functional literacy, and the relationship between the proxy and the thing of interest (the ability to read, the ability to read and write) varied by place, period, and the way reading and writing were taught — in some contexts reading was taught before, and to more people than, writing, so that signature literacy systematically understates reading ability. It is *selected*: the marriage register captures only those who married, and at the age they married, excluding the never-married and shifting with the marriage rate. And it is *category-unstable*: what counted as a valid signature, and the registration practice itself, varied across jurisdictions and reforms. None of these problems is visible in the numbers; all of them require knowing how the data was made. A literacy series can be a perfectly good piece of evidence — but only once you have established what its selection and its proxy actually capture, and that establishing is historical work, not statistical work.

The practical reflex: for any historical dataset, ask what would have had to happen for a unit to appear in it, and whether that condition is correlated with the variable you are studying. If it is — and it usually is — the data is selected on the outcome, and the analysis must address the selection explicitly rather than proceeding as though the surviving units were representative.

## 3.4 Classification instability in practice

The category problem of 3.2 is worth a concrete pass, because its consequences are so easy to miss and so damaging when missed.

Occupational data is the classic case. The occupational categories of successive censuses were repeatedly reorganised; the treatment of women's work, in particular, shifted between censuses in ways that make a "decline" or "rise" in female employment across a category change largely an artefact of recording rather than a change in what women did. A series of "the agricultural labour force" or "domestic servants" that crosses one of these reorganisations is measuring, in part, the statisticians' changing taxonomy. Electoral data carries the same trap in a different form: the British electorate before and after each franchise reform is not the same population sampled twice but a differently constituted body, so that a "swing" measured across a reform conflates a change in who voted with a change in how the constant voters behaved — and the two are not separable without external information. Recorded crime is perhaps the most instructive case of all, because here the data-generating apparatus and the phenomenon are especially easy to confuse. Recorded crime measures the activity of the recording system — the reach of policing, the propensity to report, the definitions of offences, the administrative thoroughness of the courts — at least as much as it measures criminal behaviour. An increase in recorded crime that coincides with the creation of a professional police force is, to an unknown but possibly large degree, an increase in *recording*, and the "dark figure" of unrecorded offences stands between the series and the behaviour it is taken to measure. A historian who reads rising recorded crime as rising criminality has confused the apparatus with the world, which is the characteristic error this whole chapter exists to prevent.

The operative discipline is the same throughout: a time series is only as continuous as its definitions, and the burden is on you to establish definitional continuity before you interpret a trend. Where you cannot establish it, the honest move is to break the series at the join and treat the segments separately, rather than to draw a single line across a discontinuity and narrate it as change.

## 3.5 Money and magnitude across time: the worked spine

The debate over the standard of living during the British Industrial Revolution, roughly the 1790s to the 1850s, is the best single training ground in the discipline, because it forces every problem in this chapter into the open at once and because — crucially — it has never been definitively settled, which is itself the lesson.

The core question seems quantifiable: did the material condition of the working population rise or fall during early industrialisation? The "optimist" case rested substantially on real-wage series, and the construction of those series is a seminar in the index-number problem. A money-wage series must be deflated by a cost-of-living index to yield real wages, and there is no single correct deflator. The basket of goods that defines the index changed over the period — as consumption patterns, available goods, and relative prices all shifted — so that a fixed-basket index becomes progressively less representative the further it is carried from its base, while a changing-basket index loses comparability across time. (This is the index-number problem you already know from modern price indices, but it bites far harder over sixty years of structural economic change than over a few years of modern data, because the basket and the relative prices move so much more.) Different defensible choices of deflator, base year, and basket yield real-wage series that diverge enough to support different answers to the original question. The optimist real-wage revision associated with Lindert and Williamson moved the consensus toward rising real wages across the period; but the magnitude of the rise, and even its existence in particular decades, remained sensitive to exactly these index choices, and the pessimist response contested them on their own ground. A reader trained to think a real-wage series is a clean measurement should sit with the fact that the series is the *output* of contestable construction choices, and that reasonable historians, making different reasonable choices, get different answers from "the same" data.

The most instructive turn in the debate, methodologically, is what happened when historians recognised the limits of the wage data and went looking for a measure less dependent on those construction choices. The anthropometric move — using human stature, recorded in military, prison, and other records, as an index of the "biological standard of living" — was a response to exactly the problem this chapter describes: heights reflect net nutritional status (food intake minus the demands of work and disease) and so capture something real-wage series miss, and they do not depend on choosing a price deflator. The anthropometric evidence complicated the optimist picture, suggesting that the stature of cohorts born across the central decades of industrialisation stagnated or even declined even as real wages were said to be rising — a divergence that, if real, points to deteriorating conditions (urban disease environments, the intensity and disamenity of industrial work, inequality in the distribution of gains) that money wages alone do not register. But the height data brings its own full set of the problems of 3.3 and 3.4: military and prison records are *selected* populations (recruits and convicts are not the general population, and the selection into them shifts with the economy), the records' survival is partial, and the measurement and recording standards varied. The anthropometric series is not a clean escape from the construction problems of the wage series; it is a *different* set of construction problems, partly independent of the first — which is exactly why it is valuable. The convergence or divergence of two series built on partly independent selection and construction assumptions is more informative than either alone, for precisely the reason 1.3 gave: independence is what makes corroboration probative. Where the wage and height series agree, the agreement is robust to the construction choices of each; where they diverge, the divergence localises the disagreement to the assumptions that differ between them, and tells you where to look.

The standard-of-living debate, then, is not a failure of quantitative history but a model of it done honestly: numbers deployed seriously, their construction exposed rather than hidden, rival series built on independent assumptions and compared, and a conclusion held as the best current reading rather than announced as a measurement. The thing to carry from it is that the debate's persistence is not a scandal to be resolved by better arithmetic. It reflects genuine, irreducible uncertainty about how to construct the measures, and the maturity of the field lies in living with that uncertainty rather than legislating it away with a confident decimal.

## 3.6 Correlation, causation, and aggregation

You do not need to be told that correlation is not causation. But the historical setting has three specific features that sharpen the familiar warnings into something worth restating.

The first is small N. The quantitative historian frequently has not a large sample but a handful of cases, or one — one country's trajectory, one episode, one crisis. With N very small, the leverage that large-sample statistics provides simply is not available, and a regression run on a dozen country-years is not doing what a regression on a large modern panel does, however similar the output looks. Much apparent quantitative rigour in historical argument is a small-N qualitative comparison wearing the costume of statistics, and the costume should be removed so the actual inferential basis — comparison of a few cases — can be judged for what it is.

The second is unobservable confounding that cannot be remedied. In a modern study you can often measure and control a confounder; in history the confounder frequently left no usable record, so the control is impossible in principle, not merely in practice. An estimated association between two historical variables is therefore exposed to confounding by factors you cannot observe and cannot rule out, and the appropriate confidence is correspondingly lower than the same association would warrant in a setting where the confounders were measurable.

The third is the **ecological fallacy**, which is endemic in historical work because historical data so often exists only in aggregate. The fallacy is the inference from an aggregate-level correlation to an individual-level relationship — and the classic demonstration, Robinson's, showed that aggregate and individual correlations can differ not only in magnitude but in sign. The historical illustration most worth holding in mind is the analysis of the Nazi vote. It is well established at the aggregate level that, in late-Weimar elections, more heavily Protestant and more rural areas tended to return higher shares for the NSDAP. The tempting inference — that Protestants, or rural voters, were correspondingly the Nazi voters as individuals — does not follow from the aggregate correlation, and the careful individual-level reconstruction of the Nazi electorate (which required methods designed precisely to recover individual behaviour from aggregate data) produced a more complicated and in places quite different picture than the simple ecological reading suggested. The aggregate correlation is real; the individual inference is unlicensed; and the gap between them is exactly the ecological fallacy. Whenever your data is aggregate and your claim is about individuals — about how *people* of some kind behaved — you are at risk of it, and the claim must either be restricted to the aggregate level the data actually supports or defended with the specific apparatus that ecological inference requires.

There is a connection here to a discipline that recurs throughout serious historical work: the injunction to stop at the level your evidence actually reaches. Aggregate data licenses aggregate claims; a correlation licenses a statement about association; a small-N comparison licenses a cautious, mechanism-focused argument. Pushing any of these to a stronger claim — individual behaviour, causation, a precise effect size — is the numerical form of the overreach that 1.1 called leaching and 1.5 called false precision. The number does not exempt you from the discipline of stopping where the evidence stops; if anything, because a number looks authoritative, it makes the discipline harder and more necessary.

## 3.7 What quantitative method does well

This chapter has been mostly cautionary, and it would leave a false impression if it ended there, because the cautions are the price of a genuine and considerable power. Used with the source criticism this chapter demands, quantification does things that qualitative method cannot.

It establishes magnitude. Qualitative sources are full of claims of scale — contemporaries complained of "soaring" prices, "vast" migrations, "rising" crime — and these claims are themselves evidence (of perception), but they are notoriously unreliable as evidence of actual magnitude, because perception of scale is shaped by attention, anxiety, and rhetorical convenience. Numbers, even imperfect ones, discipline the impressionistic claim: they can establish whether the "soaring" was a doubling or a few per cent, whether the "vast" migration moved thousands or millions, and that disciplining is a real contribution to knowledge. Relatedly, quantification can *falsify* a qualitative claim that would otherwise float free. The assertion that some phenomenon was widespread, or growing, or characteristic, is often testable against aggregate data, and a qualitative narrative that the numbers contradict has a case to answer. This is quantification's most valuable role in a largely qualitative field: not to replace the narrative but to constrain it, to rule out the impressionistic claims the data cannot bear, and to force the surviving claims to be consistent with the measurable record. And it enables genuine comparison across cases and over time, where definitions can be held stable — putting magnitudes side by side in a way that prose comparison cannot achieve. The point is not that numbers are more objective than words; this chapter has argued at length that they are not, that they are sources with all the partiality of sources. The point is that they are sources of a particular kind, capable of establishing and disciplining magnitude in a way no other source can, and a history that refuses them on the grounds of their imperfection forfeits that capability while gaining no immunity, since the qualitative sources are no less partial. The mature position is neither the cliometric faith that proper measurement settles historical questions nor the humanistic suspicion that numbers falsify, but the working recognition that quantitative evidence is evidence — to be criticised at its source, deployed within its limits, and held to conclusions its construction can support.

## 3.8 Main conclusions

-   **A historical dataset is a source before it is a dataset.** Subject the data-generating process to the source criticism of Chapter 2 — who counted, under what categories, for what purpose, with what incentive to misreport — before any statistical procedure. The numbers measure the apparatus as much as the world.

-   **Unlearn the four modern-data assumptions.** Historical data was not generated by a process aimed at accuracy; it is not a sample from a stable population by a modellable mechanism (so the inferential apparatus of standard errors and confidence intervals mostly does not apply); its categories are not stable across the series; and reported precision routinely exceeds real accuracy.

-   **Selection and survivorship are the pervasive, silent threat.** The units that left records are systematically unlike those that did not, and the selection is usually correlated with the very variable under study. Ask what a unit had to do to appear in the data, and whether that condition is tied to the outcome.

-   **A time series is only as continuous as its definitions.** Splicing across an unannounced category change makes a change in taxonomy masquerade as a change in the world; break the series at the join rather than drawing a line across it.

-   **Money and magnitude across long spans depend on contestable construction.** The index-number problem bites hard over decades of structural change; expose the deflator, base, and basket choices rather than hiding them, and report no more precision than the data supports.

-   **Aggregate data licenses only aggregate claims.** The ecological fallacy — inferring individual behaviour from aggregate correlation — is endemic where historical data exists only in aggregate, and small N plus unobservable confounding further limit causal inference. Stop at the level the evidence reaches; a number does not lift the discipline of stopping, it makes it harder.

-   **Within its limits, quantification has real and distinctive power:** to establish magnitude, to falsify impressionistic qualitative claims, and to enable genuine comparison. It is evidence — partial like all evidence, but uniquely able to discipline scale — and a serious history neither worships nor refuses it.

## 3.9 Further reading

*Feinstein, Charles H., and Mark Thomas (2002) —* Making History Count: A Primer in Quantitative Methods for Historians*.* The book to read if you want the statistics done properly for historical data, pitched at a reader who can handle regression and wants it applied with attention to the historical pitfalls. The closest match on this list to your own quantitative starting point, and the one least likely to bore you with basics.

*Hudson, Pat, and Mina Ishizu (2016) —* History by Numbers: An Introduction to Quantitative Approaches*.* The standard modern introduction, broader and gentler than Feinstein and Thomas, and especially good on the source-critical questions — where the data comes from and what it can bear — that this chapter has treated as primary. Read it for the judgement rather than the technique.

*King, Gary (1997) —* A Solution to the Ecological Inference Problem*.* The technical treatment of the 3.6 problem, and the apparatus behind the careful reconstruction of aggregate-only electorates such as the Nazi vote. Demanding and specialised; for the reader who wants to know what it actually takes to license an individual claim from aggregate data rather than merely to be warned against it.

*Floud, Roderick, Robert W. Fogel, Bernard Harris, and Sok Chul Hong (2011) —* The Changing Body: Health, Nutrition, and Human Development in the Western World since 1700*.* The mature statement of the anthropometric programme that gave 3.5 its sharpest turn — the biological standard of living, its sources, and their selection problems. A demonstration of quantitative history at its most ambitious and most candid about the limits of its own data.
