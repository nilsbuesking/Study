# Chapter 8 — Research Workflow

## 8.1 The workflow is the method, made physical

The preceding chapters described the operations of the craft — criticising a source, reading a genre, handling data, building an argument, writing it, situating it in the literature — and the forms the resulting work takes. This chapter describes the system that lets you perform those operations at the scale of a real project — over months or years, across hundreds of sources, without losing the thread, the references, or the work itself. A workflow is not administrative overhead bolted onto the intellectual work; it is the intellectual work given a body, and a bad workflow degrades the thinking as surely as a good one supports it.

A workflow serves two masters, and most failures come from neglecting one of them. The first is *not losing things*: capturing sources and their locators as you encounter them, keeping every extract traceable to its origin, and protecting the accumulated work against loss. The second, less obvious and more important, is *developing understanding*: processing what you read into thought rather than merely storing it, so that the system grows your comprehension rather than your hoard. A workflow that captures everything and understands nothing is an expensive way to procrastinate; a workflow that generates insight but cannot trace a quotation to its page is a liability waiting to discredit you in a footnote. The chapter recommends concrete tools, because vague advice about "good organisation" helps no one, but the principles are what matter and they will outlast any particular piece of software. Where this chapter names a tool, read it as the current best instrument for a durable principle, not as the principle itself.

## 8.2 The shape of the research process

The first thing to get right is the shape of the process, because the most common structural error is to imagine research as a waterfall: read everything, then think, then write, in sequence. This is wrong, and believing it produces both paralysis and bad work. Research is iterative. You read toward a question (1.1, 4.2), which means you do not read everything — you triage, skimming to orient and reading deeply only where the question demands it, and the question itself sharpens as you read, sending you back to the sources with better-formed demands. And you write throughout, not at the end, because — as 5.1 argued — writing is not the transcription of finished thought but the process by which thought is formed and its gaps exposed. You do not know what you do not understand until you try to write it down; the blank you cannot fill is the research you have not yet done. The writing is therefore a diagnostic instrument as much as an output, and a researcher who defers all writing until the reading is "complete" defers the very activity that would reveal the reading was incomplete in a specific, fixable way.

The practical consequence is a cadence of alternation: read with a question, take notes that process rather than store, attempt to write the argument the notes are accumulating toward, discover from the attempt what is missing or unconvincing, and return to the sources with that specific deficiency in view. The cadence aligns with the section-by-section drafting-and-review discipline that serves analytical work well: write a section, see what it exposes, revise, proceed. The point throughout is that reading, note-taking, and writing are not three phases but three continuous activities that feed one another, and a workflow should be built to support the alternation rather than to enforce a false sequence.

## 8.3 The note system: capture, process, connect

Notes are where reading becomes understanding, and the design of the note system is therefore the centre of the workflow. The most useful framework distinguishes three kinds of note by function, and keeping them distinct prevents the characteristic confusions.

**Reference notes** hold the bibliographic facts and live in the reference manager (8.5): the citation, the locator, the source's identity. **Literature notes** record what a source says — its argument, its evidence, the passages that matter — and the single most important discipline here is to write them *in your own words*. Restating a source's argument in your own language is the act that converts reading into understanding, because you cannot restate what you have not grasped; a literature note that copies or lightly reshuffles the source's phrasing has tested nothing and is the 4.6 ventriloquism trap committed at the note-taking stage, where it will later contaminate your prose without your noticing. **Permanent notes** — your "building block" notes — record your own ideas: a single point, stated atomically, in your own words, and connected to the other notes it bears on. These are the units your eventual argument will be built from, and they are permanent in the sense that they are written to last and to be reused across projects rather than discarded after one essay.

Three best-practice disciplines sit above this structure and are worth installing deliberately, because their absence causes specific and serious failures.

The first is the **three-layer capture discipline**, which prevents the most dangerous note-taking error of all: the later conflation of a source's words, your paraphrase of them, and your own ideas. Every note that draws on a source should keep three things visually distinct — the source's *exact words*, in quotation marks, with the page locator; your *paraphrase* of the point, in your own words; and your own *commentary*, clearly marked as yours. Months later, when you draw on the note, this separation is what stands between you and two catastrophes: quoting as your paraphrase something that was actually the source's exact wording (inadvertent plagiarism), and attributing to a source an idea that was actually your own commentary, or the reverse. A note that does not preserve these boundaries is a time bomb, and the discipline of marking them at the moment of capture — when you still know which is which — is cheap insurance against an expensive failure. Establish a fixed convention (quotation marks and a page number for the source's words, a consistent prefix or formatting for your own commentary) and never deviate from it.

The second is the warning against the **collector's fallacy**: the mistaking of accumulation for progress. It is satisfying to gather sources, to highlight, to file PDFs, and none of it is thinking. A note you never process into your own words, never link to anything, and never use is not an asset but dead weight that disguises procrastination as diligence. The corrective is the 4.7 principle that selection is the work: a smaller body of notes that have been genuinely processed and connected is worth more than a vast unprocessed library, and the measure of a note's value is whether it has changed or connected to your understanding, not whether it exists. Be willing to read without noting where the reading only orients, and reserve the labour of permanent notes for what actually advances the thinking.

The third is the use of **Maps of Content as argument scaffolds**, which is the bridge from the note system to the writing. An index or map of content that gathers the permanent notes on a topic should be allowed to evolve from a navigational list into a structured outline — an ordering of your building-block notes that is, in effect, the skeleton of the eventual chapter or essay. When the map is arranged so that its sequence of notes traces a claim leading to a claim leading to a conclusion, it has become the topic-sentence spine of 4.4 in embryo, and the move from a well-developed map to a first draft is then a matter of writing the connective prose between points you have already established and ordered. The note system, used this way, does not merely store the materials of the argument; it incubates the argument's structure, so that drafting begins from a scaffold rather than a blank page.

## 8.4 The reading journal and its tags

A reading journal that logs what you have read and your evaluative response to it is a valuable instrument, and a categorical tagging scheme — marking each entry as, for instance, *supports the argument*, *complicates it*, *to verify*, *disagree*, or *inconsistent* — turns the journal from a record into a working tool, provided the tags are understood as more than filing labels. Each tag is an intellectual obligation, a debt the workflow is tracking on your behalf.

Read the tags through the handbook's concepts and the obligations become clear. A *to verify* tag is the traceability discipline (5.8) holding a claim in escrow: it marks something you intend to use but have not yet confirmed at its source, and it is a debt that must be paid before the claim may enter your argument as fact — an unpaid *to verify* is exactly the unverified assertion that 6.3 warned travels through literatures acquiring false authority. A *complicates* or *disagree* tag flags counterevidence, and 4.5 dictates what is owed: not suppression but confrontation, the steelmanning of the opposing position and the honest decision whether to concede-and-limit, reinterpret, or absorb it. The temptation to let the *disagree* entries quietly lapse is the temptation to bury counterevidence, and the journal exists partly to make that lapse visible to you. An *inconsistent* tag — a source at odds with itself or with others — is a flag for source criticism (Chapters 1 and 2): an inconsistency is rarely just noise, and is often the thread that, pulled, reveals something about a source's production or a received account's weakness. Used this way, the journal is a queue of obligations the workflow will not let you forget, and working through the queue — paying the verifications, confronting the disagreements, criticising the inconsistencies — is a large part of what turns a body of reading into a defensible argument. Keep it lean: a tag applied to everything tags nothing, and the scheme works only if the marked entries are genuinely the ones that carry an obligation.

## 8.5 The toolchain: a reference manager and a plain-text workshop

For the concrete tooling, the durable best practice is a division of labour between two systems: a reference manager that owns the bibliographic truth and the source documents, and a plain-text note-and-writing environment that owns the thinking. Zotero and Obsidian are the natural current choices for the two roles, and the architecture matters more than the brand names: the reference manager is the single authoritative source of citation data, and the notes environment links *to* it rather than duplicating it, so that bibliographic facts are entered once and never re-keyed by hand. Re-typing a citation into a note is not merely tedious; it creates a second copy that will drift out of agreement with the first, and the discipline of a single source of bibliographic truth eliminates a whole category of error.

The linchpin that makes the two systems cohere — and the piece most often missing from an otherwise sound setup — is a **stable citation key**. A reference manager extension that generates durable, human-readable citekeys (the Better BibTeX extension for Zotero is the standard) gives every source a fixed identifier that does not change when you reorganise your library, and that identifier becomes the permanent anchor by which your notes refer to their sources. An Obsidian note that cites a source by its citekey will still resolve correctly years later and across reorganisations, where a note that referred to a source by a copied-out title or an unstable internal link will rot. Install the citekey backbone early, before the library is large, because retrofitting stable keys onto a mature mess of notes is painful. With it in place, the Obsidian-side integration (the plugin that imports a source's metadata and your annotations into a note) can generate a literature-note stub for each source, pre-populated with the citekey and citation, into which your own-words processing then goes.

The reading-and-annotation layer fits naturally onto the hardware you have. Annotating PDFs on a tablet with a stylus, then having those annotations flow into the reference manager and from there into an Obsidian literature note, is an efficient pipeline — provided the annotations are not mistaken for the processing. Highlighting is capture, not understanding (8.3): the highlights and margin notes are raw material, and the literature note written in your own words from them is where the thinking happens. The pipeline should end in prose you wrote, not in a collection of coloured passages. For output, because the notes are plain-text markdown carrying citekeys, the eventual manuscript can be processed into a fully formatted document with citations and bibliography generated automatically from the reference manager — which means you write with lightweight citekeys and let the machinery produce the formal apparatus, never formatting a footnote by hand until the very end, if ever. The general principle behind every part of this is to automate the mechanical (citation formatting, metadata entry, bibliography generation) precisely so that no automation touches the judgement (what a source means, what to claim, how to argue), which is the same line 8.8 will draw for AI.

## 8.6 Backup, longevity, and the plain-text argument

A point that is dull until the day it is catastrophic: your accumulated notes and drafts are, after a year or two of work, irreplaceable, and they must be protected with a seriousness proportionate to that fact. The single most important thing to understand here is that **synchronisation is not backup**. A sync service that mirrors your notes across devices propagates every change instantly — including deletions and corruptions — so that a file destroyed or mangled on one device is destroyed or mangled everywhere within seconds, and the sync has faithfully ensured you cannot recover it. Sync solves availability across devices; it does nothing for recovery from error, and relying on it as your safeguard is a common and serious mistake.

The remedy is *versioned* backup, held separately from the sync: a backup system that keeps dated historical copies, so that you can recover the state of a file as it was last week, before the corruption or the regretted deletion. The standard discipline is the 3-2-1 rule — at least three copies of the work, on at least two different kinds of storage, with at least one copy held off-site — and for a body of work representing years of irrecoverable intellectual labour, that discipline is not paranoia but ordinary prudence. Set up automatic versioned backup, confirm periodically that it is actually running and that you can actually restore from it (an untested backup is a hope, not a safeguard), and treat the integrity of the archive as a standing responsibility rather than an afterthought.

The choice of plain-text markdown for the notes, beyond its convenience, is itself a longevity decision and the right one. A plain-text file is readable by any text editor, on any system, indefinitely, with no dependence on a particular program remaining available or a proprietary format remaining supported; markdown adds lightweight structure without sacrificing that independence. Work stored this way is insulated against the obsolescence that strands documents in defunct applications and unreadable formats, and against the risk of a notes application's vanishing or changing beyond use. You will change tools over a scholarly career; plain text is what survives the changes, and committing the durable record of your thinking to a format that outlives any single program is a quiet but real form of protecting the work.

## 8.7 Archival and manuscript sources: the digital pipeline

Work with manuscript and archival sources adds a stage to the pipeline, and the discipline at that stage is an extension of the source criticism of Chapters 2 and 3 turned upon your own materials. The pipeline runs from the source image through transcription to a verified transcript and thence into notes, and each arrow carries an obligation.

Handwritten-text recognition has transformed the transcription of difficult hands, including the German scripts whose reading is a specialised skill, and a recognition platform can train a model on a particular hand to produce a working transcript of material that would otherwise take far longer to read. But a recognition transcript is a *derived source with its own error layer*, and it must be treated as such: it is the output of an imperfect process, and to cite from an uncorrected machine transcript is to cite a source you have not actually verified, with all the 6.3 danger of an unverified claim plus a layer of mechanical error on top. The transcript is evidence-grade only after you have checked it against the source image, and the workflow must keep the image alongside the transcript precisely so that verification — and later re-verification, when a claim turns out to rest on a contested reading — is always possible. The recognition output is a powerful first draft of a transcription, never a finished source.

Two further disciplines belong here. The first is **traceability**: every extract must carry enough provenance — archive, collection or fonds, folder or volume, document, and where relevant the image identifier — that any claim built on it can be traced back to the precise location it came from, both because the eventual footnote (5.8) requires it and because you yourself will need, months later, to find the document again to check a reading. Record the full provenance at the moment of capture, into the note, never trusting memory to reconstruct it. The second is **securing access in advance**: sources held only behind a connection that may be unavailable when you need them — a lending library that may not be reachable, a repository that may be inaccessible from where you are working — should be brought into local, durable storage before the writing session that depends on them, so that the work is never blocked by the absence of a source you knew you would need. The general principle is that the materials an argument rests on should be under your own control, verifiable and available, by the time you are writing the argument that rests on them.

## 8.8 Best-practice use of IT and AI

Two threads run through the tooling of this chapter, and it is worth drawing them out explicitly, because the same principle governs both ordinary IT and the use of AI: **automate or delegate the mechanical, never the judgement.**

For IT generally, this means letting software absorb the drudgery that does not require thought — citation formatting, metadata entry, bibliography generation, backup, synchronisation, full-text search across your library — precisely so that your attention is reserved for the work that does require thought. A well-built toolchain is one in which you never manually perform a task a machine can do reliably, and never let a machine perform a task that requires the judgement only you can supply. The line between the two is usually clear, and where it blurs the safe default is to keep the judgement yours.

AI tools sharpen this principle to a point, because they can convincingly appear to perform the judgement work, and the appearance is where the danger lies. The useful frame is the distinction between **formative** and **substitutive** use. Formative use deploys the tool to develop *your own* understanding and *your own* argument: as an interlocutor to stress-test reasoning and surface objections you had not considered (the steelmanning discipline of 4.5, outsourced to a tireless if unreliable sparring partner); as an explainer of unfamiliar concepts, methods, or historiographical positions, giving you orientation you then confirm against the literature; as a check on your own prose for the failures of Chapter 5; as a generator of counterarguments to test a thesis against. These uses are legitimate and genuinely valuable, and they share a structure: the tool assists the formation of an understanding that remains yours, and nothing the tool produces enters your work as either content or fact without passing through your own judgement and verification. Substitutive use, by contrast, has the tool *do the work that is supposed to be yours*: generating prose for submission, originating the analytical architecture of an argument, deciding the thesis or the structure, or standing in for the reading. The objection to substitutive use is not only the integrity rule that scholarly work must be your own; it is that substitutive use defeats the purpose, because the capacities a historian is training — to think by writing (5.1), to originate an argument, to hold a complex source in the mind — are precisely the capacities that atrophy when the work is outsourced, so that substitutive use erodes the very skill it appears to assist. The cold draft, written from your own notes in your own voice, is not an inefficiency to be optimised away; it is where the thinking happens and the voice forms, and protecting it is protecting the thing the training is for.

Several specific hazards demand a discipline of their own, and a critical interlocutor states them plainly rather than softening them.

The gravest is **fabrication**. These tools generate plausible text, and plausibility is not truth: they will produce citations to works that do not exist, page numbers that are invented, and quotations — attributed to historians or to historical figures — that were never written or said, all rendered in the confident register of fact. For a historian this is the most dangerous failure mode there is, because a fabricated citation or a misattributed quotation that reaches your footnotes is the kind of error that discredits a piece of work entirely and can follow a scholar's reputation. The non-negotiable rule that follows is absolute: **anything an AI produces that will enter your work as fact must be independently verified at the original source.** A reference from an AI is a lead to check, never a source to cite; a quotation from an AI is a string to confirm against the original text, never a quotation to reproduce. The tool may point you toward a source, but the source itself — read, located, verified — is the only thing that may be cited, and the verification is yours to perform every time, without exception.

A subtler hazard is **distortion in summary**. An AI summary of an argument flattens and smooths it, and in doing so can misrepresent exactly the things Chapter 6 says to attend to — the structure of the disagreement, the unstated assumptions, what the framework cannot see. A summary is at best an orientation, useful for deciding whether and how to read a work; it is never a substitute for reading the work you intend to argue with, because you cannot criticise (6.2) an argument you know only through a lossy compression of it, and you will mistake the compression's flattening for the work's actual shape. Use summary to triage, never to engage.

Finally, **institutional norms and disclosure**. The rules governing AI use in scholarship are evolving rapidly, vary by institution and by context, and are frequently stricter than a user might assume — and they differ sharply between settings, with the expectations around application materials and formal publication typically the most demanding. For work that represents you to others as your own — application materials above all, where the work is taken as direct evidence of your own capacities, and formal publication, where authorship carries specific responsibilities — the safe and correct default is conservative use, the kind that leaves the substance unambiguously yours, together with whatever disclosure the relevant rules require. When in doubt, consult the actual current policy of the institution or publisher in question rather than relying on a general impression, because the impression is likely to lag the rule, and the cost of a misjudgement in this area is high. The through-line across every one of these hazards is the same: the AI is an interlocutor and a mechanical assistant, never a source of facts, never a source of citations, and never the author. Hold that line and the tool is genuinely useful; blur it and it becomes, sooner or later, a liability.

## 8.9 The workflow as a whole

Assembled, the pieces form a single pipeline that is the method of this handbook given a working body. A question, sharpened from a topic (4.2), drives a triaged programme of reading. The reading is captured into a reference manager that owns the bibliographic truth, and processed — in your own words, with the three layers kept distinct — into literature notes and then into atomic permanent notes that are your building blocks. The reading journal tracks the obligations the reading incurs: the verifications owed, the counterevidence to be confronted, the inconsistencies to be criticised. The permanent notes, linked and gathered into a map of content, incubate the structure of the argument, so that the map becomes the topic-sentence spine of a draft. The draft is written section by section — early and iteratively, because writing is how the gaps are found — and then revised twice over: once for the argument (Chapter 4), confronting its counterevidence and supplying its warrants, and once for the prose (Chapter 5), restoring the agency the passive deletes and cutting the hedges that carry no information. The footnotes are generated from the locators captured at the point of reading, so that every claim is traceable to the source a reader could check. And the whole, throughout, is protected by versioned backup against the loss that synchronisation cannot prevent. Each stage instantiates an operation the earlier chapters described; the workflow is what lets those operations be performed not once, on a single source, but across the hundreds of sources and the months of work that a real piece of history demands.

## 8.10 Main conclusions

-   **The workflow is the method made physical, and serves two masters:** not losing things (capture, traceability, backup) and developing understanding (processing notes into thought, not hoarding). Neglect either and the thinking degrades.

-   **Research is iterative, not a waterfall.** Read toward a question, write throughout rather than at the end (writing is how you find the gaps), and alternate reading, noting, and drafting rather than sequencing them.

-   **Notes come in three kinds — reference, literature, permanent — and the disciplines above them matter most:** write literature notes in your own words (the understanding test and the anti-ventriloquism of 4.6); keep the three capture layers (source's exact words, your paraphrase, your commentary) visually distinct to prevent inadvertent plagiarism; resist the collector's fallacy; and let maps of content evolve into argument scaffolds.

-   **Tag the reading journal as a queue of obligations,** not a filing system: every *to verify* is a debt to pay before use, every *disagree* a steelman owed (4.5), every *inconsistent* a thread for source criticism.

-   **Divide the toolchain between a reference manager (bibliographic truth) and a plain-text workshop (thinking),** linked by a stable citation key, with bibliographic data entered once and citations generated mechanically. Automate the mechanical so no automation touches the judgement.

-   **Synchronisation is not backup.** Keep versioned, tested, 3-2-1 backups of irreplaceable work, and store the durable record in plain text, which outlives any single program.

-   **Treat your own transcripts as derived sources with an error layer:** verify machine transcription against the image before citing, keep the image alongside the transcript, record full provenance at capture, and secure access to sources in advance of the writing that depends on them.

-   **With AI, automate or delegate the mechanical, never the judgement.** Formative use (stress-testing, orientation, prose-checking) develops an understanding that stays yours; substitutive use (generating prose, originating arguments) both breaches integrity and atrophies the skill it appears to assist. Verify every AI-produced fact, citation, and quotation at the source without exception; treat summaries as triage, not engagement; default to conservative, disclosed use where work represents you as your own. The AI is an interlocutor, never the author.

## 8.11 Further reading

*Ahrens, Sönke (2017) —* How to Take Smart Notes*.* The fullest case for the note system of 8.3 — the literature/permanent distinction, the discipline of writing in your own words, the collector's fallacy, and the claim that a well-built note system is a thinking instrument rather than a storage system. Repetitive in places, but the core argument is the right one and worth absorbing.

*Eco, Umberto (1977; English translation 2015) —* How to Write a Thesis*.* Pre-digital in its mechanics and the better for it, because the principles show through the obsolete card-index detail: how a research project is scoped, how reading becomes argument, how the work is structured over time. Wise, humane, and history-adjacent in sensibility; read it for the shape of the process (8.2), not the technology.

**The Programming Historian (programminghistorian.org).** An open, peer-reviewed collection of practical tutorials for digital historical method, including handwritten-text recognition, reference management, and text analysis. The place to go for the concrete, current how-to of the tools this chapter treats at the level of principle — and free, which the books are not.

**On AI: current institutional and disciplinary guidance, not a fixed text.** The norms governing AI in scholarship are moving too fast for any book to remain authoritative, and they vary by institution, publisher, and context. The correct reference is therefore the actual current policy of the body whose standards apply to the work in hand — a prospective department's admissions guidance, a journal's authorship policy, a university's academic-integrity code — consulted directly rather than through general impression. The principles of 8.8 are stable; the rules are not, and the rules are what you will be held to.

## Coda: the operations as one method

The handbook has run to eight chapters, and the operations they describe are, in the end, one. Source criticism (Chapters 1 and 2) establishes what your evidence can bear. The criticism of quantitative evidence (Chapter 3) extends that discipline to data, which is evidence that disguises its own partiality behind a number. Argument construction (Chapter 4) turns the criticised evidence into a claim, by making explicit the inference to the best explanation that the whole enterprise rests on. Prose (Chapter 5) is the medium in which that argument either keeps its precision or loses it, because in history the sentence is where the thinking is. Historiography (Chapter 6) situates the argument in the conversation that gives it meaning and tests whether it contributes or merely redescribes. The forms of output (Chapter 7) fix what any of this is for on a given occasion, because the genre of the deliverable determines what counts as the work being done well, and scopes the ambition to what the occasion can honour. And the workflow (Chapter 8) is the body that lets all of this be done at scale and over time, without loss and with understanding.

The unifying commitment beneath them is a particular intellectual honesty: the refusal to claim more than the evidence bears, whether the overclaim takes the form of a source pressed past what it can prove, a number reported to a false precision, an argument that suppresses its counterevidence, a sentence whose syntax asserts an agency the record will not support, a citation deferred to instead of assessed, a contribution claimed where only redescription has occurred, an output scoped beyond what its constraints can honour, or a workflow that hoards instead of understanding. Method, on this view, is not a set of techniques bolted onto curiosity but the form that disciplined honesty takes when it is applied to the recovery of the past from the traces it happens to have left. The techniques of this handbook are the particular shapes that honesty assumes at each stage of the work.

## Glossary of Key Terms

**Advance versus accumulate** (4.1) — The test distinguishing an argument from a survey: an argument advances, leaving the reader argumentatively further along at each step, whereas a survey merely accumulates facts. Applied at the scale of the paragraph (4.4) and of the whole work.

**Agentless passive** (5.3) — A passive construction that deletes the actor ("the decision was taken"). Legitimate where agency is genuinely unknown or diffuse, but an evasion when used to avoid committing to who acted; in a discipline whose work is the attribution of agency, every passive should be a decision rather than a reflex.

**Argument from silence (argumentum e silentio)** (1.4) — The inference from the absence of a specific expected item in the record. Licensed only where there are positive grounds to expect the item's presence; otherwise the silence carries no weight. See also Two registers of absence.

**Classification instability** (3.4) — The change over time in the definitions of the categories ("occupation," "unemployment," "crime") through which historical data was recorded, so that a series spanning a redefinition measures the shifting taxonomy as much as the changing world.

**Collector's fallacy** (8.3) — The mistaking of the accumulation of sources and notes for intellectual progress. A note never processed into one's own words, linked, or used is not an asset but disguised procrastination.

**Competence versus contribution** (7.2) — The master distinction among output genres: apprentice genres (writing sample, MA dissertation) are assessed on demonstrated competence ("can this person do history well?"), while professional genres (article, monograph) are judged by whether they advance knowledge. Confusing the registers is a category error in either direction.

**Corroboration and convergence** (1.3) — The earning of a claim's warrant through the agreement of several sources. Probative only to the degree the sources are independent; agreement among sources sharing an origin adds confidence without adding evidence. See Independence.

**Defeasibility** (1.5) — The property of a historical claim of being open to overturning by new evidence or a better explanation. A claim's resistance to such overturning measures its strength rather than guaranteeing its truth.

**Descriptive versus informative abstract** (7.10) — A descriptive abstract states what a piece is about (its topic and method); an informative abstract states what it argues and finds. Scholarly work needs the informative kind, and writing the descriptive kind by mistake — omitting the findings — is the characteristic error.

**Ecological fallacy** (3.6) — The unlicensed inference from an aggregate-level correlation to an individual-level relationship. Aggregate and individual correlations can differ even in sign; aggregate data licenses only aggregate claims.

**Evidence** — See Source versus evidence.

**False precision** (1.5, 3.2) — The reporting of a result, often numerical, to a resolution the evidence cannot support: a confidence interval on non-sampled data, a figure carried beyond its real accuracy, or a flatly asserted claim where the evidence supports only a probable one.

**Formative versus substitutive use (of AI)** (8.8) — Formative use deploys a tool to develop one's own understanding and argument (stress-testing, orientation, prose-checking); substitutive use has the tool do the work that should be one's own (generating prose, originating arguments). The latter both breaches integrity and atrophies the skill it appears to assist.

**Genre, as constraint and as information** (2.1, 7.1) — The kind of a document or deliverable, known before reading or writing. The genre of a source tells you in advance how it was produced and where to aim suspicion; the genre of an output is a constraint structure that determines what counts as the work being done well.

**Hedge** — See Qualifier versus hedge.

**History versus historiography** (6.1) — History is the past and its study; historiography is the study of the writing of history — the body of scholarship and the analysis of how it took its shape. A secondary work is an argument about the past and, at the same time, a primary source for the discipline of its own moment.

**Independence (of sources)** (1.3) — The condition, required for corroboration to be probative, that sources do not derive from a common origin, channel, or interest. The burden of demonstrating independence rests on the historian; even professionals mistake shared-origin material for independent confirmation.

**Inference to the best explanation** (1.5) — The inferential structure of historical argument (also called abduction): the conclusion warranted is not the one merely possible or merely consistent with the evidence, but the one that explains it better than the available rivals, judged on explanatory breadth, economy of assumption, coherence, and survival of objection. Comparative and defeasible.

**Leaching** (1.1) — The error of letting a source's genuine security as evidence for a modest claim seep across into a maximal claim it cannot support (a show-trial transcript that reliably records a confession read as proof of guilt). The commonest single failure in historical argument.

**Literature review as argument** (6.7) — A review of scholarship understood not as a catalogue of who said what but as an argument about the state of the field, organised by the structure of the debate and ending by exposing the problem the writer's own work addresses.

**Map of content (as argument scaffold)** (8.3) — A note that gathers and orders the permanent notes on a topic, allowed to evolve from a navigational index into the structured outline — the topic-sentence spine — of an eventual chapter or essay.

**Over-scoping** (7.2) — The cardinal cross-cutting failure of output: attempting more than the word limit, the time, the sources, or the writer's competence can sustain, producing superficiality. Depth is bought only by limiting breadth.

**Paragraph as a move** (4.4) — The principle that each paragraph should make one identifiable move in the argument — advance one claim — rather than contain one topic. Diagnostic: state in a sentence what the paragraph does to the argument, not what it is about.

**Prose choices as analytical choices** (5.1) — The governing claim of the chapter on writing: because the discipline's questions are about agency, causation, and degree, the grammatical subject, the modality, and the inherited vocabulary each make substantive claims, so that loose prose performs loose analysis.

**Qualifier versus hedge** (4.3, 5.4) — A qualifier states a claim's strength and carries information about how to hold it ("almost certainly"); a hedge ("perhaps," "arguably") dissolves the claim and carries only the writer's anxiety. Test: removing a qualifier changes the strength claimed; removing a hedge changes only the confidence of the tone.

**Redescription versus contribution** (6.6) — The distinction, central to positioning, between genuinely advancing knowledge and merely restating an existing position in new vocabulary. A reframing earns the name of contribution only if it generates a different handling of the evidence — a different reading, prediction, or decidable claim.

**Reified collective subject** (5.3) — A grammatical subject that attributes unitary agency to a collective ("Germany decided," "the army wanted") which did not act as one, smuggling in a substantive and possibly unsupported claim. Unpacking it into specific actors usually improves the analysis.

**Selection is argument** (4.7) — The principle that choosing what to include and what to leave out is itself a primary way an argument is made; the kitchen-sink inclusion of everything known argues nothing.

**Source versus evidence** (1.1) — A source is anything surviving from the past that can be interrogated; evidence is a source put to work in relation to a specific question. Nothing is evidence in the abstract, so the question is never "is this a good source?" but "what is this good evidence for?"

**Steelman** (4.5) — To confront the strongest version of an opposing argument rather than a weakened caricature (a strawman). Required because, under inference to the best explanation, a claim earns its standing only by surviving the strongest objection.

**Survivorship and selection bias** (3.3) — The systematic difference between the units that left records and those that did not, where the filter is usually correlated with the variable under study. The pervasive, silent threat to quantitative history, and the corpus-level form of the second register of absence.

**Sync is not backup** (8.6) — The principle that a synchronisation service propagates deletions and corruptions across devices and so cannot protect against error; recovery requires separate, versioned, tested backups (the 3-2-1 rule).

**Three-layer capture** (8.3) — The discipline of keeping, in any note drawn from a source, three things visually distinct: the source's exact words (quoted, with locator), one's paraphrase, and one's own commentary. Prevents the later conflation that produces inadvertent plagiarism.

**Three kinds of note** (8.3) — Reference notes (bibliographic facts, in the reference manager), literature notes (what a source says, in one's own words), and permanent notes (one's own ideas, atomic and linked). Keeping them distinct prevents characteristic confusions.

**Two registers of absence** (1.4) — The distinction between a specific expected-but-missing item (the strict argument from silence, sometimes usable as positive evidence) and the systematic non-survival of whole classes of source (corpus-level survival bias, a hazard to guard against). "We have no sources" is not "it did not happen."

**Warrant** (4.3) — The statement of why a given piece of evidence supports a given claim — the connective reasoning that licenses the move from grounds to conclusion, and the component most often missing from inexperienced argument. In history, the warrant is frequently an act of source criticism.

**Witting and unwitting testimony** (1.2) — The witting testimony of a source is what its author intended to convey; the unwitting testimony is what it reveals incidentally. A purpose-built source is least reliable on its witting message and often most reliable on what it discloses in spite of itself.
