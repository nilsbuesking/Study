# Chapter 6 — Working with Historiography

## 6.1 History and historiography

A distinction has been running beneath the whole handbook and now needs stating outright: the difference between history and historiography. History, loosely, is the past and the study of it; historiography is the study of the writing of history — the body of scholarship a subject has accumulated, and the analysis of how and why that scholarship took the shape it did. The primary sources of Chapters 1 and 2 are the historian's evidence about the past. The secondary literature — the books and articles other historians have written — is something else, and the commonest confusion at this level is about exactly what.

The secondary literature is, first, a set of arguments to be criticised, not a set of authorities to be cited. This is the governing principle of the chapter, and it inverts the instinct most students bring. A historian's having argued something is not evidence that it is so; it is an argument that it is so, and an argument is to be assessed, by the same standards of inference to the best explanation you would apply to any reasoning, rather than deferred to because of who made it. The footnote that cites a distinguished historian as though the citation settled the matter is the argument-from-authority version of the 4.6 failure — the quotation that argues for you — and it abdicates the analytical work that engaging with the literature is supposed to perform.

The secondary literature is, second and more subtly, itself a kind of primary source — for a different question. A book by Lefebvre or Cobban is evidence about the past it studies only at one remove, through the quality of its argument; but it is direct, first-order evidence about the discipline at the moment it was written — about what questions seemed worth asking, what frameworks were available, what was politically and intellectually at stake for historians of that generation. Reading a secondary work this way, as a document of its own moment, is what allows you to understand a historiographical tradition rather than merely to inventory it, and it is the application to scholarship of exactly the source criticism Chapter 2 applied to documents. This chapter, then, is about two operations: reading the literature analytically, and positioning your own argument within it. They are the same skill viewed from two directions, because you cannot locate your own argument in a conversation you have not first learned to hear.

## 6.2 Reading a secondary work analytically

To read a secondary work analytically is to attend to *how* it argues, not only to *what* it argues — to decompose it into its working parts rather than to extract its conclusion. The conclusion is what a careless reader takes away; the apparatus is what a useful reader examines. A reliable decomposition asks a stable set of questions of any monograph or article.

What is the thesis — the central contestable claim — and what question is it the answer to? (The question is often unstated and must be reconstructed; recovering it tells you what the work is really doing, because, as 1.1 insisted, the question shapes everything that counts as an answer.) What evidence and method support the thesis, and what is the work's relationship to the sources — does it rest on new archival research, on the reinterpretation of known material, or on the synthesis of others' findings? (The three carry different authority and are vulnerable in different ways: a synthesis inherits the weaknesses of what it synthesises; a reinterpretation stands or falls on whether its reading is better than the one it displaces.) What does the work argue *against* — who is its antagonist? (Almost every serious work is positioned against prior scholarship, and finding the antagonist locates the work in its debate and reveals what it takes to be the error worth correcting.) And, most demanding, what does the work's framework make visible, and what does it occlude? Every analytical framework is a way of seeing that is also, necessarily, a way of not seeing: it foregrounds some kinds of evidence and some kinds of cause and pushes others out of view, and the blind spot is not a defect peculiar to bad work but a structural feature of all of it. Identifying what a powerful framework cannot see is frequently where the opening for your own contribution lies.

The deepest move here is to apply the witting/unwitting distinction of 1.2 to the secondary work itself. Its witting testimony is its explicit thesis — what it sets out to argue, and the part it has defended most carefully. Its unwitting testimony is everything it discloses without meaning to: the assumptions it does not argue because it does not notice it is making them, the silences where its framework has nothing to say, the period preoccupations legible in what it finds important. A secondary work is least reliable, exactly as a primary source is, on the questions it was built to settle — there its author has marshalled every resource — and most revealing, often, in what it takes for granted. The assumptions a historian shares with their generation are precisely the ones they do not defend, because they did not appear to need defending, and those unexamined commitments are both the work's vulnerability and your best evidence about the intellectual world that produced it.

## 6.3 The historian's authority is not evidence

It bears separating out and stating plainly, because the instinct it corrects is so strong: deference is not analysis. The eminence of a historian, the prestige of a press, the frequency with which a claim is repeated — none of these is evidence for the claim, and treating them as such is a failure of exactly the kind the whole handbook works against. A claim repeated through a literature without ever being re-examined acquires the *appearance* of corroboration while having none, because the repetitions are not independent (1.3): they descend from a common source, and a hundred citations of one unverified assertion are one assertion, not a hundred. The discipline is to trace a load-bearing claim back to the evidence on which it originally rested, and to assess that evidence, rather than to weigh the claim by the company it keeps.

This does not license the opposite error, which is to treat expertise as worthless and to imagine that every question must be reopened from the sources by each new historian. Expert consensus has rational weight: it is, defeasibly, evidence that the arguments behind it have been tested and have survived, and a lone student who dismisses a settled finding without engaging the reasoning behind it is not being rigorous but reckless. The point is not to disregard authority but to understand what kind of thing it is — a summary of arguments that have so far held, not a substitute for them — so that you extend it the provisional, defeasible weight that surviving arguments deserve, and remain ready to reopen it when you have a reason that engages the argument rather than merely the conclusion. To defer is to treat the consensus as a fact; to engage is to treat it as the current best explanation, held exactly as strongly as the reasoning that supports it and no more. The second is the historian's stance.

## 6.4 Mapping a debate: schools, axes, and kinds of disagreement

A mature field is not a heap of individual opinions but a structured set of positions, and learning to map that structure is what turns a reading list into a comprehended debate. The lazy map is the spectrum — the placing of every historian on a single line, optimist to pessimist, left to right, traditionalist to revisionist — and it is lazy because it assumes the disagreement has one axis when it usually has several, and because a one-dimensional line flattens distinctions that matter. The better map identifies the *axes* of disagreement: the distinct questions on which historians divide, which may be orthogonal to one another, so that two historians can agree on one axis and oppose each other on a second.

The analytically decisive move in mapping a debate is to diagnose what *kind* of disagreement is in play, because the kind determines what, if anything, could resolve it, and misdiagnosing the kind is how historians talk past one another. Disagreements come in distinguishable types. Some are **evidential** — about what the sources actually show — and these are, in principle, the kind archives can settle: new evidence, or the correction of a misread source, can move them. Some are **methodological** — about how the sources should be read, what counts as a valid inference, how a measure should be constructed — and these are not settled by more evidence, because the parties disagree about what to do with the evidence they have. Some are **interpretive** — about what an agreed body of fact *means*, what framework best makes sense of it — and these turn on the inference-to-best-explanation criteria rather than on the discovery of new facts. Some are about **the question itself** — about what is even worth asking — and here the parties are not giving rival answers to one question but pursuing different questions, so that they are not, strictly, disagreeing at all but passing each other by. And some are **evaluative** — about what matters, what counts as significant or as a good outcome — and these reach beyond what evidence can adjudicate into the values the historian brings.

The standard-of-living debate of 3.5 is a clean specimen of how these layers stack within a single controversy. Part of it is evidential (what do the wage and height series show?); part is methodological (how should the cost-of-living deflator be constructed, and does signature literacy measure literacy?); and part is evaluative and conceptual (what *is* a standard of living — material consumption, biological welfare, the autonomy and texture of working life?), so that historians who agree on every number can still disagree about the answer because they disagree about the question the numbers are meant to answer. To map this debate as a single optimist–pessimist spectrum is to miss that its persistence is not stubbornness about the evidence but a genuine layering of evidential, methodological, and evaluative disagreement, only the first of which more research could touch. When you map a debate, separate the layers, and ask of each disagreement what could in principle resolve it; the answer tells you whether you are looking at a question for the archive, a question for method, or a question that will not be settled because it is, at bottom, about what to value.

## 6.5 The generational structure of debate

Historiographical debates have histories, and positions within them are very often reactions to predecessors rather than independent responses to the evidence. The recurring shape is generational: an orthodoxy is established; a revisionist generation attacks it, frequently by turning new evidence or new methods against its weakest points; and a post-revisionist generation works in the rubble, salvaging what survived the attack and building a more qualified settlement. Recognising this shape protects you from two errors — taking the current orthodoxy as simply *true* rather than as the latest product of this process, and reading older historians as merely benighted rather than as having asked the questions their own moment made askable.

The historiography of the French Revolution is the textbook case, and it will serve as this chapter's running example. For much of the twentieth century the orthodoxy was the "social interpretation," elaborated by Georges Lefebvre and Albert Soboul among others: the Revolution as a bourgeois revolution, the rising capitalist middle class overthrowing a feudal aristocratic order, a decisive stage in a broadly Marxist scheme of social development. The revisionist attack, opened by Alfred Cobban and pressed by others, was in large part evidential and prosopographical: examined closely, the revolutionary "bourgeoisie" turned out to consist substantially of officeholders, lawyers, and professionals rather than capitalist entrepreneurs, and the "feudalism" they supposedly overthrew did not match the social reality the category was meant to name — the orthodoxy's central terms, in other words, did not fit the people and structures they purported to describe. François Furet then made a different kind of move altogether: rather than offering a rival answer to the social-causation question, he *shifted the question*, displacing social class as the central category and reinterpreting the Revolution through its political culture, language, and ideology. This is the 6.4 point in motion — Furet's intervention was not a disagreement with Lefebvre on the same axis but a change of axis, a claim that the question the social interpretation had been answering was the wrong question to centre. To place Lefebvre, Cobban, and Furet on a single spectrum would entirely miss that Cobban disputed the orthodoxy's evidence while Furet disputed its question, and that these are different operations with different implications.

Reading the older work in its context completes the discipline. The social interpretation was not a naïve error to be congratulated on having transcended; it was a powerful framework that organised an enormous amount of evidence and answered the questions a particular intellectual and political moment found pressing, and it is itself a primary source (6.1) for that moment. To judge it only by whether it survived later criticism is to commit, against historians, the anachronism Chapter 1 warned against committing against historical actors: the judging of people by knowledge and standards not available to them. Understand why a historian asked what they asked, with what available to them and what at stake, before you weigh how well their answer has lasted.

## 6.6 Positioning your own argument

Everything so far serves the practical end of this chapter, which is locating your own argument in the conversation you have learned to hear. The space to occupy lies between two failures. One is **capitulation**: deferring to the literature so completely that you have no argument of your own, only a summary of others' — the literature review that never turns into a thesis. The other is **ignoring**: writing as though the scholarship did not exist, reinventing what is known and colliding with established findings in ignorance of them — which an examiner reads not as originality but as not having done the reading. Between them lies positioning: stating precisely how your argument stands in relation to what has been argued, which requires both that you have an argument and that you know the conversation well enough to say where yours fits.

The available moves are several, and naming them helps you see which one you are actually making. You may **extend** an existing argument to new evidence or a new case; **qualify** it, accepting it within limits you specify; **complicate** it, showing it to be true but more entangled than its proponents allow; **synthesise** two positions previously held to be opposed; **overturn** a claim by defeating the evidence or reasoning behind it; or **shift the question**, arguing — as Furet did — that the field has been centring the wrong problem. Each is a legitimate contribution, and stating which you are making disciplines your own sense of what you have actually done.

The most important warning in this section concerns a move that masquerades as the others: **redescription mistaken for contribution.** It is possible to take an existing, well-understood position, restate it in a new and more congenial vocabulary, and persuade yourself that you have offered a fresh interpretation when you have only relabelled an old one. This is the most seductive of self-deceptions in positioning, because the new vocabulary genuinely feels like new understanding — the relabelled position seems sharper, more yours — while adding nothing the field did not already possess. The test for whether a reframing is a contribution or a redescription is exact and worth applying mercilessly to your own work: **does the reframing generate something the prior description did not** — a different reading of specific evidence, a different prediction about what the sources will show, a different research programme, a claim that could be true under your description and false under the old one? If it does, it is a contribution, and the new vocabulary is earning its keep by doing analytical work. If it does not — if everything that was true under the old description remains true under yours and nothing new becomes visible or decidable — then however satisfying the new terms, you have redescribed, not contributed, and the honest recognition of that is worth more than the comfortable belief otherwise. A reframing that cannot be cashed out into a different handling of the evidence is a change of clothes, not a change of position, and the discipline is to demand of your own best ideas that they pay this price before you credit them as new.

## 6.7 The literature review as argument

The literature review is where positioning is performed, and it is the place students most reliably revert to the container of 4.1. A review is not a catalogue — not a sequence of "X argued this, Y argued that, Z took a different view" — because a catalogue makes no argument and therefore does no work; it merely demonstrates that reading has occurred. A review is an argument about the *state of the field*: it is organised by the structure of the debate (the axes and kinds of disagreement of 6.4), not by author or by chronology, and its purpose is to bring the reader to see the problem, gap, or unresolved tension that your own work will address. It ends, in effect, by earning your thesis — by showing why, given where the conversation has reached, the intervention you are about to make is the one the field needs.

The organising principle is therefore the same as for any argument: it advances rather than accumulates. A catalogue accumulates names; an analytical review advances toward a problem, each paragraph establishing another feature of the debate's structure until the opening for your contribution stands exposed. The catalogue treats the historians as a list to be got through; the review treats them as the holders of positions whose relations to one another — where they genuinely disagree, where they only seem to, what each can and cannot see — are the actual subject. The difference is visible in the topic sentences (4.4): in a catalogue they name historians ("Cobban argued…"); in a review they name moves in the debate ("The orthodoxy's central categories proved vulnerable on precisely the evidential ground it had claimed as its strength…"), with the historians appearing as the agents of those moves rather than as the items of a list.

## 6.8 Worked example: from catalogue to engagement

The distinction is clearest on a single passage. Here is a catalogue, on the historiography of the French Revolution:

> Historians have long debated the nature of the French Revolution. Georges Lefebvre and Albert Soboul argued that it was a bourgeois revolution that overthrew feudalism. Alfred Cobban disagreed, arguing that the revolutionaries were not really a capitalist bourgeoisie. François Furet took a different approach, focusing on political culture and ideology rather than social class. More recently, historians have continued to debate these questions. This essay will build on this scholarship.

Diagnose it against the chapter. It is organised by author, not by the structure of the debate (6.7); its topic sentences name historians rather than moves. It registers that Cobban "disagreed" and Furet "took a different approach" without diagnosing what *kind* of disagreement each represents (6.4) — and so misses that Cobban contested the orthodoxy's evidence while Furet contested its question, which are different operations with different consequences. It gestures at "more recently… continued to debate" without saying what is now at stake, the evasion of a writer who has not mapped the present state of the field. And it closes with the emptiest of positioning moves — "will build on this scholarship" — which is capitulation in the costume of engagement (6.6): it commits to no move, locates no gap, and earns no thesis. The passage proves that reading happened and does nothing further.

Now an engagement with the same materials:

> The interpretive history of the French Revolution turns less on a single disagreement than on a shift in what historians have taken the central question to be, and the distinction matters for how the debate can be carried forward. The mid-century orthodoxy — the social interpretation of Lefebvre and Soboul — answered a question about social causation: which class made the Revolution, and against which order? Its vulnerability, exposed by Cobban and the revisionists, was evidential and lay precisely on the ground it had claimed as its strength: the revolutionary "bourgeoisie," examined closely, proved to be officeholders and professionals rather than the capitalist entrepreneurs the framework required, and the "feudalism" they were said to overthrow fitted the social reality poorly. Had the debate stopped there, it would have remained a dispute on a single axis — a better or worse answer to the social-causation question — resolvable, in principle, by further prosopographical evidence. Furet's intervention changed its shape, because he did not offer a rival answer to that question but argued that it was the wrong question to centre, displacing social class in favour of the Revolution's political culture and language. This is why the field cannot be mapped as a spectrum from orthodox to revisionist: Cobban and Furet were not at different points on one line but operating on different axes, the one disputing the orthodoxy's evidence and the other its question. The unresolved problem this leaves — and the opening this essay addresses — is that the evidential and the political-cultural readings have largely proceeded as alternatives rather than being brought to bear on each other, so that what a social analysis corrected by Cobban's evidence would look like *once reconnected to* Furet's political-cultural frame remains substantially unattempted.

The engagement organises by the structure of the debate, diagnoses the kinds of disagreement at work, declines the false spectrum, reads each move for what kind of move it is, and ends by locating a specific problem — and a specific, statable contribution (reconnecting the corrected social analysis to the political-cultural frame) that is a synthesis move in the sense of 6.6, not the empty "building on" of the catalogue. Whether that contribution is genuine or a redescription would then face the 6.6 test: does reconnecting the two frames generate a different reading of specific evidence, or merely a more capacious vocabulary? The engagement has at least put a claim on the table that the test can be applied to, which the catalogue never did.

## 6.9 Main conclusions

-   **The secondary literature is argument to be criticised, not authority to be cited.** A historian's having argued something is an argument, assessed by the standards of inference to the best explanation, not evidence settled by eminence; and repetition across a literature is not corroboration when the repetitions are not independent (1.3).

-   **Read a secondary work by decomposing it** — thesis, question, evidence and method, antagonist, and above all what its framework makes visible and what it occludes — and apply the witting/unwitting distinction to it: it is least reliable on what it was built to argue, most revealing in the assumptions it does not defend.

-   **Authority is defeasible weight, not fact.** Neither defer to consensus as settled truth nor dismiss it as worthless; treat it as the current best explanation, held exactly as strongly as the reasoning behind it.

-   **Map a debate by its axes, not a spectrum, and diagnose the kind of disagreement** — evidential, methodological, interpretive, about-the-question, or evaluative — because the kind determines what could resolve it, and misdiagnosis is how historians talk past each other.

-   **Debate is generational.** Orthodoxy, revision, post-revision; positions are often reactions to predecessors; the current orthodoxy is itself a product of the process and a primary source for its moment, to be read in its own context rather than judged anachronistically.

-   **Position between capitulation and ignoring,** and name your move — extend, qualify, complicate, synthesise, overturn, or shift the question. Beware above all **redescription mistaken for contribution**: a reframing earns the name of contribution only if it generates a different handling of the evidence, not merely a more congenial vocabulary.

-   **The literature review is an argument about the state of the field,** organised by the structure of the debate and ending by earning your thesis — not a catalogue of who said what. Its topic sentences name moves, not authors.

## 6.10 Further reading

*Green, Anna, and Kathleen Troup (1999) —* The Houses of History: A Critical Reader in Twentieth-Century History and Theory*.* The most practical single book for the work of 6.4: an anthology of the major schools and turns — empiricism, the Annales, Marxist history, the cultural turn, and the rest — each introduced and then represented by a primary text, so that you can hear the traditions in their own voices and learn to map them. The book to use for building the mental map this chapter assumes.

*Iggers, Georg G. (1997) —* Historiography in the Twentieth Century: From Scientific Objectivity to the Postmodern Challenge*.* The clearest narrative survey of how the discipline's frameworks succeeded one another across the twentieth century — the generational structure of 6.5 traced across the field as a whole. Read it for the through-line that connects debates which otherwise look unrelated.

*Breisach, Ernst (1983) —* Historiography: Ancient, Medieval, and Modern*.* The long reference work, for when you need to place a tradition in the full sweep rather than the twentieth century alone. Not to be read through so much as consulted, but unmatched for situating a school in the longer history of historical writing.

*Furet, François (1978; English translation 1981) —* Interpreting the French Revolution*.* Doubles as the source behind the worked example and as a model of the positioning move it describes — the argument that a field has been centring the wrong question, executed at the highest level. Read it to watch a major reframing being performed, and then apply the 6.6 redescription test to it yourself: a useful exercise precisely because the answer is not obvious.
