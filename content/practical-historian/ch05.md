# Chapter 5 — Writing, Style, and the Form of Historical Prose

## 5.1 Prose is not the dressing on the argument

It is tempting, having built an argument, to treat the writing of it as cosmetic — a matter of making sound reasoning presentable. This is a mistake, and in history a peculiarly costly one. The prose is not a layer applied over the argument; it is the medium in which the argument exists, and in a discipline that cannot offload its precision onto formalism the way the sciences can, the precision of the thought lives or dies in the precision of the sentence. A historical claim that is loosely written is loosely *made*: the looseness is not a presentational flaw concealing a sound claim beneath, but is the claim, now imprecise. This chapter's governing proposition is therefore stronger than the usual injunction to write clearly. It is that **in history, prose choices are analytical choices** — that the grammatical subject you select makes a claim about who acted, the modality you reach for makes a claim about how confident the evidence permits you to be, and the vocabulary you inherit makes claims, often without your noticing, that may not be the claims you intend. Bad historical prose does not merely obscure good analysis; it performs bad analysis, asserting things the writer did not mean and failing to assert things the writer did. To write well, in history, is to think with precision in public, and the operations that follow are the operations of that precision.

## 5.2 The register of historical prose

Historical writing occupies a register of its own, between the sciences and the rest of the humanities, and recognising what that register demands clarifies much of what follows. The sciences can lean on notation and formal apparatus to carry precision, so that their prose may be plain to the point of plainness without loss; the precision is elsewhere. Some literary and theoretical writing, at the other pole, may be deliberately suggestive, generative, open — its value partly in what it opens rather than what it pins down. History can do neither. It has no formalism to offload precision onto, so the precision must be carried in ordinary prose, in the exactness of the word and the structure of the sentence; and its claims are answerable to evidence and must be stated definitely enough to be tested, so it cannot retreat into the merely suggestive. Historical prose is thus precise ordinary-language prose, and the discipline of the register is to achieve in plain words a precision that other fields achieve by other means or do not seek.

The conventional register is one of controlled authority: confident without stridency, qualified without timidity, written in the past tense for events and what is sometimes called the analytical present for the claims of historians ("Robinson and Gallagher argue," even of an argument made decades ago, because the argument is treated as a present fact of the literature). It avoids two opposite vices. The first is the breathless — the inflation of significance, the reaching for drama, the prose that tells the reader how to feel rather than giving them grounds to judge. The second, more common in academic work and more insidious, is the bureaucratic: the nominalised, agentless, abstraction-clogged prose that mistakes opacity for rigour and sounds learned only because it is hard to read. The register to aim for is neither; it is the prose of a writer who knows precisely what they are claiming and how strongly, and says it in the fewest words that carry that precision.

## 5.3 Syntax as analysis: agency and the grammatical subject

The most important thing this chapter has to say is about the grammatical subject, because in history the subject of a sentence is a claim about who or what acted, and the discipline's central questions are questions about agency.

Consider the agentless passive: "mistakes were made," "the decision was taken," "war was declared." The construction deletes the agent — it reports an action while declining to say who performed it — and in a field whose analytical work is very largely the attribution and distribution of agency, the reflexive passive is the silent abdication of that work. Sometimes the passive is exactly right: when the agent is genuinely unknown, when the agent is truly diffuse and naming one would falsify, or when the proper focus of the sentence is the thing acted upon rather than the actor. The point is not to ban it — a blanket prohibition is itself a failure of judgement — but to make every passive a *decision* rather than a reflex, because each one is a choice not to name an agent, and that choice should be made because the analysis warrants it, not because naming the agent was hard. When you find a passive in your draft, ask whether you are using it to make a claim (the agency was genuinely diffuse) or to avoid making one (you have not determined, or do not wish to commit to, who acted). The first is analysis; the second is evasion wearing the costume of objectivity.

The opposite error is subtler and, in writing about states and institutions, more pervasive: the **reified collective subject**. "Germany decided," "the army wanted," "Britain feared," "the working class believed" — each attributes unitary agency to a collective that did not, in fact, act or want or fear as one, and in doing so each smuggles in a substantive claim (that the collective was a unitary agent) which the writer may not intend and the evidence may not support. The phrase "Germany sought colonies" is not a neutral shorthand; it is an assertion that there was a single German purpose, and if the historical reality was a contest among ministries, commercial lobbies, and individuals pursuing divergent aims, then the smooth collective subject has erased precisely the analysis the history requires. This is the syntactic form of a methodological commitment, and it cuts to the centre of how causation and responsibility are assigned: to write "the system produced an outcome no one chose" is a different claim from "the minister chose the outcome," and a different claim again from "the outcome was reached" — and the three are three different histories, distinguishable only in the syntax. The discipline is to notice when your grammatical subject is a collective, and to ask whether you mean to claim that the collective acted as one. If you do, and can defend it, keep it. If you do not, unpack the collective into the specific actors whose agency you can actually evidence — which will frequently improve the analysis, because the act of unpacking forces you to determine who actually did what, the very question the reified subject let you skip.

## 5.4 Modality: the calibration of confidence

Chapter 4 distinguished the qualifier, which states a claim's strength and matches it to the evidence, from the hedge, which dissolves the claim into nothing. At the level of prose, this distinction becomes a discipline of modality — of the words by which you signal how strongly a claim is held — and getting it right is one of the surest marks of a controlled historical writer.

A qualifier carries information: "almost certainly," "the balance of the evidence suggests," "probably," "possibly but on undocumented grounds" each tells the reader something specific about how to hold the claim, and the calibrated "almost certainly a forgery" of 1.6 was a qualifier doing exactly its job. A hedge carries only anxiety: the reflexive "perhaps," the throat-clearing "it could be argued that," the apologetic "to some extent," "in a sense," "arguably," scattered through a draft not to calibrate any particular claim but to avoid the discomfort of commitment. The test that separates them is exact and worth applying mechanically: remove the modal word and ask what changes. If removing it changes the strength the sentence claims — makes a properly tentative claim sound certain — it was a qualifier, and it was carrying information; restore it. If removing it changes nothing except to make the sentence more direct and more confident, it was a hedge, and it was carrying only the writer's nerves; cut it. A paragraph from which every hedge has been cut and every genuine qualifier retained reads as the work of someone who has decided what they think and how strongly the evidence lets them think it — which is precisely the impression of controlled authority 5.2 described.

The calibration runs in both directions, and the opposite failure is as real. Under-hedging — the bald assertion of what is in fact uncertain, the flat declarative where the evidence supports only a probable — is the false precision 1.5 warned against, now committed in prose. To write "MacDonald formed the National Government out of principled conviction" where the evidence supports only "his conduct is consistent with conviction but does not exclude ambition" is to claim, in the syntax of certainty, more than you can defend, and an examiner reads the overclaim as a failure of judgement exactly as they read the dissolving hedge. The instrument of modality exists to make the prose say neither more nor less than the evidence warrants, and the well-calibrated sentence is one in which the modality is invisible because it is exactly right — present where uncertainty is real, absent where the claim is secure, and never deployed as a nervous reflex in either direction.

## 5.5 The inherited vocabulary carries arguments

A term of art is rarely neutral, and the words a historian inherits — from the participants and from previous historians alike — frequently carry arguments folded invisibly inside them. To use such a word without noticing what it commits you to is to let your nouns argue for you, and to make claims you never examined.

Two sources of loaded vocabulary deserve vigilance. The first is the participants' own language — the witting testimony of 1.2 in lexical form. When the actors in a period coined a phrase to frame their situation favourably, adopting that phrase as your descriptive vocabulary adopts their framing: to write of a "stab in the back" without flagging the term is to ventriloquise a political myth; to call a colonial war a "pacification" is to assume the conclusion the participants wished assumed. The second source is historiographical: the schools and traditions that study a period coin terms that are themselves compressed arguments. "Appeasement," "totalitarianism," "the scramble for Africa," "decline," "the Eastern Question," "feudalism" — each carries an interpretive payload, and to deploy one as though it were a transparent label is to take, unawares, the position the term encodes. The connection to 4.6 is direct: there it was the close paraphrase that smuggled in a source's framing; here it is the single loaded word doing the same work more economically. And the connection to Chapter 6 is anticipatory: a historiographical school's vocabulary is its argument in portable form, and using the vocabulary is half-joining the school.

The discipline is not to purge all loaded terms, which is impossible and would leave you mute — much of the indispensable vocabulary of history is load-bearing in this way. It is to know which of your terms carry arguments, and to use them deliberately: either committing to the framing knowingly, because you have judged it correct and will defend it, or flagging the term to mark your distance from its payload ("what contemporaries called the 'pacification'"), or substituting a more neutral formulation where neutrality serves the analysis. The unforgivable move is the unconscious one — to have taken a substantive position because a word came pre-loaded and you did not stop to weigh it. Read your own draft for its nouns, and ask of the load-bearing ones whether you meant to assert what they assert.

## 5.6 Narration and analysis

History is distinctive among the disciplines in that it must both narrate and analyse — both tell what happened and explain it — and managing the relationship between the two is a craft the sciences and the analytical humanities largely escape. The two modes fail in opposite directions when used alone. Pure narration without analysis is the chronicle of 4.7: a sequence of events recounted without the reasoning that would make it an argument, leaving the reader informed of what happened and innocent of why it matters. Pure analysis without narration drifts toward the unanchored and the abstract, loses the reader who needs to see the sequence to follow the claim, and — more seriously — can lose the contingency and texture that are themselves historically significant, flattening a process that mattered as a process into a static set of factors.

The skilled writer braids the two, and the braiding is governed by the argument's needs rather than by default. Narration is used selectively, where the argument requires the reader to see a sequence unfold — to grasp the order in which things happened, because the order is part of the claim (the chronology that served as warrant in 4.8 is narration doing analytical work), or to feel the contingency of an outcome that hindsight has made to seem inevitable. Analysis is used to step back from the sequence and weigh it. The shift between the modes should be deliberate and, usually, signalled, so that the reader knows whether they are being shown what happened or being told what to make of it. The default mode of an analytical essay is analysis; narration is the instrument reached for when the argument needs the reader to see motion, and a writer who narrates by default — who tells the story and hopes the analysis will be inferred — has reverted to the chronicle. The question to ask at each transition is whether the reader needs, at this point, to watch the events move or to stand back and judge them; the answer dictates the mode.

## 5.7 Rhythm, emphasis, and the architecture of the sentence

Below the level of these large choices sits the craft of the individual sentence and paragraph, where rhythm and structure carry emphasis whether or not the writer intends them to, so it is better to intend them. A few principles do most of the work, and this section is deliberately brief because the principles are simple even though their execution is not.

Vary sentence length, and use the variation. A short sentence after several long ones lands with force precisely because of the contrast; a paragraph of uniformly long sentences exhausts the reader and buries its own emphases, while a paragraph of uniformly short ones reads as a list and denies the reader the subordination that shows how ideas relate. Subordination is itself analysis: what you place in the main clause and what you consign to the subordinate clause is a claim about relative importance, and the same facts subordinated differently make different claims — "although the army remained loyal, the coalition had already fractured" and "although the coalition had fractured, the army remained loyal" emphasise opposite things from identical materials. Exploit end-weight: the end of a sentence is its stress position, and the most important word or idea should fall there rather than trailing off into qualification, just as the most important sentence of a paragraph usually belongs at its end or its beginning rather than buried in the middle. And cut the throat-clearing with which nervous prose begins its sentences — "It is important to note that," "It can be seen that," "It is interesting that" — which delays the actual claim and signals that the writer is warming up rather than speaking. Begin with the claim. The reader will decide for themselves whether it is important and interesting, and telling them in advance only postpones the evidence on which that judgement depends.

## 5.8 The footnote: what it can legitimately do

The footnote is part of the argument, not an administrative appendix bolted on for form's sake, and treating it as part of the argument disciplines its use. It has three legitimate functions. The first and primary is citation: locating the evidence for a claim so that a reader can find the source and apply their own source criticism to it — which is the basic scholarly contract, and the point at which the warrant of 4.3 is made checkable. Every claim of fact should be traceable to the evidence behind it, because the reader is entitled to verify the move from source to claim that the main text asserts, and an unsourced claim of fact asks for a trust the scholarly form does not extend. The second function is the substantive or discursive note: a qualification, a subsidiary point, a complication that bears on the claim but would derail the main text if admitted to it, parked in the note where the interested reader can find it and the main argument can proceed unencumbered. The third is the historiographical note: situating a claim against the literature — registering who has argued otherwise, where the debate stands — without cluttering the text with a literature review the argument does not need at that point.

The illegitimate uses are the mirror of the legitimate. The footnote as dumping ground — the repository for material the writer could not bear to cut but could not justify including — is cutting deferred, not cutting done, and the reader feels the indiscipline. The footnote that hides a weakness — that buries an inconvenient qualification or a contrary source where it will be least noticed — is a form of the suppression 4.5 condemned, merely relocated. And the padding citation, the reference appended to look learned rather than to locate evidence, is throat-clearing in the apparatus. The test for any note is the test for any sentence: does it do work the argument needs? If it locates evidence, qualifies precisely, or situates honestly, it earns its place. If it exists to store, to hide, or to impress, it does not.

## 5.9 Worked example: revising for prose

As in 4.8, the principles are clearest applied to a passage. Here is one that is not argumentatively empty — it has real content — but stylistically poor, on removed terrain: the partition of Africa.

> It is important to note that the scramble for Africa was perhaps one of the most significant developments of the late nineteenth century. Africa was partitioned by the European powers in a remarkably short space of time. The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 was convened, and rules for the partition were agreed upon. Europe was, to some extent, driven by a combination of economic, strategic, and prestige motives. Germany sought colonies, while Britain was concerned to protect its existing interests. It could be argued that the continent was carved up with little regard for its inhabitants, and by 1900 almost all of Africa had been brought under European control.

Diagnose it. It opens with throat-clearing ("It is important to note that") and immediately hedges its own significance into mush ("perhaps one of the most"). It uses the inherited loaded term ("the scramble") as though it were neutral, when it is a contemporary coinage carrying a whole framing (5.5). It is built on agentless passives — "Africa was partitioned," "was convened," "were agreed upon," "had been brought under control" — that delete the actors from a passage whose entire subject is who acted (5.3). Where it does supply subjects, they are reified collectives — "Europe was driven," "Germany sought," "Britain was concerned" — asserting unitary purposes the evidence may not support. And it hedges throughout ("to some extent," "It could be argued") in the nervous register that carries no information (5.4). The content is not wrong; the prose makes claims the writer did not examine and fails to make the claim the evidence supports.

Now a revision, fixing the prose — and watch what the fixes do to the analysis:

> The partition of Africa — "the scramble," in a contemporary coinage that already carried the breathless tone of the thing it named — compressed the European seizure of almost an entire continent into barely two decades. The grammatical temptation is to write that "Europe partitioned Africa," but the abstraction conceals the analysis, because there was no single European agent and the actors were various and frequently at cross-purposes. Bismarck's government convened the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 not to partition the continent but to regulate a competition already under way among chartered companies, missionary societies, individual adventurers, and rival foreign ministries — initiatives the metropolitan governments often ratified after the fact rather than directed from the start. To write that "Germany sought colonies" attributes to a state a settled purpose that the German colonial push, owing as much to commercial lobbies and to Bismarck's tactical calculations within European politics as to any national design, did not possess. Unpacking the collective subjects is where the analysis is won: once "Britain was concerned" and "Germany sought" are resolved into the specific governments, companies, and men who were concerned and who sought particular things, the partition ceases to resemble the coordinated design that the smooth passive "Africa was partitioned" implies, and begins to look like what the term, taken literally, describes — a scramble: a set of competing and loosely controlled initiatives that the metropolitan states raced to legitimise.

The revision cuts the throat-clearing and the hedges; it handles the loaded term by flagging it rather than swallowing it, and then earns the right to use it literally at the close. It replaces the agentless passives and reified collectives with named and graded agents — and in doing so it is *forced into the analysis the original avoided*, because deciding who to make the subject required deciding who actually acted, which surfaced a substantive claim (the partition as uncoordinated scramble ratified after the fact, rather than coordinated design) that the original prose had no means of making. That is the chapter's whole argument in miniature: the prose discipline was not cosmetic. Fixing the sentences fixed the thinking, because in history the sentences are where the thinking is.

## 5.10 Main conclusions

-   **In history, prose choices are analytical choices.** Loose writing is loose thinking made public; the precision of the claim lives in the precision of the sentence, because the discipline has no formalism to carry it elsewhere.

-   **The register is precise ordinary-language prose in a key of controlled authority** — confident without stridency, qualified without timidity — avoiding both the breathless and the bureaucratic.

-   **The grammatical subject is a claim about agency.** Make every passive a decision, not a reflex (it deletes the agent); and resist the reified collective subject ("Germany sought") that asserts a unitary agency the evidence may not support. Unpacking the collective usually improves the analysis by forcing you to determine who actually acted.

-   **Calibrate modality in both directions.** Keep the qualifier that carries information about a claim's strength; cut the hedge that carries only anxiety (test: does removing the word change the strength claimed, or only the confidence of the tone?). And do not under-hedge into the false precision of the bald declarative.

-   **The inherited vocabulary carries arguments.** Participants' phrases and historians' terms of art alike come pre-loaded; know which of your nouns are load-bearing positions, and use them deliberately, flag them, or replace them — never adopt a framing unawares.

-   **Braid narration and analysis to the argument's need.** Narrate where the reader must see a sequence move; analyse to weigh it; signal the shift; and do not revert to the default chronicle that tells the story and hopes the analysis is inferred.

-   **Build the sentence for emphasis** — vary length, subordinate to show importance, exploit end-weight, cut the throat-clearing — and **treat the footnote as part of the argument**, locating evidence and qualifying honestly, never storing, hiding, or padding.

## 5.11 Further reading

*Gay, Peter (1974) —* Style in History*.* The history-specific treatment and the one to read first: close studies of the prose of Gibbon, Ranke, Macaulay, and Burckhardt, arguing that in history style and substance are not separable — that how these historians wrote was inseparable from what they were able to claim. The scholarly vindication of this chapter's governing proposition.

**Orwell, George (1946) — "Politics and the English Language".** An essay, and the indispensable short statement on the agentless passive, the dead metaphor, the inflated diction, and the way slovenly prose enables slovenly and evasive thought. Directly behind 5.3 and 5.5; read it for the polemic and the examples, with the caveat that its rules are best taken as provocations rather than laws.

*Pinker, Steven (2014) —* The Sense of Style*. The modern, linguistically informed treatment, and the corrective to Orwell where Orwell overreaches: Pinker is precise about when the passive is the right choice (cohesion, the proper management of given and new information) rather than always a vice, which sharpens 5.3 beyond a blanket suspicion. The book on this list most attentive to* why the rules are rules.

*Sword, Helen (2012) —* Stylish Academic Writing*.* Specifically on academic prose and the antidote to its characteristic bureaucratic register — evidence-based, drawn from a large survey of what published scholarship actually does, and unusually concrete about how to escape the nominalised, agentless default that 5.2 warns against. The most practical of the four for the particular task of writing scholarship rather than prose in general.
