# Chapter 2 — Primary Source Analysis by Type

## 2.1 Why genre is itself evidence

Chapter 1 argued that a source has no significance until a question is put to it. This chapter adds a second principle that operates before you have read a single word of content: **the genre of a source is itself a piece of information.** A Foreign Office minute, a parliamentary speech, a published memoir, a private letter, a propaganda poster — each is produced under characteristic conditions, for characteristic purposes, by characteristic processes, and those conditions stamp the source with characteristic strengths and characteristic distortions *regardless of what it happens to say*. Knowing what kind of document you are holding tells you, in advance of reading it, where to aim your suspicion and what the source is likely to be good evidence for.

This is why "primary source analysis" cannot be a single technique. The questions you bring to a diplomatic dispatch — who drafted it, for which reader, through what chain of revision, with what awareness that other governments might one day see it — are not the questions you bring to a diary, where the live issues are contemporaneity, intended audience, and the gap between the private and the performed self. A reader who applies one undifferentiated suspicion to everything will mistrust the wrong things. The genre-specific reflexes in this chapter are an extension of the witting/unwitting distinction of 1.2: each genre has a characteristic witting register (what documents of this kind are *for*) and therefore a characteristic location for its unwitting testimony (what documents of this kind disclose in spite of themselves). What follows works through the principal genres in turn. In every case the structure is the same: what the genre is, the production conditions that shape it, where its distortions characteristically lie, how to read it against the grain, what it cannot tell you, and a worked example.

## 2.2 Diplomatic documents

Diplomatic records are the most institutionally layered of all the genres, and the layering is the first thing to understand. A single "document" — a dispatch from an ambassador, say — is rarely the utterance of a single mind at a single moment. It is the visible tip of a process: the man on the spot reports; a desk officer in the ministry minutes the report, summarising and assessing it for superiors; the assessment is itself minuted by more senior officials, each adding a layer of interpretation in the margin; and a political decision, when it comes, is taken on the basis of this accreted commentary rather than on the raw original. The British Foreign Office "minuting" tradition is the classic instance: the substantive thinking often lives not in the dispatch but in the marginal minutes written up the chain of command, where officials say what they actually think in a way the formal record never does. The practical instruction is to find the whole process, not just the headline document. The dispatch tells you what was reported; the minutes tell you how it was received, and the second is usually the more revealing.

The production conditions impose a characteristic distortion: diplomatic documents are written in awareness of multiple audiences, including hostile and future ones. A foreign ministry knows that its archives may one day be opened, captured, or selectively published; an ambassador writes partly for the historical record and partly to protect his own position; formal notes between governments are composed in the knowledge that they may be published to justify a course already chosen. The formal, foreign-facing layer of the diplomatic record is therefore the most performed and the least candid — it is the genre's witting testimony at its most managed. (This is also precisely why a published diplomatic document *collection*, edited after the fact by a government with an interest in the verdict, demands the source-criticism of 1.4 applied to the collection as a whole, and not merely to the documents within it.)

How to read against the grain: separate the registers. Treat the formal note or published dispatch as evidence of position-taking — of what a government wished to be seen to hold — and look to the internal minutes, the private telegrams, and the unguarded marginalia for evidence of what was actually thought. What diplomatic documents cannot reliably tell you, on their own, is what was decided in the conversations that left no paper, or the real weight of a consideration that it was impolitic to record. Absence here is governed by 1.4: a matter may be missing from the file because it was settled face to face, not because it was unimportant.

*Worked example.* In December 1935 the British Foreign Secretary, Samuel Hoare, and the French premier, Pierre Laval, agreed a secret plan to end the Italo-Abyssinian war by handing Mussolini the greater part of Abyssinia. The plan was leaked to the French press, the public reaction in Britain was ferocious, and Hoare was forced to resign. The episode is a clean demonstration of why the diplomatic record must be read in layers. The public British position — support for the League of Nations and for collective security against Italian aggression — was the witting, foreign-facing testimony. The Hoare–Laval terms were the private reality, and they existed in documents never meant to surface. Read only the formal, published layer and you reconstruct a Britain committed to collective security; read the leaked private layer and you find the same government privately negotiating the dismemberment of the victim. Neither layer is "the truth" tout court; each is reliable evidence for a different question — the public layer for the position Britain wished to occupy, the private layer for the policy it was actually prepared to pursue — and the historian's task is to hold both and explain the distance between them, not to choose one and discard the other.

## 2.3 Official, administrative, and legislative records

This genre comprises the state recording its own workings and the society it governed: the debates of legislatures, the reports of official inquiries and commissions, and the routine files of administration — censuses, police records, registers, tax rolls. Its great strength is coverage and continuity; its great weakness is that the categories through which the state recorded the world were the state's categories, and they encode the assumptions, priorities, and blind spots of the recording apparatus rather than a neutral description of reality.

Begin with legislative debates, because here a specific and common error needs correcting. Students routinely treat *Hansard* — the report of debates in the British Parliament — as though it were a verbatim transcript. It is not, and never has been. The reports were historically compiled and tidied, and members have long been able to correct the record of their own remarks; the printed speech is a smoothed and lightly edited version of what was said, not a recording of it. More importantly, a parliamentary speech is among the most purely *witting* of all sources: it is performance for the record, addressed simultaneously to the chamber, the press gallery, the constituency, and posterity. Read for what the speaker believed, it is treacherous; read for what a speaker judged it expedient to say in public, in that forum, at that moment, it is excellent. The unwitting testimony lies, as always, to the side of the performance — in the assumptions a speaker could take for granted in his audience, the arguments thought worth making, the things that did not need to be said because everyone present already shared them.

Official inquiries and royal commissions repay the same double reading. A commission is convened with a remit, and the remit, the selection of witnesses, and above all the framing of the questions put to them shape the evidence gathered — sometimes decisively. The depositions are answers to questions, and leading questions produce leading answers. Yet within that shaped frame, the testimony of witnesses — especially their incidental, unwitting detail about how life was actually lived and work actually done — can be among the richest social-historical evidence the period offers. The two readings must be kept apart: the commission's *conclusions* are evidence of the agenda that convened it; the witnesses' *incidental disclosures* are evidence of the conditions described, to the degree that the leading frame can be discounted.

*Worked example.* The English Poor Law Report of 1834, produced by the Royal Commission of 1832–34, is the textbook case of conclusions that ran ahead of evidence. The Commission's recommendations — the principles of "less eligibility" and the workhouse test that shaped the New Poor Law — substantially reflected the prior convictions of its leading members, and the vast body of testimony it gathered was organised and deployed to support a diagnosis that was, in its essentials, settled before the evidence was weighed. Used as evidence of *actual* conditions of poor relief across England, the Report must be handled with the greatest caution, because its selection and framing were governed by the case it existed to make — the leaching warning of 1.1 in institutional form. Used as evidence of the assumptions and anxieties of the reforming administrative mind of the 1830s — its model of the pauper, its fear of dependency, its confidence in deterrence — it is a document of the first importance. As so often, the source that fails for one question is decisive for another.

Routine administrative records — censuses above all — present a subtler version of the categories problem. The census does not merely count; it counts *under categories*, and those categories (of occupation, of household, of status) are constructions that change over time and impose a grid on a social reality that did not come pre-sorted into them. To use the census well you must understand the classification before you trust the count. This is the point at which the documentary analysis of administrative records hands over to the quantitative analysis of the data they contain, and that is the business of Chapter 3, where the instability of historical categories and the problem of the non-representative record are treated directly. Here it is enough to register the principle: an administrative record is a document before it is a dataset, and its categories are themselves unwitting testimony about the mind of the state that drew them.

## 2.4 The press: newspapers and periodicals

A newspaper is not a record of events; it is a manufactured daily product, shaped by ownership, commercial pressure, the technologies and economics of news-gathering, and an editorial line. Treating the press as a transparent report of what happened is the cardinal error. What a newspaper is reliably good evidence *for* is, first, what a particular readership was offered as its picture of the world on a given day, and second — read carefully and in the aggregate — something of the climate of opinion the paper both reflected and sought to shape. It is poor evidence, on its own, for the events it purports to describe.

Three production facts govern the genre. First, ownership and the proprietorial line: in the modern period the great popular papers were instruments of their proprietors as much as commercial enterprises — Northcliffe's control of the *Daily Mail* and *The Times*, Beaverbrook's of the *Daily Express*, are the British paradigms — and a paper's treatment of a question frequently reflects an owner's politics or a campaign he had chosen to wage. Second, the news agencies: much of what appeared in many mastheads originated with a handful of wire services — Reuters, Havas, Wolff and their successors — so that apparent corroboration across titles is frequently nothing of the kind. This is the independence problem of 1.3 in its purest commercial form: ten papers carrying the same agency dispatch are one source, not ten, and counting mastheads measures the reach of an agency, not the confirmation of a fact. Third, the internal differentiation of the paper: news report, editorial or "leader," correspondence, and the planted or "inspired" story occupy different evidential registers, and a competent reading distinguishes them. The leader is the paper's witting voice; the news columns are shaped but purport to report; the inspired story is an interested party using the paper as a conduit, and identifying it is a real source-critical skill.

How to read against the grain: ask who owned the paper, who its readers were, where a given item actually came from, and which register it occupies, before you ask whether what it says is true. What the press cannot tell you, on its own, is what actually happened, or what the public actually thought — only what it was told and offered. And the genre is subject to the manufactured silence of 1.4 as much as to distortion: what a paper chose *not* to print is often as significant as what it printed.

*Worked example.* The British press handling of the abdication crisis of 1936 is the genre's silence and its framing power in a single case. For weeks while the relationship between Edward VIII and Wallis Simpson was openly reported in the American and continental press, the British newspapers — by a tacit agreement among the proprietors — said nothing, so that the British public was kept in substantial ignorance of a constitutional crisis already unfolding in plain sight elsewhere. When the silence broke in early December, the framing was equally a product of editorial choice. A historian using the British press of November 1936 as evidence of "what was happening" would reconstruct a country with no monarchical crisis at all; the silence is evidence not about the King but about the press and its relationship to the establishment. Read for what it was offered to convey, the British press of those weeks tells you almost nothing true about the events; read for the production conditions that generated the silence, it is first-rate evidence about deference, proprietorial coordination, and the boundaries of acceptable public knowledge in 1930s Britain.

## 2.5 Memoirs and autobiography

Memoir is the genre most seductive to the inexperienced and most treacherous on its own terms, because it offers what looks like privileged inside testimony — the participant's own account — while being, of all the genres, the one most thoroughly shaped by the conditions of its production. Three of those conditions are decisive. It is *retrospective*: written after the outcome is known, and therefore pervaded by hindsight, which silently converts the contingent into the inevitable and the accidental into the intended. It is *purposive*: memoirs are written to vindicate, to settle scores, to claim credit, to shape a reputation, or simply to earn money, and the narrative is bent to the purpose. And it is *reconstructive*: remembered dialogue, remembered dates, remembered motives are reconstructions, not recordings, and they drift toward the version the author has come to prefer.

The hindsight problem deserves emphasis because it is the hardest to detect. A memoirist knows how the story ended, and that knowledge reorganises the telling: decisions are narrated as though their consequences were foreseeable, warnings the author claims to have given acquire a prominence the contemporary record does not support, and the messy simultaneity of events is straightened into a clean line leading to the known result. This is teleology smuggled in as memory, and it is why a memoir's account of *intentions* and *foresight* is the part to trust least.

How to read against the grain: date everything against the contemporary record. A memoir's claims should be checked, wherever possible, against what the author and others wrote *at the time*, before the outcome was known — and where the memoir and the contemporary record diverge, the contemporary record almost always wins, because it is innocent of the hindsight that contaminates the memoir. The witting testimony of a memoir — the author's account of his own role, judgement, and motives — is its least reliable register. The unwitting testimony — the incidental texture of how an institution worked, the assumptions the author did not think to defend — survives the purpose better, exactly as 1.2 predicts.

*Worked example.* Winston Churchill's multi-volume histories of the two world wars are the supreme modern instance of memoir as a bid to control the historical record — the participant writing the history in which he intends to be the central and vindicated figure. (The line attributed to Balfour, that Winston had written a big book about himself and called it *The World Crisis*, captures the genre exactly.) The works are built on privileged access to documents, including Churchill's own contemporaneous minutes, which are quoted at length — and that apparent documentary rigour is precisely the trap, because the selection of which minutes to quote, and the framing around them, is itself an argument. The documents are real; the narrative they are marshalled to support is a case. The methodological lesson generalises to all participant-history: a memoir's use of genuine documents does not make the memoir's argument neutral, and the historian must reconstruct from the documents independently rather than accepting the author's curated path through them. The memoir is indispensable evidence — for the events, more cautiously; for the author's retrospective construction of himself, definitively — but it cannot be allowed to write the history, only to be evidence within it.

## 2.6 Diaries and private correspondence

Diaries and letters are commonly imagined as the most candid of sources — the private self, unguarded — and against the memoir they have one decisive advantage: contemporaneity. A diary entry or a letter is written before the outcome is known, and so escapes the hindsight that contaminates retrospective testimony. That is a genuine and important strength. But the "private" source is rarely as private as it looks, and the qualification is the heart of the matter.

Two production conditions shape the genre. First, the audience problem. A letter is written *to someone*, and its content is a function of the relationship between writer and recipient as much as of the events it reports: the same person describing the same episode to a parent, a minister, and a lover will frame it three ways, and none of the three is simply "the truth" — each is evidence of the relationship as much as of the event. A diary, likewise, has an implied reader, even when that reader is only the writer's own future self or an imagined posterity, and the self a diarist constructs on the page is a performance for that reader. Second, and relatedly, the awareness-of-posterity problem: many of the most valuable political and social diaries of the modern period were kept by people entirely conscious that they were creating a historical record, and that consciousness pulls the supposedly private document toward the self-presentation of the memoir. Contemporaneity protects against hindsight; it does not protect against vanity.

How to read against the grain: establish, for any letter, who the recipient was and what the relationship implied, and read the framing as evidence of that relationship; for any diary, ask who the diarist imagined would read it. What the genre cannot tell you, taken at face value, is the unperformed inner life — even the diary is a performance, merely a more intimate one. And these sources are subject to the manufactured silence of 1.4 in a particularly common form: published editions of diaries and letters have very often been expurgated, and the historian must establish whether the version in hand is the full text or a curated one.

*Worked example.* The diaries of Sir Henry "Chips" Channon, the Conservative MP and inveterate social observer of the 1930s and 1940s, illustrate every feature of the genre. They are contemporaneous and richly detailed, and their unwitting testimony about the texture of political and social life among the British governing class is of real value. But Channon wrote with evident awareness of an eventual readership and an unmistakable instinct for self-presentation, so that the "private" diary is also a performance of a self. And the genre's expurgation problem is built into their publishing history: the edition published in 1967 was heavily cut, and a great deal of what Channon actually recorded — political, personal, and sexual — was suppressed for decades, becoming available only with the later unexpurgated edition. A historian relying on the 1967 text would have encountered silences that were artefacts of mid-century editorial discretion, not of Channon's pen: the same trap as Beatrice's expurgation of Victoria's journals in 1.4, in a different register. The lesson is procedural and unglamorous but decisive: before you trust a diary or letter, establish which text you are reading.

## 2.7 Visual and material sources

The non-textual sources — photographs, posters, film, objects, buildings — are treated more briefly here, not because they are less important but because the principles already established transfer to them with only modest adjustment, and because their detailed analysis is a craft of its own that a methods handbook can point toward rather than exhaust.

The governing error is the myth of transparency, and it is most acute with the photograph. A photograph feels like a direct imprint of reality, and that feeling is exactly what must be resisted. A photograph is composed: someone chose the frame, and everything outside it is excluded by that choice; someone chose the moment; very often someone arranged the scene, and in the modern period someone could retouch the negative. Above all, the meaning of a photograph is usually fixed not by the image but by the *caption* — the same image will be made to say opposite things by different captions, and the caption is where the witting testimony lives. The image's unwitting testimony — the incidental detail in the background, the thing the photographer did not intend to record — is, as with the unwitting register everywhere, frequently the more reliable. Posters and propaganda graphics are read differently again: their value is rarely as evidence of the reality they depict and almost always as evidence of intended effect — what the propagandist believed would move a particular audience, which is unwitting testimony about the propagandist's model of that audience. Film and newsreel add the dimension of editing, and the constructed sequence must be read as construction. Material culture and the built environment — objects, monuments, the fabric of a city — are sources too, bearing evidence of the priorities and capacities of those who made and used them, though their interpretation requires techniques beyond this chapter's scope.

*Worked example.* The systematic retouching of photographs under Stalin is the visual genre's version of the manufactured record, and it ties the chapter's through-line together. As officials fell from favour and were purged, they were removed from the photographic record — airbrushed out of group portraits in which they had stood beside Stalin, so that the surviving image showed a history from which they had never existed. The case of Nikolai Yezhov, the secret-police chief excised from a photograph at the Moscow–Volga Canal after his own fall and execution, is the best-known instance, documented in David King's study of the practice. The methodological points converge here: the photograph is not transparent (it was altered); the alteration is a manufactured silence in the 1.4 sense (an absence that is an artefact of production, not of the past); and the retouched image, worthless as evidence of who stood by the canal that day, is first-rate evidence of the regime's compulsion to control the record and of the mechanics by which it did so. The doctored photograph fails utterly for one question and answers another decisively — which is, in the end, the lesson of the whole chapter.

## 2.8 Main conclusions

-   **Genre is information you have before you read a word.** Each document type is produced under characteristic conditions that stamp it with characteristic strengths and distortions; knowing the genre tells you in advance where to point your suspicion and what the source is likely to be good evidence for.

-   **Diplomatic records must be read in layers.** The formal, foreign-facing document is the most performed; the internal minutes and unguarded marginalia carry the candid thinking. Reconstruct the whole process, and treat published collections themselves as edited sources.

-   **Official records encode the state's categories, not neutral reality.** Read legislative debate as public performance (Hansard is not a verbatim transcript), read an inquiry's conclusions as evidence of its agenda and its witnesses' incidental detail as evidence of conditions, and remember that a census is a document before it is a dataset.

-   **A newspaper is evidence of what a readership was offered, not of what happened.** Establish ownership, readership, the actual origin of an item (the wire-service trap of 1.3), and which register — report, leader, or inspired story — it occupies; and weigh what was not printed.

-   **Memoir is the most seductive and least reliable genre on its own terms.** Retrospective, purposive, and reconstructive, it must be dated against the contemporary record, which wins where the two diverge; trust its incidental texture over its account of intentions and foresight.

-   **Diaries and letters buy contemporaneity at the price of the audience problem.** They are shaped by the writer–recipient relationship and by awareness of posterity, and their published editions are frequently expurgated — establish which text you are reading before you trust it.

-   **No source is transparent, the photograph least of all.** Composition, framing, captioning, and retouching make the image an argument; read its intended effect as evidence of the maker's mind, and treat the manufactured record (the airbrushed photograph, the silenced press, the expurgated diary) as evidence of the power that manufactured it.

## 2.9 Further reading

*Dobson, Miriam, and Benjamin Ziemann, eds. (2009) —* Reading Primary Sources: The Interpretation of Texts from Nineteenth- and Twentieth-Century History*.* The natural companion to this chapter, and the book to own if you read only one. Successive chapters take a single genre — diplomatic documents, newspapers, personal testimony, film, photographs, and more — and set out how to interpret it, with the methodological reflection this handbook compresses into a single chapter. Modern-period focused, which most source-criticism manuals are not.

*Marwick, Arthur (2001) —* The New Nature of History: Knowledge, Evidence, Language*.* The source of the witting/unwitting distinction used throughout, with extended practical guidance on the criticism of sources. Opinionated and combative, but unusually concrete about the actual operations of reading a document.

*Burke, Peter (2001) —* Eyewitnessing: The Uses of Images as Historical Evidence*.* The best single introduction to treating visual sources — paintings, prints, photographs, film — as evidence, and to the disciplines required to do so without naivety. The proper expansion of 2.7.

*King, David (1997) —* The Commissar Vanishes: The Falsification of Photographs and Art in Stalin's Russia*.* Doubles as the documentation behind the 2.7 worked example and as an object lesson in its own right: a visual demonstration, image by image, of the manufactured record, and of how comparison between versions exposes it. A book to look at as much as to read.

*Jordanova, Ludmilla (2000) —* History in Practice*.* Broader than this chapter, but excellent on the material and visual dimensions of historical evidence and on the relationship between sources and the practice that uses them; a good bridge toward the chapters on argument and historiography that follow.
