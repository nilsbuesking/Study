# The Practical Historian

## Contents 

**Chapter 1 — The Epistemological Foundation.** Source versus evidence, witting and unwitting testimony, independence, the argument from silence, and what "proof" means in history.

**Chapter 2 — Primary Source Analysis by Type.** Reading the diplomatic, official, press, memoir, diary, and visual genres against the grain.

**Chapter 3 — Quantitative Methods and Their Limits.** What data is as a source, and what quantitative instincts must unlearn in the archive.

**Chapter 4 — Essay and Argument Construction.** Thesis, warrant, the paragraph as a move, counterevidence, and the uses of quotation.

**Chapter 5 — Writing, Style, and the Form of Historical Prose.** Prose choices as analytical choices: agency, modality, and the inherited vocabulary.

**Chapter 6 — Working with Historiography.** Reading secondary work as argument, mapping a debate, and positioning a contribution.

**Chapter 7 — Forms of Output: Genre, Constraint, and Assessment.** What each kind of deliverable is for, what makes it good, and how it is judged — including the abstract.

**Chapter 8 — Research Workflow.** The system that performs all of the above at scale: notes, tools, backup, and the use of IT and AI.

**Coda; Glossary of Key Terms.**

## A note on what this handbook is, and is not

There is no shortage of books that tell you what historians have argued about the nature of their discipline. Marc Bloch's *The Historian's Craft* and John Tosh's *The Pursuit of History* between them give a reader the philosophical foundations of source criticism and a map of the major historiographical movements of the last century and a half. This handbook does not attempt to repeat that work, and it assumes you have done some version of that reading or will do it alongside this.

What those books do not do — what almost no methods book does well — is tell you how to perform the operations the discipline actually consists of. How do you read a diplomatic dispatch so that you extract from it what it can legitimately be made to yield, and no more? When is a number a piece of evidence and when is it a trap? How do you turn a folder of annotated sources into a paragraph that establishes a claim rather than merely gesturing at one? These are practical skills, learned in most British history degrees through three years of trial, correction in face-to-face tutorials, and imitation. The aim of this handbook is to make the tacit explicit: to set out, in usable form, the operations that the special subject and the dissertation will otherwise teach you only obliquely.

Each chapter is designed to be read on its own. But the practical chapters all rest on a small number of epistemological commitments, and if those commitments are left implicit the practical advice tends to float free of its justification — you end up with a list of rules whose rationale you cannot reconstruct, which means you cannot apply them to cases the list did not anticipate. This first chapter therefore does the foundational work. It is not philosophy of history for its own sake. It is the minimum conceptual apparatus you need in order for the rest of the handbook to make sense, and it is built around the distinctions that will recur, by name, throughout the later chapters.
