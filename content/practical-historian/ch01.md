# Chapter 1 — The Epistemological Foundation

## 1.1 The question is prior to the evidence

The single most consequential mistake an inexperienced historian makes is to treat sources as though they came pre-labelled with their significance — as though a document were intrinsically "important" or "reliable" in the way that a coin is intrinsically gold or base metal. Sources are not like that. A source acquires significance only in relation to a question, and the same source will be strong evidence for one question, weak evidence for a second, and no evidence at all for a third.

Consider Friedrich Engels's *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845). As a description of the material conditions of industrial Manchester — housing, sanitation, wages, mortality — it is evidence of a particular and contestable kind: the testimony of a young, politically committed German observer, working partly from his own observation and partly from the reports of others, with a thesis to demonstrate. Used naively as a transparent measurement of how the Manchester poor lived, it is treacherous, because the selection and framing of the misery were governed by the argument the book existed to make. But the same text is excellent, almost unimpeachable, evidence for a different question: what a particular strand of early socialist analysis took industrial capitalism to be, how it assembled an indictment of it, and which features of the industrial city such an analysis found salient. The book that must be handled with tongs as a source for Manchester's drains is a first-rate source for the formation of a political diagnosis. Nothing about the book has changed. What has changed is the question we are asking of it.

This has an immediate practical consequence, and it is the first habit to build: *do not ask whether a source is good. Ask what it is good evidence* for*.* The question "is this source reliable?" is malformed and unanswerable as stated. The questions "is it reliable evidence that a particular event occurred?", "is it reliable evidence of what was said at that event?", and "is it reliable evidence of the motive behind it?" are three different questions with three different answers — and they routinely diverge sharply.

The most dangerous version of forgetting this is what we might call *leaching*: letting a source's security as evidence for a modest claim seep across into a maximal claim it cannot support. Take the transcripts of the great Moscow show trials of 1936–38. The confessions of Bukharin and the other defendants are entirely secure evidence for one proposition — that elaborate confessions were produced, scripted, and performed under the conditions of a Stalinist political trial. They are evidence, that is, for the existence and character of the spectacle. They are worthless as evidence for the proposition the confessions assert: that the defendants had in fact conspired with foreign powers to wreck the Soviet state. A historian who slid from "the transcript reliably records that Bukharin confessed" to "the transcript shows that Bukharin was guilty as charged" would have let the document's genuine evidential security for the first claim leach into a second claim of an entirely different order. The transcript is not unreliable; it is simply reliable for something other than what its framers wished it to establish. Watch for this move constantly, in your own drafts above all. It is the most common single failure in undergraduate and even graduate argument, and we will meet it again in every chapter that handles a particular kind of source.

The terminology worth fixing here is the distinction between a **source** and a piece of **evidence**. A source is anything surviving from the past that a historian can interrogate: a treaty, a diary, a tax register, a photograph, a building, a parish record. Evidence is a source *put to work in relation to a specific question*. Sources exist in the archive; evidence exists only in an argument. The implication, which sounds pedantic but is not, is that there is no such thing as evidence in the abstract. When you find yourself wanting to describe a source as "important," stop and force the sentence to completion: important *for establishing what?* If you cannot finish the sentence, you have not yet thought clearly enough to use the source.

## 1.2 Witting and unwitting testimony

Every source was produced by someone, for some purpose, and that purpose shapes what the source says. The naive reading takes the source's own framing at face value: it treats a memoir as a record of what happened, a dispatch as a transcript of events, a statistical return as a measurement. The critical reading recognises that the source is itself an *act* — a memoir is an act of self-presentation, a dispatch is an act of bureaucratic positioning, a statistical return is the product of a collection apparatus with its own categories and incentives — and that reading the source means reading through the act to what produced it.

The most useful tool here is the distinction, named and developed by Arthur Marwick, between the **witting** and the **unwitting** testimony of a source. The witting testimony is what the author intended to communicate: the message, the argument, the version of events the source was constructed to convey. The unwitting testimony is everything the source reveals incidentally, without intending to — the assumptions its author took for granted, the things it lets slip, the categories it reaches for without reflection, the audience it assumes, the anxieties it betrays.

The practical principle that follows is counter-intuitive and immensely powerful: **a source is often most trustworthy precisely as evidence for the things it was not trying to tell you.** Where a source is pursuing a deliberate aim — defending a reputation, justifying a decision, persuading a reader — its witting testimony is exactly the part most distorted by that aim, and must be treated with corresponding suspicion. But the unwitting testimony is incidental to the aim, and so escapes the distortion. Bloch called these "witnesses in spite of themselves," and they are the historian's most reliable informants.

Take the *Mémoires* of Talleyrand, composed and arranged in old age by a man who had served the Bourbons, the Revolution, Napoleon, and the Bourbons again, and who had every reason to present that improbable career as a record of consistent principle rather than serial accommodation. The witting testimony — Talleyrand's account of his own motives, his insistence on an underlying constancy of purpose across the regimes he served and abandoned — is among the least trustworthy material in the entire memoir literature of the period, because no source was ever more purpose-built to launder its author's reputation. On those questions you read flatly against the text. But the same memoirs are full of unwitting testimony of real value: the assumptions they make about how diplomacy was actually transacted, the offhand detail of court and cabinet procedure, the casual disclosure of what a man of his formation took for granted about negotiation, secrecy, and the management of sovereigns. Talleyrand had no incentive to falsify the things he was not aware he was disclosing, because he did not think them remarkable. On the witting questions he is an advocate; on the unwitting questions he is, despite himself, a witness.

This is not a licence to believe whatever is convenient. The unwitting testimony can be wrong too — an author's unreflective assumptions may simply be mistaken about the world, and a procedural aside may misremember as easily as a self-justification may mislead. The point is more specific: the *direction* and *source* of likely distortion differ between the witting and the unwitting registers, and a competent reading treats them differently. When you annotate a source, it is worth literally separating the two: what was this author trying to tell me, and what is this author telling me without meaning to? The second column is frequently the more valuable.

## 1.3 Corroboration, convergence, and the problem of independence

Historical claims earn their warrant in one of two broad ways. A claim may rest on a single source whose reliability for that claim is secure — a peace treaty is good evidence of its own terms, because it is the instrument that constituted them. More often, and more interestingly, a claim earns its warrant through **convergence**: several independent sources, none individually decisive, point in the same direction, and the claim becomes credible because it is hard to explain the convergence except by supposing the claim true.

The load-bearing word in that sentence is *independent*, and it is the word inexperienced historians most often ignore. Convergence is evidentially valuable only to the degree that the converging sources are genuinely independent of one another — that is, only to the degree that they do not derive from a common origin. Two sources that ultimately trace back to the same informant, the same wire report, or the same prior document do not corroborate each other at all. They are one source wearing two coats, and the apparent agreement between them is not evidence of anything except that they share an ancestor.

The trap is everywhere in the modern period precisely because the modern period is so richly documented and its documents circulate so fast. Consider the accounts of the Peterloo massacre at St Peter's Field, Manchester, on 16 August 1819, when cavalry charged a mass meeting for parliamentary reform and killed and injured members of the crowd. The event generated a flood of competing testimony, and it is tempting to count up the reports on each side as though each were an independent witness: so many accounts agreeing that the crowd was peaceable and the yeomanry murderous, so many agreeing that the meeting was seditious and the magistrates' action necessary. But the reformist accounts substantially issued from, or were shaped by, the organisers and the radical press around Henry Hunt, and the loyalist accounts substantially issued from, or were shaped by, the magistrates and their allies who needed the action retrospectively justified. Two large clusters of mutually agreeing testimony are not two hundred witnesses; they are, to a first approximation, two interested parties, each amplified. The agreement within each cluster is largely a convergence of *interest and transmission*, not of *independent observation*, and counting heads inside a cluster tells you almost nothing.

What gives the historian of Peterloo any purchase is precisely the small body of testimony that can be shown to be genuinely independent of both interested clusters — the observer present for reasons unconnected with either side, whose account was not produced to serve the reformist or the loyalist case and who can therefore corroborate or contradict either. It is the independence of such testimony, not its quantity, that makes it probative. This is the discipline to internalise: whenever you find yourself relying on agreement between sources, ask explicitly where each source came from, and whether the agreement could be explained by shared descent or shared interest rather than by the truth of the claim. Draw the stemma if you have to — sketch the lines of transmission and provenance and see whether your "independent" sources collapse into one node upstream. If they do, you have less evidence than you thought, and the honest move is to say so. We will see in 1.6 that even a professional intelligence service, presented with a forged document, fooled itself precisely by mistaking shared-origin material for "independent confirmation."

## 1.4 The argument from silence, and the two registers of absence

Sometimes the most important thing about the record is what is not in it. But absence is radically ambiguous, and it is worth being precise about *which kind* of absence you are reasoning from, because two quite different things travel under the same name and they behave differently.

The first is the **argument from silence** in its strict sense (the *argumentum e silentio*): a *specific* item missing from a body of records where you would have expected it to appear. A meeting that left no minute, a decision that generated no surviving paper, a topic that the otherwise voluminous correspondence of a well-documented figure conspicuously avoids. This kind of absence can be genuinely informative — but only when a demanding condition holds: **you must have positive grounds for expecting the thing to be present in the record if it had occurred.** Silence is evidence of absence only against a background of expected presence. Where you would expect a phenomenon to have left a trace, and it has left none, the silence carries weight; where you have no such expectation, the silence carries nothing, and an argument built on it is worthless. The condition is exacting, because it requires you to know enough about the record-generating system — its routines, its completeness — to say that *this* kind of event would normally have generated *that* kind of document, which is nonetheless not there. That is a substantial claim about an archive, and it must be argued, not assumed.

The second register of absence is broader, and it is the one that lay behind the slogan that the mere absence of sources is not in itself evidence. This is the problem of the **surviving corpus as a non-random sample**. What reaches the archive, and what survives in it, is not a representative slice of the past: it is the product of differential creation (some activities and groups generate records, others do not), differential preservation (some records are kept, others discarded), and differential survival (fire, flood, war, and deliberate destruction). You therefore cannot reason from "we have no sources about X" to "X did not happen, or did not matter" without first accounting for whether sources about X would have been created, kept, and would have survived at all. Where they would not, their absence is no evidence whatever.

The Public Record Office of Ireland is the standing illustration of why this is not a pedantic caveat. In June 1922, during the Civil War, the Four Courts in Dublin were shelled and the record treasury destroyed by fire, taking with it centuries of administrative, legal, and genealogical material — including the bulk of the nineteenth-century Irish census returns. For vast tracts of Irish social history, the absence of documentation is an artefact of a single afternoon's destruction and tells you nothing about the past it would otherwise have recorded. To argue from that silence as though it reflected the historical reality, rather than the survival history of the archive, would be a gross error. The distinction between the two registers is therefore practical: the strict argument from silence is a tool you can sometimes *use* (a specific, expected-but-missing item as positive evidence), while corpus-level absence is mostly a hazard you must *guard against* (the systematic non-survival of whole classes of source). The second register returns in Chapter 3, where it reappears as survivorship bias in quantitative data — the same problem wearing the clothes of a dataset.

There is, finally, a more insidious version of the problem, which is silence that has been *manufactured* by the very source or collection you are relying on. Queen Victoria kept a daily journal for nearly seventy years. After her death, her youngest daughter, Princess Beatrice, transcribed the journals into new volumes at her mother's instruction, expurgating whatever she judged might embarrass the family — and destroyed each original volume as she copied it. Most of the originals from 1840 onwards no longer exist; what survives for those decades is Beatrice's expurgated and partly rewritten copy. A historian working from this copy and finding a silence at some delicate juncture would be encountering not a fact about Victoria's life but a fact about Beatrice's editing. The absence looks like the past; it is an artefact of the document's production. (The point is sharpened by the rare exception that proves it: for the early period, 1832–40, an independent transcript made for Lord Esher survives, so that the editing of the later, destroyed volumes can be partly calibrated against an uncensored yardstick for the years where one happens to exist. Where you have such a yardstick you can measure the bias; for the destroyed decades you cannot, and must reason accordingly.) The defence against manufactured silence is the source-critical reflex the next chapter develops at length: never treat an edited or published collection as a transparent window onto the underlying material without first asking what its editors selected, suppressed, rewrote, and arranged, and why.

## 1.5 What "proof" means in history

A reader trained in mathematics or in quantitative finance arrives at history with an instinct about what it would mean to establish a claim, and that instinct is misleading in ways worth making explicit, because the mismatch causes real confusion.

History does not prove things deductively. A mathematical proof establishes its conclusion with certainty, given its axioms, and once proved the theorem is not subject to revision by new information. Nothing in history works this way. Neither does history operate with the formalised probabilistic standards of statistical inference, where you can attach a calibrated confidence level to a result and defend it by reference to a sampling distribution. Historians sometimes borrow the *language* of probability — "almost certainly," "on the balance of the evidence" — but the borrowing is metaphorical. There is no sampling distribution behind a historian's "probably." Consider a question of the kind historians actually argue about: why William Gladstone converted to Irish Home Rule in 1885–86. Was it the conclusion of a long-maturing moral and constitutional conviction, or a calculated response to the parliamentary arithmetic produced by the Irish Nationalist bloc, or some interaction of the two? Each reading marshals real evidence; none can be settled by computation; and a historian who announced "70 per cent conviction, 30 per cent calculation" would be manufacturing a precision the evidence cannot bear and that the question does not even render meaningful. (Chapter 3 returns to this point in the specific context of quantitative evidence, where the temptation to import false precision is most acute and most damaging.)

What history actually does is **inference to the best explanation**, sometimes called abduction. You assemble a body of evidence — sources interrogated and put to work in the ways the preceding sections describe — and you ask: what set of past circumstances would best account for this evidence? The conclusion you are entitled to is not the one that is *possible* given the evidence (many things are possible) and not the one that is merely *consistent* with the evidence (consistency is a low bar that many rival explanations will clear), but the one that explains the evidence *better than the available alternatives*. Historical claims are therefore comparative and defeasible by their nature. They are comparative because their warrant is always relative to the rival explanations actually in play; they are defeasible because a new source, or a better explanation, can overturn them, and a claim's resistance to such overturning is a measure of its strength rather than a guarantee of its truth.

What makes one explanation better than another is not a single criterion but a cluster, and it is worth holding them in mind explicitly when you assess an argument — your own or another historian's. The better explanation accounts for *more* of the evidence, leaving fewer awkward residues that have to be explained away. It requires *fewer* unsupported auxiliary assumptions — every time an explanation has to posit an undocumented meeting, an unrecorded motive, or a conveniently lost letter in order to work, its cost goes up. It *coheres* with what is independently well established about the period, its institutions, and its actors. And it *survives the strongest objections* that can be raised against it, rather than holding only so long as the obvious counter-cases are not mentioned. An argument that scores well on these counts is strong; an argument that wins only because its rivals have not been stated is weak, however internally tidy, and the discipline of stating the rivals fairly is one we will return to in the chapter on historiography.

It is worth being candid that inference to the best explanation has a known soft underbelly, because pretending otherwise produces overconfident history. The deepest objection — associated in the philosophy of science with Bas van Fraassen and usually called the "bad lot" problem — runs: choosing the best of the explanations you have thought of guarantees nothing about its truth if the true explanation is one you never considered. The best of a bad lot is still bad. There is no fully satisfying answer to this, and the honest historian does not pretend there is one. What the objection demands, in practice, is a specific intellectual discipline: that you work actively to widen the field of candidate explanations before settling on one, that you treat the explanation you find most congenial with *extra* rather than less suspicion (because the risk is precisely that congeniality, not evidential force, is doing the selecting), and that you state your conclusions with the provisionality their inferential structure warrants. A claim advanced as the best current explanation, with its rivals named and its evidential basis exposed, is doing honest work. The same claim advanced as established fact is claiming more than the method can deliver, and an experienced reader will mark it down precisely for that overreach.

## 1.6 The contested source: a worked synthesis

The concepts of the preceding sections are not used singly. A genuinely difficult source forces you to deploy all of them at once, and it is worth working one example through to show how they interlock, because the rest of the handbook assumes you can do this without prompting.

The Zinoviev Letter is among the most consequential documents in modern British political history and almost certainly a forgery. Dated 15 September 1924 and purporting to be an instruction from Grigori Zinoviev, head of the Communist International, to the British Communist Party, it reached London through Secret Intelligence Service channels and was published by the *Daily Mail* on 25 October 1924, four days before a general election that the first Labour government, under Ramsay MacDonald, then lost. No original has ever been traced. The official inquiry conducted by the Foreign Office's then Chief Historian, Gill Bennett, and published in 1999, concluded that the document was a forgery — most probably the work of anti-Bolshevik Russian émigrés — though it found no firm evidence that it was directly the work of intelligence officers.

Work the source through the apparatus. First, the question-relativity of 1.1, and the warning against leaching. As evidence for its ostensible content — that the Comintern was issuing such instructions — the Letter is worthless, because it was not written by Zinoviev or the Comintern at all. But as evidence for a different question it is first-rate: the manufacture, transmission, and electioneering deployment of the Letter is excellent evidence for the conduct of British anti-socialist politics in 1924, for the porousness of the boundary between the intelligence services and partisan press, and for the techniques of what we would now call disinformation. The forged document is a genuine artefact of the politics that produced and used it. To slide from "the Letter is a forgery" to "therefore the Letter tells us nothing" would be to make the leaching error in reverse — discarding the source's real evidential value for one question because it fails for another.

Second, witting and unwitting testimony. The Letter's witting testimony — its purported message from Moscow — is pure fabrication and worth nothing on its own terms. But the surrounding documentary record of how it was handled yields rich unwitting testimony: the assumptions officials made about Bolshevik intentions, the institutional reflexes that governed how such a document was circulated and leaked, the anxieties of 1924 legible in the very readiness to believe. The participants were not trying to tell posterity about their own credulity and partisanship; they disclose it in spite of themselves.

Third — and this is the heart of the case — corroboration and independence. When Stanley Baldwin's incoming government examined the Letter's authenticity, the intelligence official Desmond Morton produced what he presented as several converging reasons for believing it genuine, including what he called "direct independent confirmation" from other sources in London. This is precisely the 1.3 trap, sprung on professionals. The "confirmation" was not independent: the corroborating material flowed from the same milieu, and in part the same channels, that had produced and promoted the Letter in the first place. What looked like multiple witnesses converging on the truth was, on inspection, a single tainted origin reflected back from several surfaces. The apparent corroboration multiplied confidence without adding evidence — the exact failure mode the requirement of independence exists to catch. Had the question "where does each of these confirmations actually come from?" been pressed, the convergence would have collapsed into its single contaminated node.

Fourth, the inferential structure of 1.5. The responsible conclusion about the Letter is not deductive certainty — no original survives, and absolute proof of forgery is unavailable — but inference to the best explanation. Forgery is the explanation that accounts for the most evidence (the untraceable original, the provenance through émigré and intelligence circles, the impeccable political timing, the textual features), requires the fewest strained auxiliary assumptions, and best survives the strongest objections. The genuineness hypothesis can be sustained only by accumulating improbabilities. That is why the scholarly verdict is "almost certainly a forgery" rather than either "proven forgery" or "we cannot say" — the phrase states a conclusion held as the best available explanation, with its residual uncertainty exposed rather than hidden. That calibrated phrasing is not weakness; it is what a properly structured historical conclusion looks like.

## 1.7 Main conclusions

-   *Ask what a source is good evidence* for*, never whether it is "good" in the abstract.* The same source is strong, weak, or null evidence depending on the question; a source's significance lives in an argument, not in the archive.

-   **Guard against leaching.** The commonest single failure in historical argument is letting a source's genuine security for a modest claim seep into a maximal claim it cannot support — the show-trial confession that reliably records a confession being read as proof of the guilt confessed.

-   **Distinguish witting from unwitting testimony, and place your suspicion accordingly.** A purpose-built source is least reliable on exactly the questions it was built to address, and often most reliable on what it discloses incidentally, in spite of itself.

-   **Corroboration is worth only as much as the independence of the sources corroborating.** Agreement among sources that share an origin, a channel, or an interest multiplies confidence without adding evidence; the burden of demonstrating independence is yours, and even professionals fail this test.

-   **Separate the two registers of absence.** A specific expected-but-missing item can be positive evidence *if* you have grounds to expect its presence; the systematic non-survival of whole classes of source is a hazard, not evidence, and "we have no sources" is not "it did not happen." Beware, above all, silence manufactured by an editor.

-   **Historical proof is inference to the best explanation: comparative, defeasible, and evaluated against named rivals** on explanatory breadth, economy of assumption, coherence, and resistance to objection — with the congenial explanation held under extra suspicion, and conclusions phrased with the provisionality the method warrants.

## 1.8 Further reading

The two books behind this whole handbook — Bloch's *The Historian's Craft* and Tosh's *The Pursuit of History* — remain the indispensable foundation, and the chapters that follow assume them rather than replace them. Beyond those:

*Carr, E.H. (1961) —* What Is History? The classic short statement of the point that opens this chapter: that the historian and the facts are not independent, and that the questions asked do much to determine the evidence found. Dated in places and much argued-against, but still the cleanest provocation on the priority of the question.

*Evans, Richard J. (1997) —* In Defence of History*.* The most useful modern account of what historical method can and cannot establish, written as a robust defence against the stronger forms of postmodern scepticism. Directly relevant to 1.5: it takes seriously the idea that history is neither pure objectivity nor mere construction, and defends a working middle ground without complacency.

*Tucker, Aviezer (2004) —* Our Knowledge of the Past: A Philosophy of Historiography*.* The serious philosophical treatment of historical inference and evidence, including an explicitly probabilistic, broadly Bayesian reconstruction of how historians reason from evidence to past causes. Demanding, and the one on this list most likely to repay a reader comfortable with quantitative reasoning, since it formalises the intuitions behind 1.3 and 1.5 rather than gesturing at them.

Ginzburg, Carlo (1979/1989) — "Clues: Roots of an Evidential Paradigm" (in *Myths, Emblems, Clues*). An essay rather than a book, and a brilliant one: it locates the historian's inferential method in the same family as the diagnostician's and the detective's — reasoning from small, involuntary, unwitting traces to the realities that produced them. The best single thing to read on why the unwitting testimony of 1.2 is so powerful.
