# Chapter 40 — Reckoning: The Abuse Crisis, Secularisation, and Christianity's Global Picture Today

## The Abuse Crisis: Institutional Failure and Its Roots

The clerical sexual abuse crisis that became publicly visible in the United States in 2002 — when the Boston Globe's sustained investigative reporting on the Archdiocese of Boston exposed the systematic abuse of children by clergy and the systematic cover-up of that abuse by successive archbishops — constitutes the most serious institutional scandal in the history of the modern Catholic church, and one whose significance extends beyond the Catholic context to raise fundamental questions about the relationship between religious institutions and civil accountability that apply across Christian denominations. It was not the first revelation of clerical abuse — cases had been reported in individual dioceses across the preceding decades, the Servants of the Paraclete treatment centre in New Mexico had been receiving priest-abusers for reassignment since the 1940s, and the Irish church's abuse crisis had begun generating public attention in the early 1990s through television documentaries and civil litigation — but the Boston revelations, and the subsequent discovery that the pattern of abuse followed by episcopal cover-up and reassignment was not local but global and systemic, created a public reckoning whose force no institutional response has yet adequately met.

The scale of the abuse documented across subsequent investigations in the United States, Ireland, Australia, Germany, France, Chile, and many other countries is genuinely enormous: the 2004 John Jay Report in the United States found that approximately four percent of Catholic priests who had served between 1950 and 2002 had faced allegations of sexual abuse of minors, amounting to over four thousand priests and perhaps eleven thousand victims in the United States alone; the Australian Royal Commission into Institutional Responses to Child Sexual Abuse (2017) found that seven percent of Catholic priests in Australia had been accused of abuse between 1950 and 2010; the French CIASE report (2021) estimated the total number of victims of clerical abuse in France since 1950 at approximately three hundred and thirty thousand. These are not marginal numbers. They describe an institutional pattern of abuse and cover-up sustained across decades and across national boundaries, producing harm to an enormous number of real people whose suffering deserves to be stated plainly before any institutional context or explanation is introduced.

The cover-up mechanism's resemblance to something medieval has a precise institutional explanation that this overview is positioned to provide, since its roots run through the Gregorian Reform examined in Chapter 20 and the canonist tradition examined across Parts V and VI. The canonical concept of the *privilegium fori* — the clerical immunity from secular courts that was one of the central achievements of the Gregorian Reform, and one of the flashpoints of the Becket controversy in Chapter 30 — established in the mediaeval church the principle that clergy were subject to ecclesiastical rather than civil jurisdiction for offences committed in their clerical capacity. This principle, never fully extinguished even as civil states progressively asserted secular jurisdiction over clergy in criminal matters from the Reformation onward, survived in the Catholic church's institutional culture as a deeply embedded assumption: that matters involving clergy were to be handled through internal canonical processes rather than referred to civil authorities, that scandal was to be avoided through confidential resolution rather than public accountability, and that the reputation of the institution took precedence over the welfare of those harmed by its members. The specific canonical instrument of secrecy most directly implicated in the cover-up — the *Crimen Sollicitationis* instruction of 1962, which required cases of sexual abuse in the confessional and subsequently extended to abuse of minors to be handled under pontifical secrecy within the canonical system — operationalised these assumptions into specific procedural requirements that made reporting to civil authorities a canonical offence.

The institutional response across the decades of crisis has been characterised by the same pattern: initial denial, then minimisation, then the reassignment of accused priests to different parishes or different countries where their history was unknown, then the payment of civil settlements under confidentiality agreements that suppressed public knowledge of the pattern, and, when the pattern became publicly undeniable, apology without adequate accountability for the bishops and cardinals who had managed the cover-up. The specific mechanism of reassignment rather than prosecution reflects precisely the *privilegium fori* logic: treating abuse as an internal disciplinary matter subject to pastoral management rather than a crime subject to civil justice. The Vatican's own handling, including the career of figures like Theodore McCarrick — cardinal archbishop of Washington, elevated by John Paul II and welcomed at the Vatican under Benedict XVI, credibly accused of abuse of seminarians and minors over decades, and eventually reduced to the lay state in 2019 after a canonical trial — illustrated how the pattern of cover-up operated at the highest institutional levels.

Pope Francis's record on the abuse crisis is mixed in ways that honest assessment requires acknowledging. His initial mishandling of the Chilean crisis — his defence of Bishop Juan Barros against survivors' testimony in 2018, subsequently retracted after an investigation confirmed the survivors' accounts — illustrated that the protective instincts toward accused clergy had not been eliminated by the pontificate of the reform pope. His subsequent measures — the mandatory reporting requirement for abuse allegations to Vatican authorities issued in *Vos Estis Lux Mundi* (2019), the abolition of the pontifical secrecy requirement for abuse cases, and the increasing laicisation of convicted priest-abusers — represent genuine progress that the preceding pontificates had not achieved. Whether these measures are sufficient to the scale of the institutional failure they are responding to, and whether the canonical processes they rely on can provide the accountability that survivors and civil authorities require, remains actively contested.

## The Institutional Logic and the Theological Cost

The abuse crisis has a specific theological dimension that distinguishes it from a mere institutional governance failure, important though that dimension is. The theology of the priesthood that underpins the institutional culture within which the cover-up operated — the ontological transformation of the ordained priest through the sacrament of holy orders, which imparts an indelible character and configures the priest uniquely to Christ as head of the church — generated in practice a clericalism in which priests occupied a category of sacred persons whose behaviour was evaluated by different standards from those applied to lay people, and whose failures were accordingly managed within a framework of pastoral care for the priest rather than justice for the victim. The connection between high sacramental theology of ordination and the institutional protection of abusing clergy is not accidental: a framework in which the priest is understood as ontologically distinct from and elevated above the lay person creates precisely the conditions in which the priest's welfare is weighted more heavily than the lay victim's, and in which reporting to secular authorities feels like a betrayal of a sacred person to a profane jurisdiction rather than a straightforward act of civic accountability.

This does not invalidate the theology of holy orders, but it does mean that the theology cannot be held in the same form in which it generated the institutional culture that made the cover-up possible. The reckoning the abuse crisis demands is therefore not only institutional — more accountability, better reporting mechanisms, mandatory reporting laws, lay oversight — but theological: a reconsideration of what the theology of ordained ministry has been used to justify that its own best self-understanding cannot support.

## Secularisation: The European Story and Its Limits

The dramatic decline of institutional Christianity across Western Europe and, more recently and unevenly, across North America — declining church attendance, declining religious affiliation, declining influence of religious institutions on public life and personal ethics, and the emergence of "nones" (those who identify with no religious tradition) as the fastest-growing religious category in census data across much of the Western world — constitutes one of the most significant social transformations of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, and generates what the sociologist Charles Taylor in his *A Secular Age* (2007) identified as the defining question of modernity: how did it come to pass that belief in God, which was in 1500 effectively unquestionable for virtually everyone in the Latin West, became by 2000 one option among many for reflective people in Western societies, and for many educated Western Europeans simply implausible?

Taylor's answer — that secularisation is not the simple subtraction of religion from a previously religious baseline as science and education advance, but rather the creation of a new imaginative and social framework in which religious belief is no longer the default condition requiring justification but one possibility requiring endorsement among others — is considerably more philosophically sophisticated than the simple secularisation thesis it replaces, and its implications for how the churches should understand their own situation are significantly different. On the subtraction model, the churches need only wait for the irrational to be corrected; on Taylor's model, the conditions of belief have changed in ways that require genuine intellectual and cultural engagement with the specific modern understanding of self, society, and cosmos within which Christian faith must be proposed rather than assumed.

The European secularisation story, however, needs to be held alongside the global picture examined in Chapter 38. European Christianity is declining; global Christianity is not. The world has more Christians in absolute numbers than at any previous point in its history, and the proportion of the world's population identifying as Christian has remained roughly stable at around thirty percent across the past century despite massive population growth in non-Christian regions. What has changed is the geography: the Christian majority is now in the Global South, its characteristic expression is Pentecostal or charismatic, and its concerns — healing, prosperity, spiritual warfare, community solidarity, the defence of traditional social ethics — are not those of the declining European liberal Protestantism or the embattled European Catholicism that dominate Western accounts of Christianity's contemporary situation. The secularisation thesis, in its European form, should not be universalised into a global prediction without the qualification that its conditions — the specific post-Enlightenment, post-industrial, post-colonial conditions of Western Europe — are not the conditions of Christianity's actual demographic majority.

## American Evangelical Christianity and the Political Turn

The entanglement of significant portions of American evangelical Christianity with right-wing politics — a process that began with the Moral Majority of the late 1970s and accelerated dramatically with the Trump-era fusion of evangelical identity with Christian nationalist politics — represents one of the most significant and most troubling developments in contemporary Western Christianity, and one that this overview is positioned to contextualise through the structural analysis it has developed.

The historical roots run deeper than the current crisis. American Protestant Christianity has always maintained a strand of civil religion — the identification of American national destiny with providential divine purpose, stretching back to the Puritan settlements' "city on a hill" theology and running through the nineteenth century's Manifest Destiny ideology to the twentieth century's Cold War framing of the United States as the defender of Christian civilisation against godless communism — that has made the fusion of evangelical identity with American nationalist politics a recurrent rather than a novel feature of the landscape. The specifically modern configuration dates to the political mobilisation of white evangelical Christianity in opposition to the cultural changes of the 1960s and 1970s — the civil rights movement, feminism, the sexual revolution, *Roe v. Wade* — through the Moral Majority founded by Jerry Falwell in 1979, which allied conservative Protestantism with the Republican Party in a coalition that reshaped both.

What has changed in the most recent phase is the character of the Christianity involved. The evangelical-Republican alignment of the 1980s and 1990s was theologically motivated in recognisable ways: opposition to abortion on pro-life grounds, support for school prayer, resistance to gay rights on biblical grounds — positions with which one could disagree while recognising their theological derivation. The Trump-era Christian nationalism represents something theologically different and in many respects more alarming: a Christianity in which the specifically theological content has substantially receded in favour of a civilisational-cultural identity claim, in which "Christian" functions increasingly as an ethnic-cultural marker — white, American, anti-cosmopolitan, nostalgic for a social order that is understood as Christian regardless of the theological content of the people inhabiting it — rather than as a designation of theological commitment or ecclesial membership. The willingness of significant portions of evangelical leadership to endorse political behaviour — the embrace of authoritarian rhetoric, the promotion of election fraud claims, the justification of political violence on 6 January 2021 by figures who invoked Jesus — that bears no recognisable relationship to the ethical teaching of the figure in whose name they claim to speak represents a corruption of Christian political witness that the tradition's own internal resources are capable of identifying and condemning, even if the institutional mechanisms for doing so within American evangelical culture are, at present, largely inoperative.

The historical precedents this overview has examined are instructive: the German Christians' identification of the church's mission with the National Socialist movement (Chapter 37), the Southern Baptist Convention's theological defence of slavery (Chapter 35), and the Carolingian church's fusion of Christian mission with Frankish imperial expansion (Chapter 17) are all instances of the same structural pattern — the identification of the gospel with a specific political-cultural programme in ways that subordinate the former to the latter while retaining its legitimising vocabulary. That the specific content of the current American version differs from these precedents does not change the structural logic, and the tradition's own resources for prophetic resistance — Barmen's insistence that Jesus Christ alone is the Word of God the church must hear and obey, Las Casas's challenge to colonial Christianity, Bonhoeffer's critique of cheap grace — are precisely the resources that the current situation calls for.

## Women in the Catholic Church: Ordination, Equality, and the Legal Tension

The exclusion of women from ordained ministry in the Catholic and Orthodox churches — and from significant leadership roles in several conservative Protestant denominations — raises questions that sit at the intersection of theological conviction, institutional history, and contemporary equality law whose complexity requires disaggregation rather than simple characterisation in either direction.

The Catholic church's position, most authoritatively stated in John Paul II's apostolic letter *Ordinatio Sacerdotalis* (1994), is that the church "has no authority whatsoever to confer priestly ordination on women" — a formulation that claims not merely that the church has decided against women's ordination but that the question is settled by divine law rather than institutional discretion, making it, on the Catholic reading, a matter beyond the church's own competence to change rather than a disciplinary rule it happens to maintain. The theological argument rests on the all-male composition of the twelve apostles, on the tradition's unbroken practice across two millennia, and on a sacramental theology in which the priest acts *in persona Christi* (in the person of Christ) in a way that requires a natural resemblance to the historical male Christ — arguments whose premises and whose reasoning have been extensively challenged by feminist and Catholic theologians, including within the church, without however generating any movement in the official position, which the 1994 letter declared "to be definitively held by all the Church's faithful" in language widely interpreted as approaching the threshold of infallibility without formally crossing it.

The legal tension in Western European jurisdictions arises from the conflict between this position and domestic equality legislation that generally prohibits sex discrimination in employment and in the provision of services. The resolution of this tension across most European legal systems has been through religious exemptions: the UK's Equality Act 2010, for example, permits religious organisations to restrict access to roles connected to the religion's own doctrine and practice, allowing the Catholic and Orthodox churches to maintain male-only ordination without legal liability. The European Court of Human Rights has consistently upheld religious organisations' right to determine their own ministerial and membership criteria as an aspect of the freedom of religion guaranteed by Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights, treating this as a conflict between two human rights — freedom of religion and freedom from sex discrimination — in which the former takes precedence for the internal governance of religious organisations.

The theological argument the Catholic church deploys — that this is divine law beyond its own competence to alter — places it in a distinct position from institutions that maintain male-only roles as a matter of institutional preference. Whether one accepts this theological claim determines whether one reads the Catholic position as principled fidelity to divine mandate or as institutional self-serving dressed in theological vocabulary, and this overview cannot adjudicate that question; what it can do is note that the question is contested at both the theological and the legal level, that the legal exemptions currently maintained in most Western jurisdictions are subject to ongoing political and judicial pressure as equality norms evolve, and that the internal Catholic debate about women's ordination — including the question of women deacons, on which Francis has convened multiple study commissions without reaching a conclusion — is not closed despite *Ordinatio Sacerdotalis*'s declared finality.

## Christianity's Global Picture: A Stocktaking

Christianity enters the third decade of the twenty-first century as a genuinely global religion of extraordinary diversity — in its theological commitments, its institutional forms, its cultural expressions, its political alignments, and its relationship to the surrounding culture — that resists any single summary characterisation. It is simultaneously the faith of Global South Pentecostals worshipping in a Lagos megachurch and of a Norwegian Lutheran congregation of thirty elderly people; of a Bolivian Catholic community celebrating Mass in a Quechua-inflected syncretic liturgy and of an Oxford Anglican college chapel performing Evensong to Cranmer's words; of an Assyrian Christian community in Chicago reading the gospel in the Aramaic of the first century and of a Korean Presbyterian missionary working in Mongolia. The tradition's internal diversity is not a pathology to be overcome but a consequence of the translation principle that Andrew Walls identified as Christianity's defining structural feature — the gospel's capacity to become genuinely at home in every human culture without becoming permanently identified with any of them.

The challenges Christianity faces in the early twenty-first century are equally various and cannot be reduced to any single narrative. The secularisation of Western Europe and the increasingly contested relationship between religious institutions and civil authority in the post-abuse-crisis landscape are real and serious challenges for the churches of the global North. The persecution of Christian minorities across substantial parts of the Middle East, North Africa, South Asia, and parts of sub-Saharan Africa — where Christians are among the most persecuted religious minorities in the world by most metrics — represents a different and more immediately physical challenge for communities whose survival is not a metaphor. The growth of Christianity across sub-Saharan Africa, where the number of Christians has increased from approximately ten million in 1900 to over seven hundred million in 2020, represents an extraordinary vitality that the declining Western church cannot simply claim credit for and cannot afford to patronise. The internal theological and institutional divisions that separate Catholic from Orthodox from Protestant from Pentecostal, that divide Global South social conservatism from Western progressive theology, and that fracture even individual denominations along fault lines of theology, politics, and culture, represent both the tradition's richness and its institutional fragility.

What this overview has attempted to trace, across forty chapters and four files, is the story of how an obscure Jewish messianic movement in a minor province of the Roman empire became the world's largest religion — a story in which genuine sanctity and genuine atrocity, intellectual brilliance and institutional failure, prophetic courage and spectacular moral collapse have been so thoroughly interwoven that no honest account can celebrate or condemn the tradition wholesale. Christianity has shaped the world's history, for good and for ill, more profoundly than any other single institution, and the world it has shaped — with all its inherited assumptions about human dignity, social obligation, historical meaning, and ultimate accountability — continues to bear its marks whether or not the people living in it any longer acknowledge the source. Understanding that history, with the precision and the moral seriousness it demands, is what this overview has aimed to provide.
