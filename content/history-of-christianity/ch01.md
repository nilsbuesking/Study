# Part I — Origins (c. 30–150)

# Chapter 1 — Second Temple Judaism and the Historical Jesus: What Can Be Known

## The Problem of Sources

Any account of Christian origins has to begin by being honest about what kind of evidence survives and what it can support. No contemporary, neutral observer recorded the life of Jesus von Nazareth; the Roman administrative apparatus that has left such rich documentary traces for other purposes — tax registers, military diplomas, provincial correspondence — had no particular reason to notice an itinerant Galilean teacher executed under a minor procurator, and did not. What survives instead is a body of text produced by the movement that formed around him, written down decades after the events they describe, in a different language from the one he spoke, by authors writing theology rather than biography in the modern sense. This is not a scandal or a uniquely Christian evidentiary problem — it is the ordinary condition of ancient history below the level of emperors and generals — but it does mean that the question "what can be known" has to be answered with explicit method rather than confidence borrowed from either side of the modern argument about Jesus.

Two historiographical traditions have shaped how this evidence gets read, and both carry distortions worth naming before going further. Confessional scholarship, particularly in its older nineteenth- and early twentieth-century forms, tended to treat the Gospels as substantially reliable testimony requiring only harmonisation, with the burden of proof falling on anyone who doubted a given pericope. A countervailing tradition, running from the nineteenth-century "quest of the historical Jesus" through twentieth-century form criticism to some contemporary popularising scholarship, has tended toward a hermeneutic of suspicion in which the burden falls the other way: a saying or event is treated as inauthentic unless it can be positively defended, often by criteria — dissimilarity to both Judaism and the early church, multiple independent attestation, embarrassment — that are individually defensible but collectively capable of stripping away almost everything if applied with sufficient rigour. The position taken here is that both extremes overreach: the Gospels are not neutral reportage, but they are also not free invention, and they need to be read as the products of communities with theological commitments who were nonetheless drawing on and shaping real historical memory, in proportions that have to be argued case by case rather than assumed in either direction.

The non-Christian evidence is sparse but not negligible, and its limitations are instructive. Flavius Josephus's *Jewish Antiquities*, written around 93–94 CE, contains two passages of relevance: a brief, plausibly authentic notice of the execution of "James, the brother of Jesus who is called Christ" (Book 20), and the so-called *Testimonium Flavianum* (Book 18), which in its received form contains unmistakably Christian confessional language — "he was the Messiah," "he appeared to them on the third day alive again" — that a non-Christian Jewish historian writing for a Roman audience is exceedingly unlikely to have written. The scholarly consensus, though not unanimous, holds that Josephus wrote something about Jesus at this point in his narrative, and that a Christian scribe later interpolated or rewrote it to make it more confessional; attempts to reconstruct the original substrate are necessarily speculative. Tacitus's *Annals* (c. 116 CE) mentions, in the context of Nero's persecution after the fire of Rome, that "Christus" had been executed under Pontius Pilate during the reign of Tiberius — useful as independent attestation that a Roman administrator of the relevant period and office associated the movement's founder with a real execution, but it tells us nothing beyond what Tacitus's Christian contemporaries already believed and may simply reflect. Pliny the Younger's correspondence with Trajan around 112 CE, and a handful of rabbinic references of uncertain date and reliability, round out a non-Christian dossier that confirms the bare fact of Jesus's existence and execution beyond reasonable historical doubt — the fringe position that he is a purely mythical construct has essentially no purchase among working historians of the period, Christian or not — without supplying meaningful biographical content.

This leaves the Christian sources themselves as the primary evidentiary base, and they need to be read with attention to genre, dating, and dependence. The four canonical Gospels are not eyewitness accounts in any straightforward sense; the scholarly consensus dates Mark to around 65–75 CE, Matthew and Luke to roughly 80–95 CE, and John to perhaps 90–110 CE — a generation or more after the crucifixion, written by authors who were not themselves participants and who drew on earlier oral and possibly written tradition of uneven reliability. The majority position since the nineteenth century holds that Mark was written first and that Matthew and Luke each used Mark independently as a source, supplementing it with a hypothetical sayings collection conventionally labelled Q (from German *Quelle*) and with material unique to each — a reconstruction (the "Two-Source Hypothesis") that remains the working consensus, though not without dissent, particularly from scholars who favour Matthean priority. Paul's authentic letters, several of which (Romans, 1 Corinthians, Galatians among them) are earlier than any Gospel, are paradoxically both closer in time to the events and almost entirely uninterested in reporting them; Paul cites almost no biographical detail about Jesus's life and ministry, focusing instead on the death, resurrection, and theological meaning of Christ, which is itself an important datum about what mattered most urgently to the earliest movement.

## Methods and Their Limits

Form criticism, developed principally by German scholars in the early twentieth century, approached the Gospel material by trying to identify the discrete oral units — pronouncement stories, miracle accounts, parables — that had circulated before being woven into continuous narrative, and to infer the needs of the communities that preserved them. Redaction criticism, its successor, shifted attention to the evangelists themselves as theological authors actively shaping inherited material to serve particular emphases — Matthew's concern with Jesus as the fulfilment of Jewish scripture and law, Luke's interest in the marginalised and in salvation extending to Gentiles, and so on. Both approaches remain methodologically valuable, but both also illustrate the difficulty of the underlying problem: once it is established that the evangelists shaped their material theologically, the question of how much historical bedrock survives beneath that shaping becomes correspondingly harder to answer with confidence, and different scholars working from similar premises arrive at substantially different reconstructions.

The criteria of authenticity developed across the twentieth century — dissimilarity (a saying is more likely authentic if it diverges from both contemporary Judaism and later church interests, since neither source would have had reason to invent it), multiple attestation (independent appearance across Mark, Q, Paul, and other strands strengthens a claim), coherence (material consistent with an already-established authentic core), and embarrassment (details the early church would have had no motive to invent and some motive to suppress, such as Jesus's baptism by John, which implies subordination, or the crucifixion itself, a mode of execution reserved for slaves and rebels and a considerable embarrassment to claims of messianic kingship) — remain in use but have been subjected to serious internal criticism. The dissimilarity criterion in particular has been faulted for tending to produce a Jesus dissimilar from Judaism, which is historically implausible for a first-century Galilean Jew and reflects more about the criterion's construction than about the evidence; the broader "Third Quest" since the 1980s has self-consciously tried to correct this by emphasising Jesus's thorough embeddedness in Second Temple Jewish thought-worlds rather than his distinctiveness from them. The result is a field where particular claims — the broad outline of an itinerant teaching and healing ministry in Galilee, a following gathered partly from fishing communities around the Sea of Galilee, conflict with Jerusalem religious authorities culminating in arrest and crucifixion under Pilate, probably in the late 20s or early 30s CE — command something close to consensus, while almost everything more specific, including most individual sayings and the precise content of his self-understanding, remains genuinely and often sharply contested.

## Second Temple Judaism: The World Jesus Inhabited

Understanding Jesus and the movement he generated requires understanding Second Temple Judaism on its own terms, not as a static backdrop against which Christianity emerges as the more dynamic actor — a framing inherited from older supersessionist historiography and now widely rejected. The Second Temple period, conventionally dated from the rebuilding of the Jerusalem Temple after the Babylonian exile (completed 516 BCE) to its destruction by Roman forces in 70 CE, was a period of considerable internal diversity rather than a monolithic "Judaism" awaiting Christian fulfilment. By the first century CE, Jewish religious and social life was organised around several overlapping institutions and contested by several distinct movements, and Jesus's ministry has to be read against all of them.

The Jerusalem Temple remained the central institution of Jewish religious life — the sole legitimate site of sacrificial worship, administered by a hereditary priesthood under the high priest, and a major economic and political institution as well as a religious one, drawing pilgrimage traffic and tithes from across the Jewish world and functioning as the point of contact between Jewish religious authority and Roman provincial administration. The synagogue, by contrast, had emerged — its precise origins remain debated, with some scholars locating its development in the Babylonian exile and others in the Hellenistic-period diaspora — as a local institution of prayer, scripture reading, and communal gathering that did not require priestly mediation or proximity to Jerusalem, and which provided the movement's earliest organisational template once it began to spread beyond Judaea.

Several distinct groups contested the interpretation of Jewish law and the appropriate response to Roman rule, and the Gospels' frequent depiction of Jesus in conflict with "the Pharisees" needs considerable historical unpacking. The Pharisees, whom Josephus describes as influential with the general population, held that the Torah's authority extended to an oral tradition of interpretation alongside the written text, and emphasised ritual purity, tithing, and Sabbath observance as applicable beyond the priestly class to ordinary Jewish life — a programme of religious intensification and accessibility rather than the legalistic caricature later Christian polemic frequently made of it, and one that in fact shares considerable common ground with positions attributed to Jesus on points such as resurrection of the dead, which the Pharisees affirmed against the Sadducees' denial of it. The Sadducees, by contrast, were drawn substantially from priestly and aristocratic circles, accepted only the written Torah as authoritative, denied resurrection and angelic intermediaries, and held disproportionate institutional power through their control of the Temple and collaborative relationship with Roman administration — a relationship that made them, rather than the Pharisees, the more natural object of the kind of conflict the Gospels narrate in Jerusalem during the final days of Jesus's life. The Essenes, known principally through Josephus, Philo, and the Dead Sea Scrolls discovered at Qumran from 1947 onward, represented a more separatist tendency, with at least some communities withdrawing from mainstream Temple-centred life in expectation of imminent eschatological vindication and practising rigorous purity codes; whether Jesus or John the Baptist had direct contact with Qumran sectarians remains unproven and probably unprovable, though the Scrolls have been invaluable for reconstructing the range of apocalyptic and messianic expectation circulating in the period independent of Christian sources. Zealot and other revolutionary movements, more clearly attested for the period immediately before the First Jewish Revolt of 66–73 CE than for Jesus's own lifetime, represent the violent end of a broader spectrum of Jewish response to Roman occupation that also needs to be borne in mind as context, even though retrojecting the organised Zealot party of the 60s back into the 20s and 30s, as some twentieth-century scholarship did in reading Jesus as a proto-revolutionary, is generally regarded as anachronistic.

Underlying much of this diversity was a shared, though variously inflected, apocalyptic and messianic expectation — the hope, rooted in earlier prophetic and the more recent Danielic and intertestamental literature, that God would soon intervene decisively in history to vindicate Israel, judge the wicked, and establish a renewed order, often associated with the coming of an anointed figure (*mashiach*, "messiah," literally "anointed one") whose precise expected character — royal, Davidic, military liberator, priestly, or in some texts a more transcendent heavenly figure — varied considerably across different Jewish groups and texts, and which the Gospels themselves show Jesus's contemporaries struggling to map onto him. This is the immediate religious atmosphere — diverse, contested, and apocalyptically charged — within which the historical Jesus has to be situated, against an older Christian historiographical tendency to treat Judaism as legalistic and spiritually exhausted, a tendency now recognised as both historically inaccurate and theologically freighted with a supersessionism that bears some responsibility for the longer history of Christian anti-Judaism treated later in this overview.

## Jesus of Nazareth: A Sketch Within the Evidence

What, then, can be said with reasonable historical confidence? Jesus was a Galilean Jew, almost certainly born in the final years of Herod the Great's reign (Herod died in 4 BCE, creating the well-known but real chronological tension with Luke's later dating of the nativity to a census usually associated with 6 CE), raised in or near Nazareth, a small and historically undistinguished village in Lower Galilee. He appears to have begun his public activity in connection with John the Baptist, an independent prophetic and baptismal figure whose own ministry of repentance and eschatological warning is attested both in the Gospels and, independently, in Josephus — an independent attestation that lends the connection unusual historical weight, and Jesus's baptism by John is widely regarded as one of the more secure data points precisely because it satisfies the embarrassment criterion described above. Following this, Jesus conducted an itinerant teaching and, in the Gospels' consistent presentation, healing and exorcistic ministry concentrated in Galilean towns and villages around the Sea of Galilee, gathering a following that included a core group of named disciples and a wider circle, drawing on imagery of the "kingdom of God" (or "kingdom of heaven" in Matthew's more Jewish-idiom-sensitive phrasing) as the central organising theme of his teaching — a phrase whose meaning, whether a future apocalyptic event, a present transformative reality, or some combination understood differently across different sayings, remains one of the most contested questions in historical Jesus scholarship, with influential twentieth-century positions ranging from Albert Schweitzer's thoroughly future-apocalyptic Jesus to later "realised eschatology" readings emphasising the kingdom as already present in Jesus's own activity.

The teaching itself, as preserved across the Synoptic tradition, characteristically employed parables — short narrative analogies drawn from agrarian and everyday Galilean life — and pronouncements marked by a distinctive ethical intensification of existing Jewish ethical and legal categories rather than their abrogation, alongside table fellowship extended controversially to tax collectors, the ritually impure, and other marginal figures, which several strands of the tradition independently present as a particular point of friction with more purity-conscious contemporaries. Whatever Jesus's own self-understanding — and the Gospels' explicit messianic titles for him are now generally regarded by critical scholarship as reflecting, at least substantially, the post-Easter confession of the early church reading backward into the narrative rather than straightforward reportage of Jesus's own public claims, even where some authentic core of implicit or indirect self-understanding likely underlies them — his activity generated sufficient concern among Jerusalem's Temple authorities, very probably compounded by Roman administrative anxiety about public disorder during a major pilgrimage festival, that he was arrested during a Passover visit to Jerusalem, examined in some fashion by Jewish authorities (the precise legal character of this proceeding, whether a formal Sanhedrin trial or a more informal preliminary hearing, is debated, and the Gospel accounts diverge in ways that resist easy harmonisation), and handed to the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate (governing Judaea 26–36 CE), who ordered his crucifixion — a Roman, not Jewish, mode of execution, reserved for slaves, rebels, and those deemed to threaten public order, and itself among the more securely attested facts of the entire narrative, satisfying both the embarrassment and multiple-attestation criteria about as strongly as any single datum in the tradition.

The resurrection — the central claim of the movement that followed, and the subject on which Paul's earliest extant letters already presuppose a settled and apparently uncontroversial conviction within the community he is writing to, barely two decades after the crucifixion — sits outside what historical method as conventionally practised can adjudicate, since it asserts an event of a different order from those historians are equipped to verify or falsify; what can be said historically is that something happened, or was experienced, or was concluded, that generated in a body of Jesus's followers — beginning, on the earliest evidence, with figures including Peter and, on Paul's own later and striking testimony, James the brother of Jesus and eventually Paul himself, a former persecutor of the movement — a conviction sufficiently strong and sufficiently early that it reshaped a defeated and scattered following around the proclamation that their executed teacher had been vindicated by God, raised from the dead, and exalted as Lord and Messiah. That conviction, whatever its ultimate cause, is the historical hinge on which everything covered in the following chapters turns, and it is to the movement that formed around it — beginning with the apostle who did more than any other figure to carry it beyond the boundaries of Second Temple Judaism — that this overview now turns.
