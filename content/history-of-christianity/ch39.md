# Chapter 39 — Vatican II and Liberation Theology: The Church Reforms and Radicalises

## The Rupture with the Tridentine Settlement

The Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), convened by Pope John XXIII and continued under Paul VI., constitutes the most significant event in the internal history of the Catholic church since the Council of Trent four centuries earlier, and its significance is best understood precisely in terms of its relationship to the Tridentine settlement examined in Chapter 31. Where Trent had responded to the Protestant challenge by hardening doctrine, standardising liturgy, centralising authority, and drawing sharp boundaries between Catholic and non-Catholic, Vatican II — convened in a completely different historical context, one of post-war European reconstruction, decolonisation, the Cold War, and a global church whose majority was no longer European — undertook a comprehensive re-examination of the church's self-understanding, its relationship to the modern world, its internal structures, its liturgy, and its posture toward other Christians and other religions, that produced results whose implications the church has been working through, and in some cases resisting, ever since.

John XXIII's word for what he hoped the council would achieve was *aggiornamento* — an Italian term meaning "bringing up to date" or "opening the windows" — a phrase whose apparent simplicity concealed a genuine theological programme: the distinction between the deposit of faith, which was permanent and unchangeable, and the historically conditioned forms in which that faith had been expressed, which were subject to renewal and reformulation in ways appropriate to changed circumstances. This distinction — between substance and formulation, between the gospel and its cultural packaging — was not itself new; it had been the animating principle of the Catholic Modernist movement that Pius X had condemned in 1907, and several of the theologians whose work the council drew on most heavily, including Yves Congar, Karl Rahner, and Henri de Lubac, had been subjected to varying degrees of Vatican suspicion or censure before John XXIII's election transformed the institutional atmosphere. The council in effect rehabilitated what its immediate predecessors had repressed, and this institutional reversal was itself among its most significant acts.

## The Council's Achievements

The council's four sessions between 1962 and 1965 produced sixteen documents whose importance varied considerably but whose combined effect was a substantial reorientation of Catholic self-understanding across several interconnected domains.

The most immediately experienced change was liturgical. *Sacrosanctum Concilium* (1963), the Constitution on the Sacred Liturgy, authorised the use of vernacular languages in the Mass in place of the uniform Latin that Trent had mandated and that had defined Catholic worship for four centuries, introduced active participation of the laity in the liturgical action as a principle rather than an exceptional accommodation, and initiated the comprehensive liturgical reform that would produce the Novus Ordo Mass of 1969 — a reform whose theological motivation was the recovery of the early church's more participatory liturgical practice, but whose pastoral implementation generated a division within Catholicism that has not entirely healed. The traditionalist minority associated above all with Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre, who continued to celebrate the pre-conciliar Tridentine Mass and eventually consecrated bishops without papal mandate in 1988 (resulting in his excommunication), represents the most visible extreme of a broader conservative discomfort with the pace and character of liturgical change that has remained a live institutional tension.

The ecclesiological innovations of *Lumen Gentium* (1964), the Dogmatic Constitution on the Church, were theologically more fundamental than the liturgical reforms, even if less immediately visible to ordinary Catholics. Against the pyramidal ecclesiology that had dominated post-Tridentine Catholicism — in which authority descended from the pope through the bishops to the clergy to the laity — *Lumen Gentium* introduced the concept of the *People of God* as the primary category for understanding the church, placing this chapter before the chapter on the hierarchy and thereby (at least in the logic of the document's structure) establishing the common dignity of all the baptised as the foundation on which hierarchical differentiation was built rather than its precondition. The recovery of the concept of *collegiality* — the bishops' shared responsibility for the universal church, exercised in communion with and never independently of the pope — qualified the maximalist reading of Vatican I's papal primacy doctrine without formally contradicting it, in a balancing act whose tensions subsequent decades would make very visible. The document's treatment of the relationship between the Catholic church and other Christians, acknowledging that "elements of sanctification and truth" existed outside the Catholic church's visible boundaries, marked a decisive departure from the traditional *extra ecclesiam nulla salus* ("outside the church no salvation") formula in its strict exclusive application.

*Nostra Aetate* (1965), the Declaration on the Relation of the Church to Non-Christian Religions, has already been referenced in Chapter 24 for its formal repudiation of the deicide charge against the Jewish people and its condemnation of antisemitism — the most direct institutional reckoning with the theological tradition traced in that chapter. Its significance extended beyond the Jewish question to the church's posture toward Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and other world religions, which it described as containing "a ray of that Truth which enlightens all men" rather than dismissing as purely erroneous — a formulation that represented a significant doctrinal shift, even if its precise theological implications (how much saving grace, if any, operated through non-Christian religious traditions) were left deliberately unresolved and have remained contested in subsequent Catholic theology.

*Gaudium et Spes* (1965), the Pastoral Constitution on the Church in the Modern World, was the document that most directly addressed the question Chapter 36 identified as the central challenge of the preceding century: the church's relationship to modernity. Its famous opening sentence — "The joys and the hopes, the griefs and the anxieties of the men of this age, especially those who are poor or in any way afflicted, these are the joys and hopes, the griefs and anxieties of the followers of Christ" — announced a posture of solidarity with humanity rather than the defensive opposition that had characterised the Syllabus of Errors a century earlier, and committed the church to engagement with the contemporary world's questions rather than retreat from them. The document's engagement with economic and social justice, with war and peace, with culture and science, constituted an agenda for the church's social presence that liberation theology and subsequent Catholic social teaching would both draw on and contest in the following decades.

## The Post-Conciliar Tension: Reception and Resistance

The council's documents were the beginning rather than the end of the theological and institutional work they initiated, and the subsequent decades demonstrated the difficulty of implementing a reform whose different participants had understood very differently. A conservative reading of the council — associated with figures including then-Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, who as Prefect of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith from 1981 and subsequently as Pope Benedict XVI was the most influential single shaper of the post-conciliar Catholic intellectual landscape — emphasised the council's continuity with the preceding tradition, insisted that it had defined no new dogma and changed no essential doctrine, and read its innovations as developments within an unbroken trajectory rather than ruptures with the past. A progressive reading, associated with the theologians of the council's reform wing and with several of the national bishops' conferences that implemented its decisions, understood the council as having opened questions that the tradition had previously closed, having authorised a genuine development of doctrine rather than merely a change of pastoral style, and having committed the church to an ongoing process of self-renewal rather than a one-time adjustment.

Paul VI's 1968 encyclical *Humanae Vitae*, which reaffirmed the traditional prohibition of artificial contraception against the expectation of many Catholics — and against the recommendation of the majority of the papal commission he had himself convened to examine the question — that the council's spirit of renewal might lead to a change on this specific point, became the most significant single test of the post-conciliar settlement and the event that crystallised the emerging divide between the council's more and less progressive interpreters into a lasting institutional fracture. The widespread public dissent from *Humanae Vitae* among Catholic laity and clergy across much of the Western world — dissent that persists and has in many countries become simply the assumed practice of the majority of married Catholics regardless of the church's formal teaching — marked the moment at which the authority gap between the magisterium's formal position and the actual life of the Catholic people became publicly and irretrievably visible, and from which the crisis of institutional authority in the Western Catholic church has never fully recovered.

## Liberation Theology: The Preferential Option in Practice

The theological movement examined in Chapter 38's discussion of Latin American Christianity deserves treatment here as a chapter in the post-conciliar story rather than only as a geographical one, since liberation theology was simultaneously a product of Vatican II's spirit and an extension of that spirit into directions the council's documents had at most half-opened. The Medellín Conference of the Latin American Bishops (1968) — the first major regional episcopal gathering after Vatican II — explicitly applied *Gaudium et Spes*'s agenda to the specific conditions of Latin American poverty and political repression, producing documents that went considerably further than the council itself in analysing the structural causes of poverty and committing the church to the transformation of unjust social structures as a dimension of the church's evangelising mission.

Gustavo Gutiérrez's *Teología de la Liberación* (1971) provided the movement with its most systematic theological statement, and its central methodological claim — that genuine theology required beginning from the *underside of history*, from the perspective of the poor and oppressed whose suffering revealed dimensions of the gospel invisible from positions of social privilege — was not merely a political preference but a hermeneutical claim: that the interpreter's social location affected what they could see in the text, and that the poor's reading of the Exodus and the prophets and the Beatitudes was not a distortion but a recovery of meanings that the tradition's cultural captivity to the powerful had suppressed. This claim, developed in various forms by Gutiérrez, Leonardo Boff, Jon Sobrino, and others, represented a genuine and serious contribution to the theory of biblical interpretation as well as to social ethics, and its influence extended well beyond the Latin American context into feminist theology, Black theology, postcolonial theology, and the broader project of reading scripture from the margins rather than the centre.

The Vatican's response under John Paul II and Cardinal Ratzinger, examined in Chapter 38, was a genuine theological engagement rather than a purely political suppression, and the two instructions of 1984 and 1986 made real distinctions: affirming the option for the poor and the church's commitment to justice while criticising the uncritical appropriation of Marxist social analysis and what the instructions saw as the reduction of salvation to historical-political liberation. The martyrdom of Óscar Romero in 1980 — beatified in 2015 and canonised in 2018 by Pope Francis — constituted the most morally clear case in the liberation theology story: a bishop who had moved from institutional conservatism to prophetic solidarity with the poor through the direct encounter with the murder of his people, and whose death at the altar the church has now formally acknowledged as a martyrdom. The canonisation of Romero under Francis, in the same ceremony as Pope Paul VI, was itself a theological statement about the council's legacy and about what kind of Christianity the current papacy wished to affirm.

## Nostra Aetate, Antisemitism, and the Unfinished Reckoning

*Nostra Aetate*'s formal repudiation of the deicide charge and condemnation of antisemitism represented the most significant official Catholic reckoning with the history traced in Chapter 24, and its significance can hardly be overstated given the weight of the tradition it was modifying. The document's passage through the council was contested — conservative bishops argued that it implied an unacceptable revision of the tradition's teaching on Jewish culpability, and the lobbying of Arab states concerned about the geopolitical implications of Vatican recognition of Jewish innocence contributed to the document's repeated revisions and near-derailment. Its eventual passage was therefore a genuine achievement against institutional resistance rather than the natural expression of a consensus that had already formed.

The theological work *Nostra Aetate* initiated but did not complete has continued in subsequent decades through the bilateral dialogues between the Catholic church and Jewish communities, the 1985 *Notes on the Correct Way to Present the Jews and Judaism in Preaching and Catechesis*, the 1998 document *We Remember: A Reflection on the Shoah*, and the ongoing theological rethinking of supersessionism — the conviction, as Chapter 24 traced, that the Christian church has replaced the Jewish people as God's covenant community — whose abandonment some theologians regard as a necessary implication of *Nostra Aetate*'s logic and others resist as incompatible with the Christian claim about Christ's definitive significance. The question is not closed, and the history this overview has traced makes it impossible to regard it as merely academic.

## The Francis Pontificate and the Council's Contested Legacy

The election of Jorge Mario Bergoglio as Pope Francis in 2013 introduced into the papacy a figure formed by the Latin American church of the liberation theology era, committed to the Argentinian version of the option for the poor, and determined to shift the church's institutional focus and rhetorical register in ways that have simultaneously revived the council's reforming spirit and generated the most publicly visible internal Catholic conflict since the post-*Humanae Vitae* period. Francis's programmatic exhortation *Evangelii Gaudium* (2013) — explicitly citing Romero, emphasising the church of the poor over the church of the institution, and criticising what Francis called an "economy of exclusion" with a directness unusual in papal documents — constituted a deliberate alignment with the council's *Gaudium et Spes* trajectory against the more institutionally conservative tendencies of the preceding pontificates.

The specific questions that Francis's papacy has opened — the possibility of ordaining married men in remote areas without priests, the pastoral approach to divorced and remarried Catholics (*Amoris Laetitia*, 2016, and the sustained controversy it generated), the blessing of same-sex couples (*Fiducia Supplicans*, 2023), and the renewed discussion of women in the diaconate — have revealed in unusually clear form the depth of the division within the Catholic church between those who understand Vatican II as having opened a continuing process of development and those who understand it as having established a settlement to be defended against further change. The four *dubia* (formal theological questions) submitted to Francis by a group of conservative cardinals, the *Correctio Filialis* signed by scholars accusing him of heresy, and the public opposition of figures including the American Cardinal Raymond Burke represent an intra-Catholic conflict whose theological depth and institutional bitterness is, by the standards of the post-Tridentine church, genuinely unusual — a measure both of how much Vatican II changed and of how much its legacy remains contested sixty years after its conclusion.
