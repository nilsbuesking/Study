# Chapter 28 — The Political Reformation: Princes, Peasants, and the Fragmentation of Christendom

## The Reformation as Political Event

The theological revolution examined in Chapter 27 did not occur in an institutional vacuum, and its survival, consolidation, and eventual geographical distribution were determined as much by the political structures and political interests of early sixteenth-century Germany as by the persuasive force of Luther's theological arguments. The Reformation was not merely a theological event that happened to have political consequences; it was, from an early stage, simultaneously a theological, political, social, and economic upheaval in which different participants — princes, urban magistrates, knights, peasants, humanists, and ordinary clergy and laity — found in Luther's challenge to Rome a resource that they interpreted and deployed in ways that Luther himself had neither intended nor, in several cases, welcomed.

Understanding this requires a brief orientation to the political structure of the Holy Roman Empire, within which the Reformation's early development occurred. The empire was not a centralised state but a complex confederation of several hundred territories — electoral and non-electoral principalities, free imperial cities, ecclesiastical territories, and imperial knights — each with its own ruler exercising considerable internal sovereignty, united under an elected emperor whose authority over the constituent territories was substantially limited by the constitutional rights of the imperial estates gathered in the Imperial Diet. The emperor Charles V, elected in 1519 and facing the Lutheran crisis from its earliest stages, was simultaneously Holy Roman Emperor, King of Spain, ruler of the Habsburg Netherlands and the rapidly expanding Spanish empire in the Americas, and the principal defender of the Ottoman frontier — a combination of responsibilities that made sustained, focused attention to the German religious crisis structurally difficult and that gave the German princes leverage in their dealings with the imperial court that they systematically exploited.

This political structure meant that the decisive question for the Reformation's survival was not whether Luther could persuade Rome — he could not — but whether enough German princes could be persuaded or moved by interest to adopt and protect the new theology in their territories, beyond the reach of effective imperial or papal coercive action. That this happened, and happened with sufficient speed and in sufficient territorial mass to become irreversible within a decade of Worms, reflects a convergence of genuine theological conviction on the part of some princes, material interest on the part of many, and the specific constitutional vulnerabilities of the empire that gave territorial rulers the practical capacity to act on whichever of these motivations prevailed.

## The Knights' Revolt and Its Lessons

The first attempt to convert the Reformation's theological energy into political action came not from the princes but from the imperial knights — a class of small, directly imperial landholders whose economic position had been deteriorating across the later fifteenth century under the pressure of the consolidating territorial princes, and who saw in the Reformation both a genuine spiritual opportunity and a chance to restore their position through the secularisation of ecclesiastical territories. Ulrich von Hutten, the humanist propagandist whose vigorous anti-Roman pamphleteering had made him one of the most effective publicists of the early Reformation, and Franz von Sickingen, the leading military figure among the knights, launched the Knights' Revolt of 1522–1523 against the Archbishop of Trier, framing the attack in evangelical terms as an assault on clerical tyranny.

The revolt was rapidly crushed by the combined forces of the Rhenish princes — who had no interest in seeing the imperial knights' position strengthened regardless of the theological framing — and von Sickingen died in the siege of his own castle. The episode's significance for the Reformation's subsequent development was as a negative lesson about what kind of political base could sustain it: freelancing military action by a socially marginal class without the backing of territorial sovereignty was not that base. The Reformation would survive through princes, not knights, and the clarity of that lesson after 1523 shaped how Luther and his associates subsequently managed the movement's political relationships.

## The Peasants' War: 1524–1525

The most dramatic and most morally consequential political episode of the early Reformation was the Peasants' War of 1524–1525, the largest popular uprising in German history before the nineteenth century, in which an estimated hundred thousand to three hundred thousand peasants took up arms across a vast swathe of central and southern Germany, Alsace, and Austria, demanding the redress of the economic, legal, and social grievances that decades of seigneurial pressure had accumulated.

The peasants' demands, articulated most clearly in the Twelve Articles of the Swabian Peasants (1525), were a mixture of economic complaints — the abolition of serfdom, the reduction of dues and labour obligations, the right of communities to elect their own pastors — and evangelical theological claims: specifically, the assertion that communities had the right to choose and dismiss their own pastors, and that dues and obligations not demonstrably warranted by scripture were not binding on Christian consciences. The appeal to scripture and to evangelical principles was direct, deliberate, and in large measure sincere: the Twelve Articles cited the Lutheran gospel's proclamation of Christian freedom as the theological basis for demands that were simultaneously spiritual and material, and many of the peasants and their leaders were genuine Reformation sympathisers who understood their uprising as an expression of the evangelical liberty Luther had proclaimed.

The connection between Luther's theology and the peasants' cause was not fabricated by the peasants' opponents: Luther had indeed proclaimed Christian freedom, had challenged the authority of tradition and institution over the individual conscience, and had used language — the freedom of a Christian, the priesthood of all believers — that could be and was read as having social and political implications beyond the strictly theological domain in which he had intended them to operate. The peasants were not misreading Luther; they were applying his principles to their own situation in ways that his principles, taken at face value, could support.

Luther's response was one of the most morally troubling episodes of his career, and it cannot be softened without misrepresenting the historical record. He initially attempted a mediating position — his *Admonition to Peace* (April 1525) acknowledged the validity of some peasant grievances while urging both sides to negotiate — but as the revolt spread and violence escalated, he pivoted with brutal abruptness to an unequivocal endorsement of its suppression. His pamphlet *Against the Robbing and Murdering Hordes of Peasants*, written in May 1525 at the height of the uprising and published just as the princes' armies were beginning their murderous campaigns of reconquest, called on the rulers to "smite, slay, and stab" the rebellious peasants, promising that anyone killed suppressing the revolt would die as a martyr, and characterising the peasants as mad dogs that must be killed before they destroy everyone. The pamphlet's timing and tone appalled many of his own supporters; his subsequent attempt to qualify its meaning in an *Open Letter on the Harsh Book Against the Peasants* (June 1525) satisfied almost nobody, since its essential content was that the princes had been right to kill and that the dead peasants had received what they deserved.

The theological rationale Luther offered was a sharp distinction between the spiritual kingdom — the domain of the gospel, Christian freedom, and grace — and the temporal kingdom — the domain of law, order, and coercive authority, which God had entrusted to secular rulers for the restraint of sin and whose legitimate exercise of force was not to be confused with the spiritual liberty the gospel proclaimed. The peasants had conflated the two kingdoms, attempting to derive social and political transformation from evangelical principles that properly applied only to the spiritual sphere; in doing so they had made themselves rebels against divinely constituted authority, and the princes' violent response was not merely politically justified but theologically mandatory. This two-kingdoms doctrine — developed partly in response to the crisis and partly as a systematic working-out of positions Luther had already taken — became a foundational element of Lutheran political theology, with consequences that extended across the following centuries: it made Lutheran Christianity structurally supportive of established political authority in a way that would prove, in the twentieth century, both an institutional tendency and a moral catastrophe.

Between seventy-five thousand and a hundred thousand peasants were killed in the suppression of the revolt, with the Swabian League's campaign of reconquest notable for the systematic brutality with which the princes asserted the consequences of rebellion. The Peasants' War left the German peasantry in many regions in a substantially worse legal and economic position than before it began — the lords' response to the challenge to their authority was, predictably, to tighten rather than relax their grip — and left Luther's reputation among the rural population permanently damaged. More structurally, it sealed the Reformation's identification with the princes rather than with popular social movements, and established the pattern — the Reformation as an elite-driven process of territorial adoption rather than a bottom-up social transformation — that would characterise its subsequent development in the German lands.

## The Princes' Reformation: Territorial Adoption and the Emergence of Lutheran Churches

The Reformation's consolidation in the German territories occurred through the mechanism of territorial adoption — the decision by a prince or urban magistracy to introduce evangelical practices and eventually Lutheran doctrine in their territory — rather than through any single central authorising act, and its institutional form was shaped by the political and constitutional arrangements within which each adoption took place.

The theological justification for princely authority over the church in their territories drew on Luther's own writings on temporal authority and on the concept of the prince as an "emergency bishop" (*Notbischof*) — a figure who, in the absence of the legitimate Catholic episcopal structure, had the obligation as a Christian ruler to ensure the proper ordering of church life in his territory. This was, to put it plainly, a significant expansion of the principle Luther had invoked against the papacy: having rejected Rome's authority over territorial churches, the territorial prince stepped into the vacancy. The irony — that the Reformation, which had proclaimed the liberation of the Christian conscience from institutional ecclesiastical authority, produced in Lutheran Germany a church under tighter territorial state control than almost anywhere in Catholic Europe — was noted by contemporaries, including some of Luther's own allies, and has been observed by historians ever since. Luther was aware of the problem and uncomfortable with it; the *Visitations* of the Saxony churches he supported in the late 1520s, which sent teams of inspectors to assess the state of reformed parish life, revealed a state of lay religious ignorance so profound that he concluded that some form of authoritative teaching and institutional structure was indispensable regardless of the theological cost — a conclusion reflected in the production of his *Small Catechism* and *Large Catechism* (both 1529), among the most practically effective pieces of religious education the Reformation produced.

The institutional form of the Lutheran territorial church that emerged across the German Protestant territories through the 1520s and 1530s — a church whose doctrine was defined by Lutheran confessional standards, whose worship was conducted in German using Luther's liturgical reforms, and whose governance was effectively in the hands of the territorial prince operating through consistories (mixed ecclesiastical-secular administrative bodies) rather than through an independent episcopal hierarchy — provided the template that the Peace of Augsburg in 1555 would eventually constitutionalise as the settlement of the religious question within the empire.

## The Diet of Speyer and the Name "Protestant"

The term "Protestant" entered the historical record at the Diet of Speyer in 1529, when the Catholic majority reversed the more permissive arrangement of the first Diet of Speyer (1526) and sought to restrict the spread of evangelical practice pending a general council. The evangelical princes and cities issued a formal protest (*Protestatio*) against this decision — hence "Protestant" — asserting that in matters concerning God's honour and the salvation of souls, each must stand before God and give account for themselves, and could not submit to majority decisions. The Protestatio was simultaneously a claim of individual conscience and a claim of territorial sovereignty: its signatories were exercising both at once, in a document that, for all its theological language, was also a constitutional political act within the empire's legal framework.

The Augsburg Confession, presented to the emperor at the Diet of Augsburg in 1530 and principally drafted by Philip Melanchthon — Luther's closest intellectual ally, considerably more irenic in temperament and more disposed to seek doctrinal formulations that might bridge the gap with Catholic theology — was the first major confessional statement of Lutheran doctrine, designed to demonstrate to Charles V that the evangelical position was not heretical innovation but a return to authentic Christian and patristic teaching. Its reception — Charles V declined to treat it as sufficient grounds for reconciliation — marked the point at which the hope of reunion within a reformed Catholic church under imperial supervision became definitively unrealistic, and the Schmalkaldic League, the defensive alliance of Protestant princes and cities formed in 1531, institutionalised the political-military dimension of the Protestant position in a form that Charles V would eventually challenge militarily in the Schmalkaldic War of 1546–1547.

## The Peace of Augsburg, 1555, and Cuius Regio Eius Religio

The Peace of Augsburg (1555), negotiated after Charles V's partial military success in the Schmalkaldic War had proven insufficient to restore Catholic uniformity in the Protestant territories, constituted the first formal legal settlement of the religious division of Germany, and its terms both reflected and reinforced the structural character of the German Reformation. Its central principle — conventionally summarised in the later formula *cuius regio, eius religio* (whose region, his religion), which was not itself used at Augsburg but accurately captures its logic — established that each territorial ruler had the right to determine the religious confession of his territory, choosing between Catholicism and the Augsburg Confession (Lutheran Protestantism), with the provision that subjects who could not accept their ruler's choice had the right to emigrate. The settlement explicitly excluded the Reformed (Calvinist) tradition, which was not covered by its terms — a gap that would contribute to the tensions leading to the Thirty Years War.

The Peace of Augsburg was not a settlement based on religious toleration in any modern sense: it did not grant individuals the right to choose their own religious affiliation regardless of where they lived, but granted territories the right to choose their confession, with individuals whose conscience diverged from their ruler's choice having only the option of emigration rather than protected domestic dissent. What it was, more accurately, was a constitutional settlement of a political problem within the empire's existing framework — an acknowledgement that the religious division had produced territorial facts that could not be reversed by the means available, and that a workable modus vivendi for the coexistence of two confessions within a single constitutional framework required accepting those territorial facts as the basis of legal settlement. Its religious pluralism was a pluralism of territories rather than of individuals, and its stability proved limited: the Thirty Years War (1618–1648), examined in Chapter 33, demonstrated how fragile a settlement based on territorial confessional uniformity proved when the boundaries of that uniformity were contested and the political incentives for confessional aggression remained powerful.

## The Radical Reformation: Anabaptists and the Left Wing

The Reformation's story cannot be told entirely through its magisterial expressions — Lutheran, Reformed, and Anglican — without acknowledging the constellation of movements conventionally grouped as the Radical Reformation, whose participants drew conclusions from the reformers' own principles that Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin alike regarded with as much alarm as Rome did. The most significant and most widespread of these were the Anabaptists — "rebaptisers," a hostile label applied by opponents to groups who rejected infant baptism and insisted on the baptism of adult believers who could make a conscious profession of faith, since infant baptism was, on their reading, scripturally unwarranted and therefore no true baptism at all, whatever the institutional churches of either confession maintained.

The Anabaptist movement was not a single unified church but a diverse family of groups with varying theological emphases, spread across the Swiss Confederation, southern Germany, the Low Countries, and Moravia from the mid-1520s onward, united by the rejection of infant baptism and by a generally more thoroughgoing application of the *sola scriptura* principle to the ordering of the church than the magisterial reformers were prepared to follow. Where Luther had retained infant baptism, a professional clergy, and the territorial church under princely authority, the Anabaptists typically insisted on gathered congregations of visible believers, lay leadership, the separation of the church from the state and from any coercive instrument of enforcement — and on the pacifism that several but not all Anabaptist groups derived from their reading of the Sermon on the Mount.

This rejection of the sword — and of the oath, of civic office, and of the other forms of participation in coercive political life that the magisterial reformers accepted as legitimate for Christians — placed the Anabaptists outside the political framework within which both Catholic and Protestant churches operated, and made them a threat to the social order that Protestant and Catholic authorities agreed in suppressing with unusual ecumenical consistency. The Münster episode of 1534–1535, in which a group of radicalised Anabaptists under Jan van Leiden seized control of the Westphalian city and established a short-lived theocratic commune marked by polygamy, millenarian fervour, and increasing violence before its bloody suppression by an alliance of Catholic and Protestant forces, provided the magisterial Reformation with the evidence it needed to associate all Anabaptism with social disorder — an association that was unfair to the pacifist majority of the movement but that proved effective as propaganda and that licensed the persecution of Anabaptists across virtually all of the territories where the magisterial Reformation had established itself.

The Mennonites — followers of Menno Simons, the Dutch former priest whose leadership gave the surviving Low Countries Anabaptism its most durable institutional form — represent the most significant survival of the Anabaptist tradition into subsequent centuries, persisting through systematic persecution by cultivating the capacity for community cohesion and geographical mobility that diaspora existence required, and eventually finding in the relative tolerance of the Dutch Republic and later of Pennsylvania a space in which their distinctive community life could be sustained. The Anabaptist tradition's insistence on the voluntary gathered church, the separation of church and state, and the primacy of community discipline over territorial enforcement constitutes one of the more consequential minority theological programmes of the Reformation era, its influence extending forward through the Baptist tradition, through the Free Church movements of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and into the constitutional separation of church and state that the American experiment would eventually institutionalise — not without Anabaptist precedent, even if the direct lines of influence are complex and mediated.
