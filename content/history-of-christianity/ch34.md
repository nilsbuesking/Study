# Part VII — Christianity and Empire (1500–1900)

# Chapter 34 — Mission and Empire: Christianity in the Americas, Africa, and Asia

## The Inseparability of Mission and Empire

The relationship between Christian mission and European colonial expansion across the period roughly 1492–1900 is one of the most morally complex in the entire history of Christianity, and it requires the same analytical discipline applied to the Crusades and the Inquisition: neither romantic idealism about missionary sacrifice and spiritual transformation nor a totalising condemnation that reduces all missionary activity to colonial ideology is adequate to the evidence. The missionaries who crossed oceans and deserts to reach populations no European had previously contacted included genuine saints of extraordinary courage and pastoral dedication alongside agents of cultural destruction whose activities prepared or accelerated the dispossession, exploitation, and decimation of the peoples they claimed to serve. Both are historical facts; the evidence requires holding them together.

The structural relationship between mission and empire was, however, not merely a matter of individual missionaries making individual moral choices within a neutral institutional framework. The papal bulls of the late fifteenth century — the *Inter Caetera* of Alexander VI (1493), which divided the non-Christian world between Spanish and Portuguese spheres of influence and granted the right of conquest and Christianisation to those powers — embedded the missionary enterprise within a framework of colonial sovereignty from the outset, such that the church's presence in the Americas, Africa, and Asia arrived, in most cases, in the hold of colonial shipping, under the protection of colonial arms, and with the practical dependence on colonial infrastructure that made genuine independence of judgement about colonial practice structurally difficult even when missionaries possessed it theoretically. This does not make every missionary a colonial agent, but it does mean that the mission-empire relationship needs to be understood structurally rather than case by case, with the structural relationship providing the context within which individual acts of courage, pastoral dedication, and prophetic resistance to colonial violence can be properly situated and credited.

## The Americas: Conquest, Conversion, and Catastrophe

The Spanish conquest of the Americas, which proceeded with devastating speed from Columbus's landfall in 1492 through the subjugation of the Aztec and Inca empires in the 1520s and 1530s, confronted the church with a set of questions about the nature and rights of the peoples encountered that the existing theological tradition was entirely unprepared for and that the economic and political interests of the colonial enterprise made it practically difficult to answer honestly. The population catastrophe that accompanied conquest — driven primarily by epidemic disease to which indigenous American populations had no immunity, but compounded by the violence of conquest, forced labour in mines and plantations, and the disruption of agricultural systems — reduced the indigenous population of the Caribbean and mainland Americas by estimates ranging from fifty to ninety percent across the sixteenth century, one of the most devastating demographic collapses in human history.

The church's response to this catastrophe was divided and contested in ways that the standard picture of a uniformly complicit institutional church obscures. The Dominican friar Antonio de Montesinos, preaching in Hispaniola in 1511, delivered what is one of the earliest and most powerful denunciations of colonial violence in the historical record, asking the assembled colonists "by what right and by what justice do you hold these Indians in such cruel and horrible servitude?" — a challenge that drew furious response from the colonists and initiated the legal and theological debate about indigenous rights that would occupy the following decades. His fellow Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas, who had himself initially been a holder of an encomienda (a grant of indigenous labour) before a conversion experience led him to become the most sustained and most influential critic of colonial violence in the sixteenth century, spent the rest of his long career — he died in 1566 at more than ninety years of age — documenting the atrocities of the conquest in his *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias* and arguing before the Spanish crown and in public theological debate for the full humanity and rationality of indigenous Americans, their right to political self-determination, and the illegitimacy of the conquest itself.

Las Casas's advocacy contributed directly to the New Laws of 1542, which attempted to regulate the encomienda system and protect indigenous people from the worst abuses of forced labour, and to the Valladolid Debate of 1550–1551, in which he faced Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, who had argued on Aristotelian grounds that indigenous Americans were "natural slaves" whose conquest and forced labour was both legitimate and beneficial. The debate is remarkable as an early instance of an official confrontation between these positions, though its practical consequences for colonial practice were limited by the economic interests of the colonists and the crown. The New Laws were widely ignored or circumvented in the colonies, and Las Casas's theological victory over Sepúlveda in the debate did not translate into the structural reform of the colonial system he sought.

The actual practice of the missions in the Americas varied considerably across region, order, and period. The Franciscan and Dominican missions in New Spain and Peru, which often operated in close proximity to colonial administrative structures and in many cases used colonial coercive power to gather indigenous people into mission towns (*reducciones*) where they could be more easily instructed, baptised, and administered, represent the most directly entangled model of mission and colonial control. The Jesuit missions in Paraguay — the famous *reducciones* (reductions) of the Guaraní, established from 1609 and forming at their height a network of thirty communities with a combined population of perhaps one hundred thousand — represent a more genuinely protective model: the Jesuits created communities specifically designed to shelter the Guaraní from the slave-raiding that devastated surrounding populations, maintained their own armed militia to resist the raiders, and developed a community life that preserved significant elements of Guaraní culture within a Christian institutional framework. The destruction of these missions following the Jesuit suppression of 1767 — when the expulsion of the Jesuits from Spanish territories left the Guaraní communities defenceless against exactly the raiders the Jesuits had protected them from — was a catastrophe that illustrates the degree to which the most benign version of the mission enterprise remained structurally dependent on the political framework it could not ultimately control.

The indigenous Christianity that emerged across the Americas was not simply an imposition of European forms but a complex negotiation in which indigenous people selectively adopted, adapted, and reinterpreted Christian symbols, practices, and narratives in ways that served their own cultural continuity and spiritual needs — a negotiation visible in the syncretic Christianity of Andean communities, in the Virgin of Guadalupe's role in Mexican Catholic identity, and in the persistence of indigenous ceremonial elements within officially Christian practice that the church has at different times condemned, tolerated, and eventually celebrated. This negotiation deserves recognition as an expression of indigenous agency rather than mere passive reception, even while acknowledging the conditions of coercion and cultural disruption within which it occurred.

## Africa: Coastal Mission, Slavery, and the Interior

The early Portuguese contact with sub-Saharan Africa from the 1440s onward initiated a pattern of coastal Christian mission that had specific and significant consequences in several regions before the interior evangelisation of the continent began in earnest in the nineteenth century. The conversion of the Kingdom of Kongo — the large, well-organised state in central western Africa whose king, João I (the name taken at his baptism), converted around 1491 and whose son Afonso I made Kongolese Christianity a genuine, sustained royal commitment across a reign lasting until 1542 — is the most significant early African Christian state, and the Kongolese Christianity it produced was not simply a Portuguese import but a genuinely African Christianisation in which Kongolese religious vocabulary, ritual forms, and political theology were integrated with Christian doctrine in ways that produced a distinctively Kongolese Christian culture.

The tragedy of early Kongolese Christianity was its intersection with the transatlantic slave trade, examined more fully in Chapter 35: Afonso I's letters to the Portuguese king, several of which survive and constitute one of the most remarkable documents of early African literary culture, include repeated and increasingly desperate complaints about the Portuguese slave trade operating in his kingdom, which was depopulating his territories and undermining the Christian kingdom he was trying to build. The response of the Portuguese crown and the Catholic church to these complaints was inadequate to the point of culpability — a pattern of institutional failure that Chapter 35 addresses directly.

The nineteenth century saw the most intensive phase of African Christian mission, conducted primarily by Protestant missionary societies — the London Missionary Society, the Church Missionary Society, the Basel Mission, and others — alongside Catholic orders including the White Fathers and the Holy Ghost Fathers, in the context of the "Scramble for Africa" that divided the continent among European colonial powers between 1880 and 1914. The relationship between missionary and colonial activity in this period was complex and varied: missionaries frequently preceded colonial administrators, providing the linguistic and ethnographic knowledge that made colonial administration more effective; they also frequently criticised specific colonial abuses, advocated for indigenous rights, and provided through mission schools the education that produced the African leadership of the twentieth-century independence movements. The figure of David Livingstone, whose exploration of central Africa was motivated by a combination of genuine evangelical mission, opposition to the Arab slave trade, and a conviction that "Christianity, Commerce, and Civilisation" together constituted the solution to Africa's problems, illustrates the ambiguity of the missionary position: genuinely opposed to the slave trade and genuinely concerned for African welfare, while simultaneously a participant in the cultural imperialism that made European colonialism seem to Europeans like an opportunity for others rather than an imposition on them.

## Asia: Ricci, the Chinese Rites, and the Limits of Accommodation

The Church of the East's extraordinary missionary history in Asia — traced in Chapter 15 from its Persian heartland through Central Asia to Tang dynasty China — had been largely destroyed by the fourteenth century through Timur's campaigns and the subsequent contraction of the Mongol world. The second great wave of Asian Christian mission, beginning with the Portuguese maritime expansion in the early sixteenth century, encountered in several regions the remnants or successors of that earlier tradition, with consequences that illustrated both the opportunity and the tragedy of Christian encounter with Asia's ancient civilisations.

The Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala — the ancient Christian community whose origins Chapter 15 traced to the Church of the East's early mission to India and whose foundation tradition attributed their beginnings to the apostle Thomas himself — encountered Portuguese missionaries in 1498 when Vasco da Gama reached the Malabar coast. The encounter was initially welcoming: these were Christians meeting Christians, and the Saint Thomas Christians hoped that the Portuguese arrival would strengthen their position against local Muslim trading rivals. The subsequent history was considerably less welcoming. The Portuguese, discovering that the Malabar Christians used a Syriac liturgy, followed Church of the East theological tradition, allowed clergy to marry, and owed obedience to a patriarch in Mesopotamia rather than to Rome, concluded that they required reform and correction rather than fraternal recognition. The Synod of Diamper (1599), convened by the Archbishop of Goa, imposed Latin liturgical forms, Roman authority, and Tridentine discipline on the Malabar Christians under conditions of Portuguese colonial pressure that made refusal practically difficult — a forced ecclesiastical submission that the community remembered as an act of cultural violence rather than spiritual guidance, and that generated a schism in 1653 (the Coonan Cross Oath) in which a large portion of the Malabar Christian community rejected Portuguese authority and sought alternative episcopal succession, eventually generating the multiple distinct denominations of Kerala Christianity that exist to the present day. The story of the Saint Thomas Christians and Portuguese mission is one of the most instructive in the entire history of Christianity's encounters with its own pluralism — the meeting of two ancient Christian traditions that ended in schism rather than recognition, because one tradition possessed the colonial power to impose rather than negotiate.

The Jesuit mission to China, initiated by Matteo Ricci (1552–1610) and developed by his successors over the following century, represents the most ambitious and most intellectually sophisticated attempt at cultural accommodation in the history of Christian mission. Ricci, a humanist-trained scientist as well as a priest, entered China not as a conqueror's chaplain but as a scholar offering European mathematics, astronomy, and cartography to the Ming court in exchange for the opportunity to present Christian teaching to the Chinese educated class. His strategy of accommodation — wearing the robes of a Confucian scholar, learning classical Chinese at a level that permitted him to engage Chinese philosophy on its own terms, presenting Christianity as the completion and fulfilment of classical Confucian moral wisdom rather than its foreign replacement, and tolerating the continuation of Chinese ritual practices including ancestral veneration and participation in Confucian ceremonies as expressions of cultural respect rather than religious idolatry — produced a genuinely remarkable intellectual encounter and a small but distinguished community of Chinese Christian converts including several court officials and mathematicians.

The Chinese Rites Controversy — the dispute over whether Ricci's accommodations were theologically legitimate or represented a compromise with paganism — generated one of the most damaging institutional decisions in the history of Catholic mission. The Dominicans and Franciscans, whose own mission in China followed a less accommodationist model and who had genuine theological objections to treating ancestral veneration as culturally neutral, appealed to Rome; after decades of debate and multiple conflicting decisions, Pope Clement XI's bull *Ex Illa Die* (1715) condemned the Chinese rites and required Chinese Christians to renounce them, producing the emperor Kangxi's furious response — he had been a supporter of the Jesuit mission and had himself certified that ancestral veneration was a civic and cultural rather than religious practice — and the eventual suppression of the Catholic mission in China. The Qing dynasty's hostility to Christianity that followed, and the subsequent identification of Christianity in Chinese culture with foreign imposition and cultural imperialism, had consequences that shaped the trajectory of Chinese Christianity for the next two and a half centuries. The decision of 1715 represents, in the assessment of most subsequent missiologists, a catastrophic failure of institutional discernment — Rome deciding, from a distance and without adequate cultural understanding, a question whose answer required precisely the kind of local knowledge and cultural sensitivity that Ricci and his successors had spent generations acquiring.

## The Structural Legacy

The five centuries of Christian mission examined in this chapter produced results of extraordinary variety and complexity: indigenous Christianities of remarkable cultural vitality across the Americas, Africa, and Asia; theological and institutional encounters that challenged European Christianity's assumptions about universality and cultural form in ways it is still digesting; and a record of entanglement with colonial violence, cultural destruction, and institutional arrogance that the churches have been slower to reckon with than the historical evidence requires. The missionary enterprise was neither purely an instrument of colonial oppression nor a selfless gift of salvation freely given to willing recipients; it was a human institution operating within the conditions of its time, capable at its best of genuine service and genuine insight, and capable at its worst of violence, condescension, and the confusion of cultural imperialism with divine mandate. Distinguishing these within the specific evidence of specific cases is the proper task of historical judgement, and it is one that the churches' own ongoing engagement with their missionary histories — through the recognition of indigenous saints, the liturgical use of vernacular languages and indigenous cultural forms, and the formal apologies for specific historical abuses that several have issued across the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries — has increasingly, if incompletely, begun to undertake.
