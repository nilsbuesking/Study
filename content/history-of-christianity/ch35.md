# Chapter 35 — The Churches and Slavery: Complicity and Abolition

## The Scale of the Crime and the Church's Involvement

The transatlantic slave trade, which transported somewhere between ten and twelve million enslaved Africans to the Americas between the mid-fifteenth and mid-nineteenth centuries, constitutes one of the largest forced migrations and one of the most sustained institutional crimes in human history. Its relationship to the Christian churches was not peripheral or incidental — it was structural, intimate, and sustained across more than three centuries, involving the active participation of Christian monarchs, Christian merchants, Christian plantation owners, and Christian theologians whose arguments supplied the intellectual justification for a system whose defenders and beneficiaries filled the pews of every major Christian denomination in the Atlantic world. Any honest account of the churches and slavery must begin by stating this plainly before examining the complexity that follows.

The theological justification for slavery was not invented for the transatlantic trade but drawn from a tradition of thought with roots in the ancient world and a continuous presence in Christian theology. Aristotle's doctrine of natural slavery — the claim that some human beings are by nature suited for servitude and benefit from the direction of a rational master — had been absorbed into mediaeval Christian thought, including Aquinas's, without fundamental challenge. The so-called "Curse of Ham" — the passage in Genesis 9 in which Noah curses his son Ham's son Canaan to be "a servant of servants," which the interpretive tradition associated, without any textual warrant, with dark skin and the African peoples — provided the most widely deployed scriptural justification, operating as a proof text across several centuries of pro-slavery theological argument despite its patent interpretive illegitimacy. Paul's letters contained repeated injunctions to slaves to obey their masters, and the broader Pauline and Petrine ethical tradition's accommodation of existing social hierarchies, including the institution of slavery, was consistently deployed against those who argued that Christian principle required its abolition.

These arguments were not marginal. They were advanced by bishops, by popes, by the theological faculties of major universities, and by the clergy of every Christian denomination with a significant presence in the slave-holding societies of the Atlantic world. The papacy's own record is particularly instructive: the same fifteenth-century papal bulls that authorised the Portuguese and Spanish colonial enterprises — *Dum Diversas* (1452) and *Romanus Pontifex* (1455) of Nicholas V — explicitly authorised the enslavement of "Saracens, pagans and any other unbelievers," providing the earliest formal ecclesiastical sanction for the Atlantic slave trade before it had fully developed. Subsequent papal statements on the subject were inconsistent and largely ineffective: Paul III's *Sublimis Deus* (1537) asserted the humanity and rationality of indigenous Americans and implicitly condemned their enslavement, but was not extended to Africans and was not enforced; Urban VIII's condemnation of New World slavery in 1639 had minimal practical effect; and the formal condemnation of the slave trade by Gregory XVI in 1837 came when the abolitionist movement had already won its political battles in Britain and was winning them elsewhere — too late, and from a church that had never mounted anything approaching the sustained institutional opposition to slavery that the gravity of the crime required.

## Institutional Entanglement: Churches as Slaveholders

The churches' complicity extended beyond theological justification to direct institutional participation in the slave system. The Catholic Church's missionary orders — the Jesuits, Dominicans, and Franciscans — held enslaved people on their mission properties and plantation estates across the Americas; the Jesuit province of Maryland held hundreds of enslaved people on its tobacco plantations, and the famous 1838 sale of 272 enslaved people by Georgetown University (then a Jesuit institution) to Louisiana planters — a transaction uncovered by modern scholarship and the subject of a formal university apology and reparations programme in the twenty-first century — is one of the most thoroughly documented cases of a religious institution directly participating in the trade in human beings. Protestant denominations were equally implicated: the Church of England's Society for the Propagation of the Gospel branded the word "Society" on the chests of the enslaved people it held on its Codrington plantation in Barbados; Anglican, Methodist, Baptist, and Presbyterian churches in the American South not only tolerated but actively provided theological support for the slave system, with the Southern Baptist Convention — founded in 1845 specifically over the question of whether slaveholders could be appointed as missionaries (the Convention held that they could) — representing the most visible institutional expression of American Protestant churches' alignment with the slave economy.

The mission to enslaved people themselves was, predictably, a mission conducted almost entirely on the slaveholders' terms. The conversion and Christianisation of enslaved Africans was promoted by some Christian slaveholders precisely because it was understood to reinforce rather than challenge the conditions of enslavement: Christian teaching emphasised obedience, patience, and the rewards of a better life to come, and the specific passages of Paul deployed in slave sermons — "servants, obey your masters" — were selected with the logic of social control rather than the whole counsel of scripture in mind. The use of Christianity as an instrument of ideological subjugation was understood as such by many of the enslaved people who experienced it, which is one of the reasons for the remarkable religious creativity — the development of distinctive African American Christian traditions that reinterpreted the biblical narrative through the lens of the Exodus story, finding in Israel's liberation from Egypt a theological warrant for resistance rather than submission — that characterised the Christianity of enslaved people in ways that their masters neither intended nor, in many cases, recognised.

## The Abolition Movement: Christians as Agents of Change

Against this record of institutional complicity, the emergence of the abolitionist movement represents one of the genuine moral achievements in the history of Christianity's engagement with political life, and it would be as dishonest to ignore this as to ignore the preceding centuries of complicity. The abolition of the British slave trade in 1807 and of British colonial slavery in 1833 were achieved substantially through the sustained political campaigning of a movement with deep roots in evangelical Protestant Christianity — the Quakers, who had been the earliest consistent Christian opponents of slavery from the seventeenth century onward; the Methodists; and the Evangelical Anglican Clapham Sect, whose leading figures William Wilberforce, Granville Sharp, and Thomas Clarkson combined genuine Christian conviction with the political skill to mobilise a mass petition movement of a scale and effectiveness unprecedented in British politics.

The theological argument these abolitionists deployed was not new in its logical content — Las Casas had made a version of it two centuries earlier — but was developed with new intensity and new political organisation: that all human beings bore the image of God (*imago Dei*), that this image was not reducible to cultural, ethnic, or social status, that the Christian gospel proclaimed the equal dignity of every human soul before God, and that any institution which systematically denied this equal dignity was therefore not merely economically or politically objectionable but a sin against both God and humanity that Christian people were obligated to oppose. The Quaker abolitionist John Woolman, whose *Journal* and *Some Considerations on the Keeping of Negroes* represented some of the most theologically careful early abolitionist writing, grounded his opposition in a thoroughgoing application of the principle that Christians were required to do to others as they would have done to themselves — a principle whose application to slavery its defenders had avoided with remarkable consistency.

What the abolition movement's Christian character requires careful qualification rather than simple celebration, however, is the question of timing and the question of what the church as an institution actually did. The Quakers had opposed slavery from the late seventeenth century, but their impact was limited by their small numbers and social marginality. The mainstream Protestant denominations did not mount sustained institutional opposition to slavery until the late eighteenth century at the earliest, and when they did, it was under the influence of a specific evangelical revival — the Great Awakening and its successors — that combined a renewed personal pietism with a social conscience that the earlier established Protestantism had not consistently sustained. The Catholic Church's formal condemnation of the slave trade came in 1837, fifteen years after Brazil's independence had made Portuguese colonialism's specific form of the trade geopolitically declining anyway, and the American Catholic hierarchy largely accommodated itself to the slave system in the Southern states as it had accommodated itself to slavery across three centuries of colonial Catholicism.

The American denominations' handling of the slavery question also illustrates the degree to which the church's internal politics reflected rather than challenged the social divisions of the surrounding society: the major Protestant denominations — Baptist, Methodist, Presbyterian — all split along North-South lines over slavery in the 1840s, with the Southern branches explicitly defending the institution on biblical grounds and the Northern branches moving toward opposition. The Southern Baptist Convention, created in 1845 in defence of the right to appoint slaveholding missionaries, did not issue a formal apology for its founding endorsement of slavery until 1995 — one hundred and fifty years after its founding.

## The Theological Reckoning

The theological arguments deployed in defence of slavery across three centuries were not peripheral misapplications of Christian doctrine by marginal figures; they were mainstream positions held by mainstream theologians drawing on real features of the biblical text and the received interpretive tradition. This is the morally and intellectually uncomfortable fact that any honest reckoning with the churches and slavery must acknowledge: the Bible does not straightforwardly condemn slavery, the interpretive tradition had accommodated it for centuries, and the abolitionists were in some respects making a theological argument that required them to go beyond — or to reinterpret — specific scriptural passages in the light of broader theological principles of human dignity and the image of God. That they were right to do so does not change the fact that they were working against a substantial body of received biblical interpretation rather than simply recovering an obvious and neglected truth.

The implications of this fact extend beyond the specific history of slavery. They illustrate a more general point about the relationship between Christian theology and social ethics: that the biblical text and the interpretive tradition have been used to sanction practices — slavery, the subordination of women, the persecution of Jewish people, the criminalisation of same-sex relationships — that subsequent generations of Christians have come to regard as wrong, and that the process by which those sanction was withdrawn involved not merely a recovery of neglected biblical teaching but a genuine development of moral understanding in which theological claims about human dignity, equality, and the character of God were used to reread and in some cases override specific textual and traditional authorities. The implications of this pattern for ongoing theological disputes about gender, sexuality, and other contested ethical questions are not ones this overview can adjudicate, but they are implications that the history of slavery and abolition makes impossible to ignore.
