# Chapter 37 — The Churches and the World Wars: Complicity, Resistance, and the Reichskonkordat

## Christianity and the First World War

The First World War confronted the Christian churches of Europe with a test that revealed, with uncomfortable clarity, the degree to which institutional Christianity had become identified with the national cultures and national interests of the states within which it operated rather than with any transnational community of faith capable of standing above national conflict. The war was not, in its immediate causes or its prosecution, a religious war; but the churches of every major belligerent power aligned themselves with their own national cause with a rapidity and a theological enthusiasm that the facts of the situation barely supported. German Protestant theologians produced the *Kriegstheologie* ("war theology") that framed the German cause as a holy defence of German Christian culture against the materialism of the West and the barbarism of the East; British clergy preached enlistment from the pulpit in terms that made fighting for King and Country virtually synonymous with serving Christ; French Catholic clergy who had been bitterly hostile to the anticlerical Third Republic discovered in the *Union Sacrée* — the political truce declared at the outbreak of war — a patriotic solidarity that temporarily overrode the theological conflicts of the preceding decades.

The institutional consequences of this national alignment were several. The failure of the major Protestant denominations in Germany, France, Britain, and Belgium to maintain any meaningful solidarity across national lines — the international ecumenical relationships that the late nineteenth century had begun to build essentially dissolved at the war's outbreak — exposed the degree to which the structural reality of nationally established or nationally embedded churches made genuine transnational Christian witness to peace or restraint practically impossible. The lone major Christian voice that maintained something approaching genuine independence from all belligerents was that of Benedict XV, who served as pope from 1914 to 1922 and whose peace efforts — culminating in his 1917 note calling for a negotiated peace that both sides ignored — earned him the contempt of all parties rather than the recognition of a genuinely impartial moral voice. His condemnation as the "pope of the Boche" in France and as pro-Allied in Germany illustrated with depressing precision how the expectation of national religious alignment had become so internalised that genuine neutrality was experienced as treachery by everyone.

The theological consequences of the war's carnage were equally significant. The collapse of the liberal Protestant optimism that had characterised the late nineteenth century's confident identification of the Kingdom of God with the progressive improvement of European civilisation — an optimism whose collapse Karl Barth, a young Swiss pastor horrified by the theological endorsement of the war by his teachers, identified as the beginning of his own theological reorientation — generated across the 1920s and 1930s the movement known as Dialectical Theology or Neo-Orthodoxy, in which Barth, Emil Brunner, and others sought to reassert the radical otherness of God's Word against the liberal tradition's identification of divine and human purposes. Barth's *Römerbrief* (1919, revised 1922), described as a bomb thrown into the playground of liberal theology, marked a genuine theological turning point whose significance extended well beyond the specific circumstances of the war, and whose insistence on the impossibility of deriving divine sanction from human cultural or political achievement would prove, in the 1930s, the most principled available basis for Christian resistance to National Socialism.

## The Reichskonkordat and the German Churches

Adolf Hitler's accession to the chancellorship of Germany in January 1933 confronted the German churches — the largest and most institutionally powerful Protestant churches in the world, and a substantial Catholic minority organised in the Centre Party that had been one of the pillars of the Weimar Republic — with a political challenge whose character was apparent enough to many contemporaries but whose full implications took time to be widely recognised.

The Catholic Church's response in the critical opening months of the Nazi regime was the Reichskonkordat, signed on 20 July 1933 between the Holy See and the Third Reich, negotiated on the German side by Franz von Papen and on the Vatican side by Cardinal Eugenio Pacelli, the Secretary of State who would become Pope Pius XII in 1939. The concordat guaranteed the institutional rights of the Catholic Church in Germany — the freedom of Catholic education, the maintenance of Catholic organisations, the legal status of church institutions — in exchange for the withdrawal of the German Catholic clergy from political activity, which in practice meant the dissolution of the Centre Party, the one major political organisation that might have provided organised Catholic resistance to the Nazi consolidation of power.

The Reichskonkordat's moral legacy is genuinely contested and needs to be stated with precision rather than simplified in either direction. Its defenders have argued that Pacelli was protecting the institutional existence of the church against a regime that, if it chose to act, could have destroyed it, and that the concordat's specific protections were worth the political price — that a church prohibited from political activity but permitted to operate its schools and organisations was more capable of preserving Christian values and eventually resisting the regime than a church that had fought and lost on the political terrain and been suppressed. Its critics — among them the historian John Cornwell, whose 1999 biography *Hitler's Pope* presented the most polemical version of this critique, and the more measured analysis of subsequent historians who have worked through the Vatican archives — have argued that the concordat lent the Nazi regime a crucial international legitimacy at the moment when it most needed it, that it provided a model which Mussolini's Italy and other authoritarian Catholic states subsequently exploited, and that the withdrawal of the Centre Party's political activity removed the only major organised political obstacle to Nazi consolidation of power in Germany. Both arguments have genuine substance. What cannot be disputed is that the concordat was a political calculation that prioritised institutional self-preservation over political resistance, and that the calculation's terms — whatever its subsequent justifications — gave the Nazi regime something it urgently sought in its earliest and most vulnerable phase.

The German Protestant churches' response to National Socialism was more visibly divided than the Catholic response, and the division is important to the historical record. The German Christians movement — a significant current within German Protestantism that enthusiastically embraced National Socialism, sought to align the church with Nazi ideology, and in its more extreme expressions attempted to produce an Aryan Christianity that stripped the Hebrew scriptures from the Christian canon and removed Jewish elements from the New Testament — represented the most flagrant theological capitulation to the regime, and the most explicit expression of the structural connection between German Protestantism's two-kingdoms tradition and its vulnerability to the claim that Christian loyalty required loyalty to the national order. The creation of the Reichskirche under the Nazi-aligned Bishop Ludwig Müller in 1933 represented the institutional expression of this capitulation, and the subsequent history of the German Protestant churches' formal leadership under National Socialism is largely a history of compliant collaboration with the regime's requirements.

Against this stood the Confessing Church, whose formation — crystallised in the Barmen Declaration of May 1934, drafted primarily by Karl Barth — represented the most principled theological resistance to the German Christians and to the state's claim over the church's confession. Barmen's insistence that "Jesus Christ, as he is attested for us in Holy Scripture, is the one Word of God which we have to hear and which we have to trust and obey in life and in death" — and its explicit rejection of "the false doctrine, as though the Church were permitted to abandon the form of its message and order to whatever it wishes or to whatever ideological and political convictions happen to be dominant" — constituted a theological refusal of the German Christians' position whose force derived from its Barthian insistence on the absolute priority of the divine Word over all human cultural and political authority.

The Confessing Church's resistance, however, needs honest qualification. Barmen was principally a resistance to National Socialism's claim over the church's internal confession rather than a resistance to the regime's antisemitic laws and racial persecution. The vast majority of Confessing Church pastors, including most of its leadership, did not oppose the Nazi state's civil antisemitism — the Nuremberg Laws, the exclusion of Jews from civil life, the progressive dispossession of the Jewish community — and some, including notable figures in the movement, endorsed it. The pastoral letter that the Confessing Church's leadership did not issue, the public denunciation of the persecution of the Jews that the Barmen Declaration's own theology logically required, is one of the most consequential silences in modern Christian history — a silence that the movement's individual heroes, examined below, could not by their personal courage remedy at the institutional level.

## Bonhoeffer, the Resistance, and the Martyrs

The figure of Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945) — the German Lutheran pastor and theologian who moved from the theological resistance of the Confessing Church to active participation in the military conspiracy against Hitler, was arrested in 1943 for his involvement in resistance activities, and was hanged at Flossenbürg concentration camp on 9 April 1945, three weeks before the German surrender — represents the most widely known and most theologically significant individual case of Christian resistance to National Socialism, and his life and thought have generated a vast secondary literature that this chapter can only indicate rather than adequately address.

Bonhoeffer's theological significance for the present overview lies in several specific contributions. His *Nachfolge* ("Discipleship," 1937) developed the concept of "cheap grace" — the grace that "costs nothing," that exempts the Christian from the demands of genuine discipleship, that allows comfortable accommodation to the existing order in the name of Christian realism — against which he set "costly grace," the grace that demands the cross. The phrase — and the critique it embodies — constituted, implicitly and then increasingly explicitly, a judgement on the church accommodations of his generation. His later *Widerstand und Ergebung* ("Letters and Papers from Prison," published posthumously), written in the final years of his imprisonment, moved toward a theological vision of a "religionless Christianity" and a "world come of age" that has been variously interpreted as a proto-secular theology, a deeply Lutheran spirituality stripped of its cultural scaffolding, and an unfinished fragment that cannot be systematised without distorting it — the diversity of readings itself indicating the fertility and the incompleteness of the thinking the prison letters represent.

Beyond Bonhoeffer, the record of Christian resistance to National Socialism includes figures whose witness deserves acknowledgement even in a compressed account: the Catholic priest Bernhard Lichtenberg, who publicly prayed for the Jews from his pulpit in Berlin's St Hedwig's Cathedral, was arrested in 1941 and died en route to Dachau; the Protestant pastor Paul Schneider, who refused to give the Nazi salute to the swastika flag and was imprisoned in Buchenwald, where he preached from his cell window to other prisoners until he was killed by lethal injection in 1939; and the thousands of ordinary German Christians who hid Jewish families, forged documents, and provided material assistance at personal risk that the institutional church's official position did nothing to encourage and sometimes actively discouraged. These individual acts of resistance matter enormously as evidence that Christian conscience, properly formed, was capable of resistance even in conditions of extreme social pressure — and they matter equally as evidence of what the institutional church's failure to provide theological leadership meant for those who acted on their own rather than with the support of an organised community.

## Pius XII and the Holocaust: A Reckoning

The conduct of Pope Pius XII. — Eugenio Pacelli, who had negotiated the Reichskonkordat and became pope in March 1939 — during the Holocaust has been the subject of sustained, and still unresolved, historical and moral controversy, and the opening of the Vatican's wartime archives in 2020 has provided new evidence without definitively settling the central questions. The basic facts are not in serious dispute: Pius XII was aware, from relatively early in the process, that the Nazi regime was engaged in the systematic mass murder of European Jews; he did not issue a public, explicit condemnation of the genocide naming it as such; the December 1942 Christmas radio address in which he came closest to public acknowledgement referred to "hundreds of thousands of people who, without any fault of their own, sometimes only because of their nationality or race, have been condemned to death" without naming Jews, Germans, or the Nazi regime; and his post-war defenders and post-war critics have constructed from these facts and from the archival evidence two substantially different narratives whose relative plausibility continues to be debated.

The case for Pius XII rests on several arguments: that public condemnation, as illustrated by the Dutch bishops' explicit 1942 pastoral letter denouncing the deportation of Jews — which the Nazis responded to by deporting Catholic Jews who had previously been exempted — would have produced intensified persecution rather than relief; that the Vatican's extensive covert operations providing false documents, money, and hiding places for Jews represented more effective practical assistance than public denunciation would have achieved; and that Pius XII operated within a tradition of papal diplomatic restraint whose abandonment would have cost the Vatican its capacity for confidential mediation in other contexts. The case against rests on the argument that a public, explicit, personal condemnation of the genocide by the most morally authoritative figure in the Catholic world might have influenced the behaviour of Catholic perpetrators, bystanders, and potential rescuers in ways that covert practical assistance could not; that the precedent of Pope Benedict XV's explicit peace appeals during the First World War demonstrates that papal public statement was not inherently self-defeating; and that the tradition of restraint that Pius XII invoked was, in the specific case of genocide on this scale, a tradition whose application constituted a moral failure regardless of its institutional logic.

What can be said with confidence, navigating between the hagiography of "Hitler's Pope" attacks and the hagiography of beatification advocates, is that Pius XII made a calculated institutional judgement that prioritised the church's diplomatic position and organisational survival over the moral demands of explicit public witness, and that the calculation — whatever its specific merits — produced a silence whose consequences for the Jewish people he was silent about were immeasurably greater than any institutional advantage the silence preserved. Whether a pope with different instincts, a different theological formation, or a less institutional conception of his role would have acted differently is a counterfactual that history cannot answer; what history can say is that the silence was a choice, that choices of this magnitude have moral weight, and that the subsequent Catholic tradition's difficulty in achieving a full and adequate reckoning with this history — from John Paul II's visit to the Holocaust memorial at Yad Vashem to the incomplete process of Pacelli's beatification, which has been stalled precisely by historical objection — reflects a genuine and unresolved problem rather than merely political pressure from outside.

## The Church of the East: The Assyrian Genocide and Modern Remnant

The recurring Church of the East thread reaches its most devastating modern chapter here. The Assyrian Christians — the modern remnant of the Church of the East, concentrated in the mountains of what is now northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, and northwestern Iran, and speaking neo-Aramaic dialects descended from the language of their ancestors' liturgy — suffered a genocide concurrent with and partially overlapping the Armenian Genocide of 1914–1918, in which Ottoman and Kurdish forces killed a substantial proportion of the Assyrian population, with estimates of the dead ranging from 150,000 to 300,000 out of a pre-war population of perhaps 600,000. The Seyfo ("sword" in Syriac) — as the Assyrians call their genocide — has received considerably less international recognition than the Armenian Genocide, reflecting the Assyrian community's smaller size, greater geographical dispersion, and weaker political representation rather than any meaningful difference in the scale or character of what occurred. Subsequent decades brought further displacement, persecution under the Iraqi and Iranian states, and the catastrophic violence of the post-2003 Iraq War and the Islamic State's campaign of 2014–2015, which destroyed the ancient Assyrian Christian communities of the Nineveh Plains — one of the oldest continuously Christian-inhabited territories in the world — and generated a refugee diaspora that has left the Assyrian Christian presence in its ancestral homeland at levels not seen since before the Church of the East's great eastward missions a millennium and a half ago. The community that once sent missionaries to China now struggles for physical survival in Sweden, Australia, and the United States.

## The Long Reckoning

The churches' conduct across the two World Wars — and above all across the Holocaust — initiated a reckoning with the relationship between Christian theology and antisemitism that the following chapter will see develop through the Second Vatican Council's *Nostra Aetate* and beyond. What cannot be overstated is the degree to which that reckoning was made necessary not only by the genocide itself but by the specific theological tradition that had prepared the ground for it: the centuries of institutional anti-Judaism traced in Chapter 24, the supersessionism that Luther's theology both inherited and intensified as traced in Chapter 27, and the institutional church's repeated choice of self-preservation over prophetic witness that the present chapter has documented across two world wars and the Holocaust. The reckoning, such as it has been, belongs to the next chapter's story; the scale of what required reckoning belongs irrevocably to this one.
