# Part II — The Pillars of the Faith

# Chapter 4 — Scripture, Canon, and Authority: How the Bible Took Shape

## A Note on This Part

This Part departs from the chronological narrative method used elsewhere in this overview. Its four chapters present, in reasonably self-contained form, the scriptural and doctrinal material a non-specialist reader needs in order to follow the controversies, schisms, and institutional history that occupy the remainder of the work. Where later Parts narrate how and why a doctrine became contested, this Part explains what the doctrine actually holds and how it reached the form in which it became contestable. Some material inevitably anticipates developments — the maturation of Trinitarian and Christological language, for instance — that are properly narrated as historical controversies in Part III; where that is so, this chapter flags the anticipation explicitly rather than pretending the doctrine arrived fully formed.

## The Hebrew Bible and the Question of "Old Testament"

Christianity's scriptural inheritance begins with the body of texts Jews call the Tanakh (an acronym for Torah, Nevi'im, and Ketuvim — Law, Prophets, and Writings) and Christians have conventionally called the Old Testament, a label this overview uses with the qualification that the term itself encodes a theological claim — that these texts are fulfilled or superseded by a "New" Testament — which is not how the texts' originating Jewish tradition understands them, and which has historically supplied rhetorical ammunition for the supersessionist tendencies examined at several points later in this overview. The earliest Christians, as Chapter 2 established, possessed no New Testament of their own; their scripture was the Jewish scriptural inheritance, generally accessed in its Greek translation, the Septuagint (conventionally dated, for the Torah at least, to the third century BCE, with other books translated over the following two centuries), which differed in scope from the later, narrower Hebrew canon that rabbinic Judaism would eventually settle on, including a number of additional books — Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, 1 and 2 Maccabees, and others, conventionally labelled the deuterocanonical books by Catholic and Orthodox tradition and the Apocrypha by Protestant tradition — whose canonical status would become a live point of dispute only very much later, at the Reformation, treated in Part VI.

The Hebrew Bible's own internal formation is itself a long and only partially recoverable process, conventionally reconstructed by critical scholarship as unfolding across roughly a millennium, from early oral and written traditions through various stages of compilation, redaction, and standardisation, with the Torah's final form generally dated to the Persian period (sixth–fourth centuries BCE), the Prophets substantially fixed by the second century BCE, and the Writings remaining somewhat more fluid until perhaps the first or second century CE — a process this overview need not trace in detail, since it lies properly within the scope of Hebrew Bible scholarship rather than Christian history, but whose basic shape is worth bearing in mind: the scriptures Jesus and Paul knew were themselves the product of centuries of editorial layering, not a text that had dropped from heaven in finished form, and earliest Christianity inherited both this scriptural corpus and, less formally, some of the interpretive habits — allegorical reading, typological reading (treating earlier figures and events as foreshadowings, or "types," of later ones), and the practice of reading scripture against scripture — that Second Temple Jewish exegesis had already developed.

## The Formation of the New Testament Canon

The process by which a specifically Christian body of authoritative texts emerged was gradual, regionally uneven, and driven as much by practical and polemical pressures as by any single doctrinal decision, and needs to be understood as a centuries-long sorting process rather than a single moment of selection. The earliest layer of distinctively Christian authoritative literature was, as established in Chapter 2, Paul's letters, circulating and being copied and exchanged among churches probably already within his own lifetime or shortly after, with a collected Pauline corpus demonstrably in circulation by the early second century (2 Peter, itself among the latest New Testament documents, already refers to a body of Paul's letters being read and, its author complains, misread). The four Gospels achieved a comparably wide and stable circulation somewhat more gradually, but by the later second century Irenaeus of Lyon could already argue, in his *Adversus Haereses*, for the specific and exclusive authority of exactly four Gospels — Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John — using an argument from the four cardinal directions and four principal winds that strikes modern readers as more numerologically than historically motivated, but which is nonetheless valuable evidence that a four-Gospel consensus, excluding the numerous other gospel-type texts then in circulation, was already sufficiently established by his time to need this kind of theological defence rather than mere assertion.

The pressure to define the canon's edges more precisely came substantially from controversy, and the case of Marcion is paradigmatic enough to warrant introduction here even though his theology proper belongs to Part III. Marcion, active in Rome in the 140s, proposed a drastically reduced canon consisting only of an edited version of Luke's Gospel and ten of Paul's letters, purged of what he regarded as corrupting Jewish-scriptural influence, on the theological grounds — examined more fully in the next chapter and in Part III — that the vengeful creator-god of the Hebrew scriptures was a different, lesser deity from the loving Father revealed in Christ. The mainstream church's reaction against Marcion's radical reduction is widely regarded by scholars as one of the more important catalysts pushing the wider church toward explicit canonical self-definition: if Marcion could propose so narrow a canon on theological grounds, the church needed to be able to say, with comparable clarity, both why the Hebrew scriptures remained authoritative for Christians (a question answered, in different ways, by the figures treated in Part III's discussion of how Christianity defined itself against Gnostic and Marcionite alternatives) and which of the numerous other early Christian writings in circulation — including, alongside the eventually canonical books, a substantial body of texts later excluded, among them various other gospels (Thomas, Peter, the Gnostic-tinged Gospel of Judas and others, mostly clustering around concerns treated in Part III's chapter on Gnosticism), apocryphal Acts, and apocalypses beyond the canonical Revelation — properly belonged within the authoritative core.

The criteria that gradually emerged for inclusion, never codified as a single formal test but recoverable from patristic discussion of disputed books, clustered around apostolicity (authorship by an apostle or a close associate of one), antiquity (a text demonstrably early rather than a later production dressed in apostolic guise), orthodoxy (consistency with what was coming to be recognised as the church's wider doctrinal consensus, itself naturally a somewhat circular criterion), and catholicity (broad, geographically widespread acceptance and liturgical use across multiple churches rather than merely local or sectarian favour). Applying these criteria did not produce instant or universal agreement: the so-called Muratorian Fragment, a damaged Latin canon list usually dated to the later second century though its dating remains debated, already lists most of the eventual New Testament while expressing reservations about certain books; several books that did eventually achieve canonical status — Hebrews (whose authorship was uncertain and contested, with even ancient authors divided over Pauline attribution), James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude, and Revelation — continued to be debated, regionally unevenly accepted, or treated with reservation well into the fourth century, particularly in the Greek East, where Revelation in particular faced persistent suspicion over its authorship and its susceptibility to overly literal millenarian readings; conversely, certain texts that did not ultimately achieve canonical status, notably the Shepherd of Hermas and the Didache, continued to be read with near-canonical respect, and sometimes bound alongside canonical texts in early manuscript collections, in some communities for considerably longer.

The list of twenty-seven books now recognised as the New Testament canon across virtually the whole of Christianity first appears, in precisely that form, in Athanasius of Alexandria's Easter letter of 367 CE, and was subsequently affirmed by regional councils (Hippo in 393, Carthage in 397) — though it is worth being precise about what conciliar affirmation actually achieved here, since it is frequently overstated in both directions: these councils did not invent or impose the canon by decree against a previously undecided or contested body of opinion so much as ratify and formalise a consensus that had already substantially emerged through nearly two centuries of the usage- and criteria-driven process just described; equally, the persistence of regional variation — the Syriac-speaking churches' continued use, for some time, of a slightly different canon, and the Ethiopian Orthodox church's broader canon, retained to the present day and including additional books such as Jubilees and 1 Enoch, that reflects this Part IV-discussed church's distinct historical trajectory — means that even the language of a single, universally settled "the canon" achieved at a single fourth-century moment requires the qualification that full uniformity across every regional church was never, in fact, fully achieved.

## Canon, Authority, and the Question Reformation Would Later Reopen

It is worth pausing on what canonisation actually claims, since the underlying logic would resurface with considerable force at the Reformation. The mainstream patristic and subsequently mediaeval position did not generally hold that scripture's authority was self-standing, requiring no further warrant, and entirely separable from the church's own authority to recognise and transmit it; rather, the dominant view held that scripture and the church's living tradition of interpretation and apostolic succession (a concept introduced in this chapter and treated more fully in Chapter 8) operated together, with the church understood as the institution within and through which scripture had been recognised, preserved, and rightly interpreted in the first place — a position later Catholic theology would formalise, partly in direct reaction against Reformation claims, as the mutual inseparability of scripture and tradition. The Reformation principle of *sola scriptura* ("scripture alone"), examined in Part VI, represented a sharp reassertion of scripture's self-sufficient authority against this older model, on the grounds that scripture's authority derived from its own divine inspiration rather than from ecclesiastical ratification — a position its Reformation proponents argued was a recovery of the earlier, purer position rather than an innovation, and one its Catholic opponents argued ignored the historically demonstrable fact, traced in this chapter, that it had been the church's own discernment process that had identified which texts counted as scripture in the first place, such that an appeal to "scripture alone" against the church's authority was, on this reading, parasitic on an authority it claimed to dispense with. This dispute, irreducibly both theological and institutional in the way the original prompt for this overview asks such disputes to be read, will recur in fuller historical context in Part VI; it is flagged here because the canon-formation process just narrated is the historical ground on which it was fought.

## Textual Transmission: How the Words Themselves Survived

A final dimension of "how the Bible took shape" concerns not which books were included but how the actual wording of those books was preserved and transmitted across the centuries before printing, a question the discipline of textual criticism exists to address. No autograph (original manuscript) of any New Testament book survives; what survives instead is a very large body of later copies — several thousand Greek manuscripts alone, ranging from small papyrus fragments such as the early second-century Rylands Papyrus (P52), containing a few verses of John's Gospel and among the earliest surviving New Testament fragments of any kind, to substantial fourth-century codices such as Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, containing most or all of the Bible — copied by hand over centuries, inevitably introducing the kind of scribal variation (accidental errors of copying, and occasionally deliberate harmonising or doctrinally motivated alteration) that any handwritten transmission process generates. The discipline of textual criticism, applying principles developed across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries for weighing the relative reliability of different manuscript families and traditions, has reconstructed a critical text that commands broad scholarly confidence for the overwhelming majority of the New Testament's content, while also identifying a small number of passages — most famously the longer ending of Mark (16:9–20) and the story of the woman caught in adultery in John 7:53–8:11 — judged on strong manuscript and stylistic grounds to be later additions absent from the earliest and best manuscript witnesses, a judgement now reflected in the textual apparatus of virtually all critical and most modern translated editions. This should be understood as a sign of the discipline's rigour rather than as grounds for a more generalised scepticism about the text's reliability: the proportion of the New Testament text affected by genuine, doctrinally consequential uncertainty is, on the assessment of the great majority of textual critics, very small, even though the total number of variant readings across the whole manuscript tradition, simply by virtue of the sheer quantity of surviving copies, is correspondingly large.

With the question of which texts constitute Christian scripture and how their wording has been transmitted now addressed, the chapters that follow turn to what the church, reading these texts, came to believe they teach — beginning with the doctrines concerning God's own nature that would occupy the church's most sustained and consequential theological energies across the patristic period.
