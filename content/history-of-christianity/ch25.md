# Chapter 25 — Avignon, Schism, and the Conciliar Crisis

## The Road to Avignon: Boniface VIII and the Overreach of Papal Power

The high mediaeval papacy's trajectory from the Gregorian reform examined in Chapter 20 to the Avignon period that opens this chapter was not a straight line of continuous growth but a curve that peaked, overextended, and broke within the space of a few decades in the early fourteenth century. The pontificate of Boniface VIII (1294–1303) represents both the fullest theoretical articulation of papal supremacy and, simultaneously, the moment at which that supremacy encountered political resistance sufficiently organised and sufficiently ruthless to expose its structural limits.

Boniface's claims were explicit and sweeping. His bull *Unam Sanctam* of 1302 — issued in the context of a dispute with the French king Philip IV over the taxation of the French clergy, which Philip had been levying for military purposes without papal consent — constituted the most uncompromising assertion of papal authority in the history of the mediaeval papacy, declaring that submission to the Roman pontiff was necessary for salvation for every human creature, and that the spiritual sword (papal authority) was superior to and capable of judging the temporal sword (secular authority) while itself being judged by no human power. The logic was the same hierarchical argument Augustine had not quite made and the Gregorian reformers had partially developed; Boniface pushed it to its theoretical endpoint.

Philip IV of France, one of the most capable and ruthless rulers of the mediaeval period, did not respond to this challenge with theological counter-argument alone. His ministers, led by Guillaume de Nogaret, mounted a systematic propaganda campaign against Boniface, accusing him of heresy, simony, and sexual irregularity with an inventiveness that reflected less concern for accuracy than determination to destroy the pope's moral authority as a prelude to institutional confrontation. The climax came at Anagni in September 1303, where de Nogaret and the Colonna family — Boniface's most bitter Italian enemies — effectively kidnapped the elderly pope in a confrontation whose physical violence is contested in the sources but whose psychological brutality is not: Boniface, treated with deliberate contempt and humiliated in his own residence, was released after three days but died within weeks, in what contemporaries interpreted, depending on their sympathies, as either a martyrdom or a divine judgement. The "slap of Anagni" (*schiaffo di Anagni*), as later Italian tradition called it, became a symbol of the papacy's vulnerability to organised secular power in a way that no previous confrontation between pope and king had quite achieved.

## Avignon: The Captive Papacy?

The Avignon papacy (1309–1377), during which the seat of the papacy relocated from Rome to Avignon in southern France — technically not French royal territory but adjacent to it, under the sovereignty of the Angevin kings of Naples — has been described by its critics, beginning with Petrarch's famous characterisation of it as the "Babylonian Captivity" of the church, as a period of French domination over a captive papacy, and by its defenders as a period of significant administrative reform and institutional development that happened, for pragmatic reasons, to occur on the western bank of the Rhône rather than the Tiber. Both characterisations contain real truth.

The move to Avignon was initially a pragmatic response to the chaotic political conditions in Rome, where factional violence between the great noble families made sustained papal residence extremely difficult, and to the election of Clement V, a Gascon who had never been to Rome and who found Avignon a convenient location close to his existing political connections. What began as a temporary expedient became, under a succession of French popes, a permanent relocation: all seven Avignon popes were French, the college of cardinals became overwhelmingly French in composition, and the entire administrative apparatus of the papal curia settled into Avignon's purpose-built palaces with the comfortable permanence of institutions that have found a congenial home. The Avignon curia was, on any measure, a sophisticated and well-organised administrative machine — its fiscal apparatus, its legal bureaucracy, and its archival systems were considerably more elaborate than anything Rome had previously sustained — and the characterisation of the Avignon period as one of simple French domination ignores the degree to which the popes of the period maintained genuine independence of judgement on many questions and pursued distinctly papal rather than straightforwardly French interests.

That said, the structural reality of French proximity and French political influence was real and consequential. The most notorious episode of the Avignon period, the suppression of the Knights Templar between 1307 and 1312, illustrates the pattern: Philip IV's desire to destroy the Templars was motivated substantially by his debts to the order and his desire to seize their extensive properties, and Pope Clement V's acquiescence in the Templar suppression — including the extraction of confessions under torture that the subsequent historical consensus has judged to be without evidential value, and the burning of the order's leaders including the Grand Master Jacques de Molay in 1314 — was shaped by his political dependence on French royal goodwill in ways that most subsequent historians have judged as a serious failure of papal independence and justice. The Templar affair illustrates more broadly how the Avignon papacy's proximity to French royal power made it vulnerable to pressure in specific politically charged situations even where it maintained real independence in others.

The longer institutional consequence of the Avignon period was the perception, which became progressively harder to rebut across the decades, that the papacy had abandoned Rome — with all the symbolic weight of Peter's grave and the apostolic heritage — for the comfort of a French-adjacent court, and that the church's universal claims were compromised by its visible identification with a single national power. This perception, articulated most powerfully by Petrarch but widely shared across non-French Europe, damaged the papacy's moral authority in ways that the administrative accomplishments of the Avignon curia could not compensate, and it gave the reform-minded critics of the papacy — including the English thinker John Wycliffe and the German polemicists associated with the Emperor Ludwig of Bavaria — both a target and a vocabulary that would survive the papacy's eventual return to Rome.

The return itself, engineered by Catherine of Siena — the Dominican tertiary whose remarkable career of pastoral activity, mystical writing, and epistolary intervention in the major political affairs of her day constitutes one of the most extraordinary cases of female religious authority in the mediaeval church — and by Gregory XI, the last French Avignon pope, who was eventually persuaded to make the journey to Rome in 1377, immediately preceded the crisis that would prove far more damaging to the papacy's authority than the Avignon period itself.

## The Great Western Schism: Three Popes and a Broken Church

Gregory XI died in Rome in 1378, triggering a papal election conducted under intense pressure from the Roman crowd, who demanded a Roman or at least Italian pope. The cardinals, predominantly French and presumably preferring a return to Avignon, elected the Italian Urban VI. under what many of them subsequently claimed was coercion amounting to duress. Urban's behaviour after his election — erratic, abusive to the cardinals, and suggesting to some observers mental instability — provided the French cardinals with both practical motive and canonical pretext to declare his election void on grounds of duress, and to elect a rival claimant, the French Clement VII, who promptly returned to Avignon. The result was the Great Western Schism (1378–1417): two simultaneous claimants to the papal throne, each recognised by different parts of Christendom, each excommunicating the other and his supporters, and each presiding over a parallel curial apparatus making the full range of papal appointments, dispensations, and financial extractions.

The political geography of the Schism's support roughly tracked existing political alliances: France, its allies Scotland and Castile, and the Aragonese territories generally supported the Avignon claimants; England, the German empire, most of Italy, and a significant portion of northern Europe recognised the Roman line. This alignment made the Schism irresolvable through purely ecclesiastical means, since neither claimant had the institutional resources to force recognition from the other's supporters, and both sides had sufficient political backing to persist indefinitely. The spectacle of two popes each anathematising the other and his followers — raising the genuinely terrifying question, for the average Christian believer, of which pope's sacraments were valid and which were therefore rendering their recipients damned rather than saved — was not merely an institutional embarrassment but a pastoral and theological crisis of the first order, striking at the foundations of the sacramental system on which ordinary Christian life depended.

The crisis was deepened when attempts to resolve it through negotiation between the two lines produced, in 1409, a third claimant: the Council of Pisa, convened by cardinals from both obediences who had concluded that neither existing claimant could be removed by ordinary means, elected a new pope, Alexander V., in the hope that both existing claimants would resign or be forced out, but both refused, leaving three simultaneous popes where there had been two.

## The Conciliar Movement: A Constitutional Theory of Church Governance

The Great Western Schism was also the crucible within which the conciliar movement — the most significant constitutional challenge to papal monarchy in the history of the mediaeval church — developed its theoretical framework and attempted its practical solution. Conciliarism, as a theological and ecclesiological position, held that a general council of the church, representing the body of the faithful through its bishops, possessed an authority superior to that of the pope in matters of faith and in cases of emergency such as schism, heresy, or papal incapacity, and that such a council could therefore be convened, could judge and if necessary depose a pope, and could issue binding definitions that the pope himself was obliged to accept.

The theoretical foundations of conciliarism drew on several sources: the conciliar practice of the early church, in which ecumenical councils had been understood as the highest doctrinal authority and had on occasion corrected or overruled individual bishops of Rome; the canonist tradition's elaboration of principles of corporate governance applicable to the church as a corporation; and the political theory of the late mediaeval period, particularly the constitutional arguments about the limits of royal authority that were simultaneously being developed in secular contexts. The most intellectually distinguished conciliarist theorists included Marsiglio of Padua, whose *Defensor Pacis* (1324) was a radical rethinking of the relationship between church and state that anticipated some Reformation positions; Jean Gerson, the Paris chancellor whose more moderate conciliarism sought to reform the papacy rather than subordinate it permanently to conciliar governance; and Francesco Zabarella, the Italian canonist who provided the most technically precise canonical case for the council's superior authority in the specific circumstances of the Schism.

The practical expression of the conciliar movement was the Council of Constance (1414–1418), one of the largest ecclesiastical gatherings in the history of the Western church, convened at the insistence of the German king Sigismund and supported by the reforming wing of the cardinals. Constance succeeded where Pisa had failed: it deposed or accepted the resignation of all three claimants — the Roman line's John XXIII (a notably unsuitable character whose deposition the council pursued with unusual vigour), the Avignon claimant Benedict XIII (who refused to resign and died in his Spanish redoubt still claiming the papacy), and the Roman line's Gregory XII (who resigned with more dignity than his competitors) — and elected a new, universally recognised pope, Martin V in 1417, ending the Schism after nearly four decades.

Constance also issued the decree *Haec Sancta* (1415), asserting that a general council held its authority directly from Christ and that even the pope was subject to it in matters of faith, reform, and schism — the clearest formal statement of the conciliarist position ever issued by a general council — and the decree *Frequens* (1417), mandating the regular convening of future councils to ensure ongoing institutional accountability. Whether *Haec Sancta* constituted binding doctrinal teaching, applicable to the church in all circumstances, or was a specifically emergency measure justified by the unique circumstances of the Schism and without permanent constitutional force, became and has remained one of the most contested questions in Catholic ecclesiology, with the conciliarist tradition citing it as a permanent constitutional settlement and the papalist tradition treating it as a crisis measure that did not alter the papacy's fundamentally superior authority once the crisis had passed.

## Jan Hus and the Council's Moral Failure

The Council of Constance achieved its institutional objectives and failed conspicuously in its moral ones, and the two things are connected in ways that illuminate the limits of conciliarism as a reform programme. The council also addressed, alongside the Schism, the challenge posed by the reforming theologian Jan Hus of Bohemia — a figure examined more fully in Chapter 26 as a precursor of the Reformation, but whose fate at Constance belongs here as evidence of the conciliar movement's character.

Hus had been summoned to Constance under a safe conduct issued by the Emperor Sigismund, having been condemned by the Archbishop of Prague and appealed to the council as the higher authority — an appeal whose logic was entirely consistent with the conciliarist principles the council was itself asserting. The council nevertheless tried, condemned, and burned Hus in July 1415, despite Sigismund's safe conduct and despite Hus's offer to recant positions that could be demonstrated from scripture to be erroneous — an offer the council declined in favour of demanding unconditional submission to its authority. The violation of the safe conduct destroyed Sigismund's credibility in Bohemia and triggered the Hussite Wars that devastated central Europe for the following decades; the burning itself illustrated that the conciliarists' challenge to papal authority did not extend to any corresponding expansion of tolerance for theological dissent below the level of the papacy. A council that claimed superior authority over the pope was prepared to burn its theological critics with as little compunction as any Inquisition operating under papal mandate.

## The Conciliar Movement's Defeat and Its Consequences

The conciliar movement's ultimate failure to institutionalise its programme — the defeat of the Council of Basel's reforming agenda by Pope Eugenius IV in the 1430s and 1440s, the papacy's reassertion of its superior authority at the Council of Florence (where, ironically, the agreement on Eastern-Western reunion that the council also produced demonstrated the papacy's remaining international prestige), and the eventual formal condemnation of conciliarism in the bull *Execrabilis* of Pius II. in 1460, which declared appeals from papal to conciliar authority heretical — had consequences that extended well beyond the immediate institutional question of who governed the church.

The defeat of conciliarism meant that the church entered the sixteenth century without any effective institutional mechanism for the kind of authoritative, reform-minded self-correction that the movement had promised. The reform councils the Reformation crisis would eventually produce — the Fifth Lateran Council (1512–1517), which met just before Luther's challenge and addressed almost nothing of consequence, and Trent (1545–1563) — were convened too late and under conditions shaped too heavily by the crisis they were responding to rather than anticipating. The intellectual energy of conciliarism did not, however, simply dissipate: it fed directly into the ecclesiological arguments of the early Reformers, above all the appeal to council against pope that Luther himself would make in the opening phases of his confrontation with Rome, and into the longer Protestant tradition of thinking about church governance as accountable to the community of the faithful rather than to a hierarchical summit. The conciliarists' defeat was the papacy's institutional victory; whether it was the church's was a question the following century would answer with devastating force.
