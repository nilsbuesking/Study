# Chapter 19 — The Filioque, Rome versus Constantinople, and the Schism of 1054

## A Schism in Slow Motion

The events of July 1054, when Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida, legate of Pope Leo IX, placed a bull of excommunication on the altar of the Hagia Sophia against the patriarch Michael Cerularius of Constantinople, who responded by convening a synod that anathematised the papal legates in turn, have a dramatic quality that has made them the conventional shorthand for the Great Schism between Eastern and Western Christianity. The convention is not entirely misleading — 1054 does mark a moment of unusually explicit and public rupture — but it substantially misrepresents the actual structure of the separation if taken to imply that a single event divided a previously unified church. The Schism of 1054 is better understood as a moment of crystallisation in a process of estrangement that had been developing across several centuries, driven by accumulated divergences in theology, ecclesiology, liturgical practice, and political circumstance, and whose full consequences — the definitive end of any realistic prospect of reunion, the hardening of Eastern and Western Christendom into two genuinely separate religious cultures — were only completed in 1204, when the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople inflicted on the Eastern church a trauma of a different and in some respects deeper order than the formal exchanges of 1054, and which is examined in its proper narrative context in Part V.

The 1054 events were also, it should be noted, legally questionable in ways that further complicate their status as the Schism's definitive moment: Pope Leo IX, in whose name Humbert was acting, had already died in April 1054, before the excommunication was delivered in July, which raises a genuine canonical question about whether Humbert had any authority to act in a dead pope's name — a question the Eastern church was quick to exploit, and which gives the entire episode the character of a legation that had exceeded its brief acting in circumstances that stripped its actions of the institutional backing they nominally claimed.

## The Filioque: A Theological Dispute and Its History

The single most theologically contested point between the Eastern and Western churches was the *filioque* — a Latin word meaning "and from the Son," added in the West to the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed's statement about the procession of the Holy Spirit. In the creed as agreed at Constantinople in 381, the Holy Spirit is described as proceeding "from the Father" (*ex Patre*); the Western addition, making the Spirit proceed "from the Father and the Son" (*ex Patre Filioque*), altered what had been agreed as an ecumenical, binding formula without the convening of another ecumenical council, and did so unilaterally — a procedural grievance whose significance the Eastern church never ceased to press, alongside its substantive theological objection to the addition's content.

The addition's origins were gradual and geographically uneven. The theological idea of the Spirit's double procession — from Father and Son, or from Father through the Son, formulations that are not quite the same and whose difference matters in the later technical debate — is present in Augustine's *De Trinitate*, the most influential Western treatment of Trinitarian theology, and in several other Western theological authors. It appears in creedal formulas at Spanish and Frankish councils from the late sixth century onward, inserted apparently without any awareness that it represented a unilateral departure from an ecumenically agreed formula; the Carolingian court at the end of the eighth century became the locus of the most vigorous Western advocacy for the addition's universal adoption, pressing Rome to include it, while the papacy initially resisted — Pope Leo III in 810 famously had the original creed without the *filioque* inscribed in Greek and Latin on silver shields displayed at St Peter's, explicitly affirming the original formula while declining to condemn the Frankish addition as heretical. The *filioque* was only formally incorporated into the creed at Rome itself in 1014, at the imperial coronation of Henry II, making its Roman adoption considerably later than its Frankish and Spanish use and further complicating any simple narrative of the West uniformly imposing an alteration the East uniformly rejected.

The theological substance of the dispute is more than a procedural grievance about unauthorised creedal alteration, though it is also that. The Eastern objection, most fully articulated by Photius, patriarch of Constantinople in the ninth century (whose own dispute with Rome constitutes the most important pre-1054 episode in the estrangement's development, examined below), was that the *filioque* compromises the monarchy of the Father — the patristic principle that the Father is the sole ultimate source and principle (*archē* or *monarchia*) of divinity within the Trinity, from whom both Son and Spirit derive their being, such that the Spirit's derivation from the Son as well as the Father introduces a second principle of origination within the Godhead that disrupts the unity of the divine source. The Western position, drawing on Augustine's more symmetrical Trinitarian theology in which the Spirit proceeds as the mutual love of Father and Son and the Spirit's double procession expresses the relational structure of the Trinity, held that the *filioque* was not a departure from Nicene orthodoxy but a legitimate theological clarification of it, expressing what the original formula implied even if it did not explicitly state.

Whether these two positions are ultimately irreconcilable has been a matter of sustained ecumenical discussion across the twentieth century, with several formal agreed statements — including a 1995 document from the Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity and a set of agreed statements from the North American Orthodox-Catholic Theological Consultation — suggesting that the apparent contradiction is substantially, though perhaps not entirely, a product of different but not necessarily incompatible theological frameworks rather than irreconcilable doctrinal commitments, and that the Western church's unilateral alteration of the creed, whatever the theological merits of its content, was an act of procedural illegitimacy whose acknowledgement by Rome might remove the most intractable element of the dispute. The Catholic church has not formally retracted the *filioque* from its own creedal practice, but has acknowledged the procedural problem and agreed to refrain from requiring its inclusion in joint Eastern-Western liturgical celebrations — a position that leaves the underlying structural question unresolved but provides a practical basis for continued dialogue.

## Diverging Ecclesiologies: The Structural Incompatibility

Beneath the *filioque* dispute, and ultimately more fundamental to the impossibility of maintaining communion, lay a structural incompatibility between two different understandings of how authority in the church was organised — an ecclesiological divergence whose roots in the different political and institutional contexts of Eastern and Western Christianity were examined across the preceding chapters and whose implications are worth drawing together explicitly here.

The Eastern ecclesiology, shaped by the conciliar tradition and by the *symphonia* of church and empire examined in Chapter 15's discussion of Byzantium, understood authority in the church as fundamentally collegiate and conciliar: the five great patriarchates of Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem constituted together the *pentarchy* (rule of five), each possessing within its own sphere a genuine and independent authority, with the Bishop of Rome holding a primacy of honour (*prōteia timēs*) as first among equals — a genuine pre-eminence in the ordering of the college, but not a jurisdictional supremacy over the other patriarchs or over the churches in their territories. Ecumenical councils, representing the whole church, were the highest doctrinal authority; no single patriarch, including Rome, could override their decisions or act as the sole and final arbiter of doctrine. This ecclesiological framework had been the practical working basis of the church across the patristic period, and the Eastern church understood the post-Constantinian papacy's increasingly assertive claims to universal jurisdiction as a deviation from it rather than its legitimate development.

The Western ecclesiology, whose development had been shaped by the very different political context of a West in which no effective imperial authority existed after the fifth century and in which the papacy had consequently been compelled to assume functions — political mediation, social organisation, the provision of moral authority for secular rulers — that in the East belonged to the emperor, had evolved, by the high mediaeval period, into something considerably more ambitious than the Eastern *pentarchy* model: a claim to universal jurisdictional primacy, grounded in the Petrine commission of Matthew 16 discussed in Chapter 8, according to which the Bishop of Rome as Peter's successor held not merely a primacy of honour but a genuine and divinely instituted authority over the entire church, including the Eastern patriarchates, whose decisions were valid insofar as they were in communion with Rome and consonant with Rome's own teaching. This claim, whose development across the patristic and early mediaeval periods was examined in Chapters 8 and 17, was not the product of cynical power-seeking alone — it was supported by a genuine theological tradition and reflected the real practical necessity, in the West's specific circumstances, of a strong central authority capable of maintaining institutional coherence in the absence of imperial power — but it was structurally incompatible with the Eastern ecclesiology in a way that could not be resolved by splitting the difference, since the two positions were not merely different emphases but different answers to the fundamental question of how the church's authority was ultimately constituted.

## The Photian Schism and the Ninth-Century Crisis

The most important precursor to 1054, and in some respects a more theologically substantive episode than the final rupture itself, was the conflict between Rome and Constantinople in the 860s associated with the name of Photius, appointed patriarch of Constantinople by the emperor Michael III in 858 in circumstances that were themselves irregular enough — he replaced the sitting patriarch Ignatius, who had not resigned voluntarily — to provide Rome with a procedural grievance. Pope Nicholas I, one of the most assertive of ninth-century popes in pressing Roman claims to universal jurisdiction, refused to recognise Photius's appointment, intervened in the affairs of the newly converted Bulgarian church (which both Rome and Constantinople regarded as within their own missionary and jurisdictional sphere in ways that were mutually incompatible), and sent legates to an 867 council that, under imperial pressure, briefly deposed Photius and restored Ignatius. Photius, from exile, wrote his *Encyclical to the Eastern Patriarchs*, which constitutes the most systematic early Greek theological critique of the *filioque*, and convened a council in 867 that condemned Nicholas I and declared Rome's actions in Bulgaria an inadmissible intrusion into Eastern jurisdiction.

The subsequent decade saw further oscillations — Ignatius restored, then Photius restored after Ignatius's death in 877, then a council in 879–880 that both Rome and Constantinople initially regarded as resolving the dispute (Rome's legates subscribed to Photius's rehabilitation) but that subsequent interpretation in both churches would read very differently, with Rome eventually treating it as not binding and the Eastern church treating it as the Eighth Ecumenical Council that had vindicated Photius and condemned the *filioque*. The Photian controversy, never fully resolved, established the *filioque* as the theological currency of East-West conflict, clarified the jurisdictional incompatibilities between Roman and Eastern ecclesiology in Bulgaria and elsewhere, and bequeathed to 1054 a legacy of unresolved grievances that made the later rupture considerably less surprising than it might otherwise appear.

## 1054: The Trigger and Its Limits

The immediate context of the 1054 rupture was a dispute over Norman-controlled southern Italy, where the Norman rulers were imposing Latin liturgical practices on previously Greek-rite communities, and Cerularius, apparently in retaliation, closed Latin-rite churches in Constantinople and directed his associate Leo of Ochrid to write a polemical letter criticising a range of Western practices — the use of unleavened bread (*azymes*) in the Eucharist, Saturday fasting, the *filioque*, and clerical shaving, among others — some of which were genuine theological differences and some of which were matters of discipline and custom whose elevation to theological crisis level reflected the political temperature more than their intrinsic importance. Pope Leo IX sent Humbert to Constantinople to negotiate; the negotiations failed, partly because Cerularius, whose own position in Constantinople was politically complex and who had reasons of his own for confrontation, refused to receive the legates with the respect they demanded; and Humbert, whose temperament was notably ill-suited to the delicate diplomacy the situation required, responded with the excommunication that Cerularius answered with counter-anathema.

What 1054 produced was thus not quite the formal mutual excommunication of the two churches that popular historiography often presents: the excommunications were directed at specific individuals (Cerularius and the legates) rather than at the Eastern or Western churches as wholes, and neither church immediately recognised the events as constituting a final, irreversible rupture — contacts and occasional attempts at reunion continued for decades and centuries afterward, and the formal lifting of the mutual anathemas by Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras I in 1964 was possible partly because the 1054 censures were understood to have been personal rather than institutional. What 1054 marked was nonetheless a real and consequential moment: the clearest public expression yet of an incompatibility that had been building for centuries, after which the realistic prospect of re-establishing the shared communion that had nominally persisted through all the preceding tensions became progressively more remote, sealed definitively not in 1054 but in 1204.

## The Fourth Crusade's Shadow: 1204 and the Real Wound

The sack of Constantinople by the forces of the Fourth Crusade in 1204 — examined more fully in its narrative context in Chapter 21 — inflicted on Eastern Christendom a wound of a different order from anything that preceded it, and its inclusion here is not chronological but structural: any account of the Great Schism that ends in 1054 without acknowledging 1204 gives a radically incomplete picture of why Eastern-Western division proved irreparable. The Latin forces' looting of Constantinople's churches, their installation of a Latin patriarch in the Hagia Sophia, their systematic transfer of Eastern ecclesiastical treasures to Western destinations (the bronze horses of the Hippodrome arriving in Venice, where they remain), and their establishment of a Latin Empire on Byzantine territory for nearly sixty years produced, in the Greek-speaking world, a trauma of betrayal — Christian crusaders sacking the greatest Christian city in the world — whose memory embedded a depth of mistrust between Eastern and Western Christianity that the formal theological disputes of 1054 alone could never have generated. Byzantine writers of the period expressed an unambiguous preference for Muslim rule over Latin Christian domination — a preference that coloured the eventual fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453 with a complexity the simple narrative of Christian civilization's defeat by Islam cannot accommodate.

The division between Eastern Orthodoxy and Roman Catholicism that has persisted from the mediaeval period to the present is thus the product of a centuries-long accumulation: diverging ecclesiologies rooted in genuinely different political and institutional histories, a theological dispute about the *filioque* that was partly terminological and partly substantive, a series of jurisdictional conflicts over missionary spheres and ecclesiastical territories, the political shock of the imperial coronation of 800, the institutional reorganisations of the iconoclast period, the Photian crisis, the 1054 trigger, and the catastrophic violence of 1204. No single cause is sufficient; no single event is the Schism; and the reunion that periodic attempts across the mediaeval and modern periods have sought — at Lyon in 1274, at Florence in 1438–1439, and in ongoing modern ecumenical dialogue — has remained elusive not because the theological differences are clearly and certainly irresolvable but because the institutional, historical, and cultural distances that have accumulated across a millennium of separate development are not dissolved by doctrinal agreement alone, however genuine the goodwill on both sides.
