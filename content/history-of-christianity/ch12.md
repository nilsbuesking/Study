# Chapter 12 — Augustine versus Pelagius: Grace, Free Will, and a Political Church

## Augustine of Hippo: The Mind Behind the Controversy

No figure in the history of Western Christianity casts a longer shadow than Augustine of Hippo, and understanding the controversy that bears his name alongside that of his opponent requires some account of the man before the argument can be properly assessed. Born in 354 in Thagaste, a small town in Numidia, to a pagan father and a devoutly Christian mother, Monica — whose own religious tenacity Augustine would later memorialise in the *Confessions* with a vividness that has made her one of the most recognisable figures in the entire patristic record — Augustine received the best Latin education North Africa could provide, pursued a career as a teacher of rhetoric that took him from Thagaste to Carthage, Rome, and eventually Milan, and spent his early adulthood in a prolonged intellectual and spiritual restlessness that took him through Manichaeism (a dualist religion of Persian origin that we need not examine in detail here but whose residual influence on his later theology, particularly his deeply pessimistic account of the material and the sexual, is much debated), academic scepticism, and Neoplatonism before his famous conversion in a Milan garden in 386, his baptism by Ambrose of Milan in 387, and his eventual return to North Africa, where he was ordained, somewhat against his wishes, as priest in Hippo Regius in 391 and as its bishop in 395.

Augustine's intellectual output across the remaining thirty-five years of his life, sustained through chronic ill-health and the administrative demands of episcopal office while the Western Roman Empire was visibly disintegrating around him — he died in 430 while the Vandals besieged Hippo — was enormous by any standard: the *Confessions*, the first sustained exercise in what might recognisably be called psychological autobiography in Western literature; the vast *City of God*, begun in the aftermath of the sack of Rome in 410 and constituting the most comprehensive Christian theology of history and politics produced in antiquity; the *De Trinitate*, a philosophical treatise on the Trinity of extraordinary depth and subtlety; and a corpus of biblical commentaries, sermons, letters, and anti-heretical works of which the engagement with Donatism examined in Chapter 11 and the engagement with Pelagianism examined here constitute the two most institutionally consequential strands. A reader who approaches the Pelagian controversy without this biographical context risks misreading it as a dispute between two equally abstract positions; Augustine brought to it a personality shaped by his own experience of what he understood to be the absolute priority of divine grace in his own conversion — he had not, on his own account, chosen his way to God through rational deliberation and moral effort but had been seized, redirected, and transformed by a grace he had not sought and could not have generated from his own resources — and this experiential dimension of his position is inseparable from its philosophical content.

## The Question of Free Will: Setting Up the Problem

The dispute between Augustine and Pelagius concerns one of the most fundamental and most persistently contested questions in the entire history of philosophy and theology: whether human beings are genuinely free in their moral choices, and what the relationship is between that freedom, however understood, and whatever divine activity bears on it. To explain what was actually at stake requires a brief philosophical detour before the historical narrative resumes, since the argument is sufficiently precise that a vague account of it produces only confusion.

The concept of free will that matters here is not the question of whether human beings feel that they are making choices, which is not in serious dispute, but a deeper question about the nature and conditions of genuine moral agency. A basic but important distinction is between what philosophers call *compatibilist* and *incompatibilist* conceptions of freedom. On a compatibilist account, a human being acts freely when they act in accordance with their own desires, deliberations, and character — freedom is the absence of external compulsion, not the absence of internal motivation, and the fact that a person's desires and character have prior causes (their upbringing, their nature, their history) does not compromise the freedom of the action that flows from those desires and that character. On an incompatibilist account, genuine freedom requires more than this: it requires that the person could, in some deeper sense, have chosen otherwise than they did — that the causal history leading to the choice does not determine it in a way that removes the agent's ultimate responsibility for it. Both positions have sophisticated modern defenders, and the dispute between them is genuinely unresolved in contemporary philosophy; the point here is that different positions on this question generate very different pictures of what divine grace can and must do in order to enable human salvation without destroying human responsibility.

Now add the theological dimension that both Augustine and Pelagius were working within: the Christian conviction that humanity is fallen, that sin is a pervasive and universal condition, and that salvation requires not merely good behaviour but a fundamental reorientation of the will toward God that sinful human nature, left to itself, cannot achieve. The question then becomes: what exactly is fallen human nature capable of on its own, and what must grace supply? Does grace work by informing the intellect and will with good reasons and good examples, leaving the will free to choose or refuse? Or does grace work by actually transforming the will itself, bending it from its disordered love of self and created things toward God in a way that the will, in its fallen state, could never have accomplished on its own initiative? And if grace transforms the will in this deeper sense, is the resulting choice still genuinely free in any meaningful sense, or has God simply overridden human agency? These are the actual questions at issue in the Pelagian controversy, and they are neither simple nor obviously resolvable.

## Pelagius: The Position and Its Appeal

Pelagius was a British monk — his precise origins are not certainly known, though Britain is the consistent ancient tradition — who arrived in Rome sometime around 380 and became a well-regarded figure in Roman aristocratic Christian circles, known for his ascetic discipline and for a morally serious, demanding reading of Christian life that struck his contemporaries as exemplary rather than problematic. His surviving writings — letters, a commentary on Paul, and the tractate *De natura* — present a position whose internal logic is considerably more coherent and theologically motivated than the label of "heresy" it eventually acquired would suggest.

Pelagius's starting point was the absolute moral seriousness of God's commands. God, being just and good, would not command human beings to do what they were genuinely incapable of doing; the entire moral architecture of scripture, with its commands, prohibitions, and assignments of responsibility and blame, presupposes that human beings have a real and present capacity to obey or disobey, to choose the good or choose evil. If human beings lacked this capacity — if original sin had destroyed or so severely damaged the will's freedom that it could not, in any meaningful sense, choose the good without prior divine intervention — then God's commands would be a cruel joke, praise and blame would be unintelligible, and moral effort would be a category error rather than a genuine obligation. Human nature, on Pelagius's account, retains after the Fall the fundamental capacity of free choice that God created it with; Adam's sin damaged Adam and set a catastrophically bad example, but it did not transmit a corrupted nature to his descendants in the hereditary, quasi-biological sense Augustine would claim. Each person begins with the same natural endowment Adam had, and is fully capable — not easily, not without struggle, but genuinely capable — of choosing to obey God's law.

Grace, on this account, is real and important but functions externally to the will rather than transforming it from within: God supplies grace in the form of revealed law and moral instruction (above all through scripture and the example and teaching of Christ), the forgiveness of past sins through baptism (which Pelagius fully affirmed), and the example of holy lives to inspire and guide. What grace does not need to do, because fallen human nature does not require it, is intervene within the will itself to bend it toward the good it would otherwise inevitably miss — because, on Pelagius's account, the will has not lost its natural orientation toward the good and its genuine capacity to choose it. The result is a theology in which moral effort is fully meaningful, divine commands make full rational sense, and human responsibility before God is uncompromised — a position that appealed strongly to the educated Roman aristocratic circles in which Pelagius moved, for whom the Stoic tradition of moral self-improvement through disciplined reason and will was deeply embedded cultural furniture, and whose members found Augustine's eventual account of human powerlessness before sin and grace not merely theologically unwelcome but morally enervating: if the will cannot choose the good without prior divine transformation, what is the point of moral effort?

## Augustine's Response: Grace, Predestination, and the Bound Will

Augustine's counter-position, developed across a long sequence of increasingly precise and increasingly uncompromising anti-Pelagian writings — *De peccatorum meritis et remissione* (411), *De spiritu et littera* (412), *De natura et gratia* (415), *De gratia et libero arbitrio* (426–427), and the two late and extreme anti-Pelagian works *De gratia et praedestinatione* and *De dono perseverantiae* (428–429) that represent his furthest development of the position — begins from a different but equally fundamental starting point: his own experience, and the scriptural testimony he found overwhelming, that humanity as it actually exists, after the Fall, is not simply imperfect or inclined toward error but radically and comprehensively disordered in its deepest motivational structure.

The Fall, on Augustine's account, was not merely a bad example whose consequences were limited to the damage done to Adam's own soul; it was a catastrophic event that corrupted human nature itself, transmitted biologically (Augustine associated this transmission with sexual generation, a connection whose problematic implications for sexuality more broadly his critics have not been slow to identify and which his own writings on the subject are not free of) to every subsequent human being. The inherited condition — original sin proper — consists above all not in specific sinful acts but in *concupiscence* (*concupiscentia*), an inordinate disordering of the will's desires: where human nature as created was properly ordered, with reason governing appetite and desire oriented toward God as the supreme good, fallen human nature is dominated by desires that pull relentlessly toward lesser goods — pleasure, power, status, self-sufficiency — with a strength and persistence that reason, unaided, cannot overcome. This is not to say that fallen human beings never do genuinely good things or that their wills are simply broken instruments incapable of any positive moral act; Augustine was careful to distinguish between the external, civic moral goods that fallen human beings remain capable of achieving through natural reason and social discipline, and the properly theological or salvific good of genuine love of God and neighbour — the latter requiring a transformation that fallen nature simply cannot generate from within itself.

This is where the question of freedom becomes philosophically precise and genuinely difficult. Augustine does not deny that fallen human beings make real choices; he insists that the will remains the proper proximate cause of human action. What he denies is that the fallen will, as it actually is before grace, has the power to will the supreme good — God — in the manner and with the totality that genuine love of God requires. The will is free, in the sense that it follows its own desires without external compulsion; but its desires are disordered, and it therefore freely and inevitably chooses things that are not God in the way that it needs to choose God. This is what Augustine means by the post-Fall condition of *non posse non peccare* — "not able not to sin" — introduced in Chapter 7: not that every act is sinful in the same degree, but that without grace the will's fundamental orientation remains self-referential and self-directed in a way that constitutes, in its depth-structure, the condition of sin even when producing civically admirable acts on the surface. Freedom, on this account, is a matter of acting from one's own will; but the will is not simply a neutral instrument that can point equally in any direction, and a will that is, at its deepest motivational level, ordered away from God rather than toward God is not genuinely free in the sense that matters for salvation.

Grace, then, must do something more fundamental than Pelagius supposed. It must not merely inform the intellect with good reasons or provide external examples, since the problem is not that the will lacks information but that it lacks the proper *orientation* — the love of God that would make the will capable of genuinely and sustainedly choosing God above all else. Grace therefore works internally, within the will, transforming its deepest desires — what Augustine calls *caritas*, the love of God and neighbour that replaces the disordered *cupiditas* or self-love that dominates the fallen will — in such a way that the person now genuinely and freely wills the good, not because they have been compelled against their own desires but because their desires themselves have been reordered by grace. This is Augustine's concept of *gratia gratum faciens*, "grace that makes pleasing," or what later tradition would call sanctifying grace: a real interior transformation of the will's motivational structure rather than an external nudge to a will that was already capable of choosing the right direction.

The implication that Augustine followed to its logical endpoint — and which drove the controversy to its most extreme positions — is predestination. If grace is strictly necessary for salvation, and if grace is God's free gift rather than a response to human merit (since any merit would itself be the product of prior grace, generating a regress that eliminates the possibility of merit being the basis of grace in any non-circular way), then the question of who receives grace and who does not becomes a question about God's sovereign will rather than human deserving. Augustine's answer, pressed to its fullest in the late anti-Pelagian writings, was a doctrine of double predestination in its implications if not always explicitly in its formulation: God from eternity elects some human beings to salvation and, by withholding the grace that would be necessary for others to be saved, effectively destines them to damnation — not by any positive act of condemnation but by the simple absence of the grace without which, on Augustine's own account, salvation is impossible. Augustine located the justice of this arrangement in the doctrine that all humanity, as members of the *massa damnata* ("damned mass") of fallen humanity, deserves damnation, so that God's election of any is an act of mercy rather than injustice, while God's non-election of others is simply the non-extension of a mercy to which no one has a claim. This argument has struck many readers, then and since, as formally valid but morally troubling in ways that it does not fully resolve: the claim that allowing someone to remain in a condition they were born into, without the supernatural assistance that alone can remedy it, is simply "not giving them something they don't deserve" rather than an active wrong depends on a conception of desert and justice that many of Augustine's critics, then and since, have found inadequate to the moral weight of the conclusion.

## The Political Dimension: From Theological Dispute to Imperial Condemnation

None of the above philosophical and theological substance can be fully understood apart from the political and institutional context in which it was contested, and it is here that the political stakes — far from being mere background to a purely theological argument — become most urgently visible.

Pelagius arrived in North Africa in 410, fleeing the sack of Rome, and found Augustine already established as the dominant theological voice of the region and deeply suspicious of his position. The dispute escalated quickly through a sequence of synodal hearings whose outcomes were far from inevitable and which reflected the specific institutional dynamics of the North African church and its relationship to Rome and the imperial court at Ravenna. Two North African councils in 416 — at Carthage and at Milevis — condemned Pelagian positions and wrote to Pope Innocent I requesting his concurrence, which Innocent supplied shortly before his death in 417. His successor Zosimus, however, initially reversed this, declaring after examining Pelagius and his associate Caelestius that he found nothing objectionable in their positions — a verdict that put the papacy temporarily in the position of defending what North Africa had condemned, illustrating with unusual clarity the absence of any settled, smooth mechanism for resolving such disputes across regional ecclesiastical jurisdictions.

What resolved the stalemate was not further theological argument but imperial intervention. The North African bishops, finding the normal synodal machinery blocked by papal vacillation, lobbied the imperial court at Ravenna, and in April 418 the emperor Honorius issued an imperial rescript condemning Pelagianism and ordering the exile of its proponents — a state coercive measure that preceded and effectively forced the hand of the subsequent Council of Carthage in May 418, which issued a formal set of anti-Pelagian canons on grace and original sin, after which Zosimus, facing both imperial pressure and the threat of a North African episcopal revolt, reversed his earlier position and condemned Pelagius and Caelestius himself. Pelagius disappeared from history at this point; Caelestius continued the struggle for some years, and the most intellectually serious continuation of the Pelagian position was taken up by Julian of Eclanum, a young Italian bishop of considerable philosophical ability whose sustained engagement with Augustine produced the sharpest and most technically demanding critique the Augustinian position received in its author's lifetime, accusing Augustine of having smuggled Manichaean dualism back into Christianity under the guise of orthodox anthropology — an accusation Augustine took seriously enough to engage at great and sometimes tedious length, without, his critics have generally judged, fully resolving.

The key point about the process by which Pelagianism was condemned is that it involved the coordination of North African episcopal pressure, papal authority, and above all imperial coercive power in a manner that reflects the post-Constantinian pattern examined in Chapter 10: theological outcomes in this period were not simply the product of the better theological argument winning open intellectual debate, but of the alliances, political pressures, and institutional resources that different parties could bring to bear on the mechanisms of ecclesiastical and imperial decision-making. Augustine himself was not naive about this — he had, as Chapter 11 established, already committed himself to the principle that imperial coercion was a legitimate tool for returning schismatics and heretics to Catholic unity, and he deployed that commitment consistently in the Pelagian controversy as well as the Donatist one. The result was a condemnation that was simultaneously a genuine theological judgement about a genuinely important theological question and a product of institutional politics in which the North African bishops, with Augustine as their intellectual leader, manoeuvred with considerable effectiveness to secure an outcome whose achievement owed as much to the court at Ravenna as to the councils of the church.

## The Augustinian Legacy and Its Ambivalence

The condemnation of Pelagianism at Carthage in 418 did not end the controversy. A qualified form of the Pelagian position — sometimes called Semi-Pelagianism in later theological usage, though this label was not used by the participants themselves — was developed in the monastic communities of southern Gaul, particularly at Lérins and Marseille, by figures including John Cassian and Faustus of Riez, who accepted Augustine's account of the necessity of grace for salvation while resisting his doctrine of predestination and insisting that human beings could at least take the initial step toward God, to which grace then responded — a position that preserved more space for human moral agency than Augustine's full account allowed while stopping well short of Pelagius's original position. This Gallic qualified Augustinianism was itself eventually condemned at the Council of Orange in 529, which affirmed a moderate Augustinianism on grace while carefully avoiding the most extreme implications of his predestinarian doctrine — a careful institutional compromise that left the Western church's official position somewhat ambiguous on the precise questions that would resurface with devastating institutional consequences when Luther and Calvin took up Augustine against the mediaeval synthesis in the sixteenth century. The Reformers, as Part VI will examine, found in Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings the most powerful available patristic ammunition for their own insistence on grace's absolute priority and human will's bondage, and read him, with some textual warrant, as teaching a version of the positions the Reformation would advance against Rome — a reading that made the question of who had the better claim to Augustine's legacy one of the central historical and theological arguments of the Reformation period.

The enduring philosophical problem the controversy identified — the tension between divine sovereignty (in its theological form: grace's absolute priority) and human responsibility (in its theological form: the meaningfulness of moral choice and the justice of judgement) — was not resolved at Carthage in 418, or at Orange in 529, or in the Reformation, or subsequently: it remains one of the genuinely open and genuinely difficult questions at the intersection of theology and philosophy of mind, which is precisely why the controversy deserves the careful philosophical unpacking this chapter has attempted rather than a compressed narrative that leaves the reader with names but no understanding of what was actually at stake.
