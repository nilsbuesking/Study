# Chapter 8 — Church, Hierarchy, and Authority: Bishops, Councils, and Apostolic Succession

## From Plural Leadership to Monepiscopacy

The New Testament's own evidence for how the earliest communities were governed is, on close inspection, considerably less uniform than the later, settled threefold structure of bishop, priest, and deacon might suggest, and this needs to be acknowledged before that later structure is explained, since the historical process by which it emerged is itself theologically significant. The earliest Pauline communities, as Chapter 2 established, appear to have been governed by a plurality of leaders bearing titles used with some apparent interchangeability and without a single, sharply differentiated hierarchy clearly distinguishing them: *episkopoi* (overseers, the term that would eventually narrow to mean exclusively "bishop"), *presbyteroi* (elders, the term that would eventually narrow to mean "priest," via the Old English and Old French derivations that produced the English word "priest" itself), and *diakonoi* (servants or deacons) appear in the Pauline and other early literature in patterns that most critical scholars read as reflecting a still-developing and regionally variable leadership structure rather than a single fixed threefold order already universally in place.

The shift toward a recognisable monepiscopacy — a single bishop holding distinctive authority over a local community, with presbyters and deacons in subordinate, clearly differentiated roles beneath him — is most vividly attested, as Chapter 3 already noted in its discussion of the persecution context, in the letters of Ignatius of Antioch in the early second century, where Ignatius insists with striking and repeated vehemence on unity with the bishop as essential to the very validity of the church's sacramental and communal life ("let that be considered a valid Eucharist which is held under the bishop," he writes in his letter to the Smyrnaeans), language considerably stronger and more developed than anything in the New Testament itself and strongly suggestive that the monepiscopal structure, however much later theology would come to regard it as primitive and apostolic in origin, was still, in Ignatius's own day and context, a relatively recent development being actively promoted and consolidated rather than a long-settled and uncontested inheritance. By the later second and third centuries, however, the threefold order of bishop, presbyter, and deacon, with a single bishop as the head of each local church (a "diocese," from the Roman administrative term *dioecesis* the church adopted for its own territorial organisation), had become the standard and essentially uncontested structure across the wider church, providing the institutional framework within which the apologetic and intellectual developments traced in Chapter 3 and the doctrinal developments traced in Chapters 5 through 7 of this Part were able to proceed with at least a recognised, if not always doctrinally unified, body of office-holders capable of claiming to speak authoritatively for their communities.

## Apostolic Succession: The Theological Claim

The doctrine of apostolic succession holds that legitimate ecclesiastical authority, and specifically the authority of bishops to teach, govern, and ordain, descends in an unbroken chain of consecration from the apostles themselves, each bishop having been consecrated by other bishops standing, in principle, in a traceable line of consecration extending back to the apostolic college Christ himself commissioned. This doctrine emerged, in its earliest clearly articulated form, not as an abstract claim about institutional continuity for its own sake but as a specifically anti-heretical and anti-Gnostic polemical tool, deployed above all by Irenaeus of Lyon in the later second century — the same Irenaeus encountered in Chapter 4 for his defence of the fourfold Gospel canon — against Gnostic teachers who claimed access to secret apostolic traditions transmitted outside the public, communally verifiable channels of ordinary church life. Irenaeus's argument, developed at length in his *Adversus Haereses* and treated more fully in its proper polemical context in Part III's chapter on Gnosticism, was that the great apostolic churches, Rome prominent among them, could each produce a documented, publicly known succession list of bishops extending back to apostolic founders, and that the doctrine taught by this publicly verifiable chain of bishops — rather than any secret, privately transmitted Gnostic tradition claiming a hidden apostolic pedigree no outside observer could check — constituted the authentic apostolic deposit of faith. Apostolic succession, on this original logic, functioned principally as a guarantee of doctrinal continuity and a check against precisely the kind of unverifiable, esoteric claims to special revelation that Gnostic teachers were making, rather than, in its earliest articulated form, primarily as a claim about the mechanical, sacramental validity of any individual ordination considered in isolation from the wider question of doctrinal fidelity — a distinction that would itself become consequential much later, in the different ways the Catholic, Orthodox, and various Reformation traditions, examined in their proper places across this overview, came to weight the relative importance of unbroken sacramental lineage as against doctrinal continuity in assessing a given church's claim to apostolic authenticity.

## Councils: Conciliar Authority and Its Theory

Alongside the individual bishop's local authority, the wider church developed, from the early fourth century onward, the practice of convening councils — assemblies of bishops, in the case of the most authoritative gatherings drawn from across the entire Christian world and hence termed "ecumenical" (from Greek *oikoumene*, "the inhabited world") — to resolve disputed doctrinal questions and issue binding definitions understood to carry an authority no individual bishop, however senior, could claim alone. The first such ecumenical council, convened at Nicaea in 325 under the emperor Constantine's direct sponsorship and treated in full narrative detail in Chapter 10, established both the conciliar mechanism's practical workability — bishops from across the empire, and in smaller numbers from beyond its frontiers, could in fact be assembled, deliberate, and issue an authoritative common definition — and a durable theological question that would recur at several of the later councils examined in Part III: what, precisely, makes a council's decisions binding and irreformable, as against merely advisory or, as the history of several council that were later judged to have erred demonstrates, capable of being subsequently overturned by a later, more authoritative gathering?

The mainstream answer that gradually crystallised, though never with the kind of single canonical articulation the doctrine of the Trinity received, held that a council's binding authority derived from a combination of factors: the council's universal or "ecumenical" representative character (a regional or merely partial gathering carrying correspondingly less weight than one genuinely representative of the wider church), the council's substantive conformity with scripture and the wider received faith of the church rather than any novel introduction, and, in the most widely held formulation, the council's subsequent reception — its acceptance, over time, by the wider body of the faithful across the whole church — as the ultimate practical confirmation that the Holy Spirit had indeed guided that particular gathering's deliberations, a criterion that necessarily operates retrospectively and which accounts for why certain gatherings that styled themselves ecumenical councils at the time (several of the Christological councils of the fifth century examined in Part IV's narrative, prior to Chalcedon's eventual settlement, claimed ecumenical status that the wider church subsequently declined to ratify) were ultimately reclassified by later tradition as merely regional, mistaken, or outright heretical assemblies — sometimes polemically labelled, as in the case of the council of 449 examined in Chapter 14, a *latrocinium* or "robber synod" — rather than genuine ecumenical councils whose authority bound the wider church.

It is worth flagging clearly, as a point of considerable later significance for the schism narrated in Chapter 19 and the Reformation narrated across Part VI, that the Eastern and Western halves of the church would eventually diverge sharply in how many councils each recognised as possessing this full ecumenical authority — Eastern Orthodoxy recognising seven ecumenical councils, concluding with the Second Council of Nicaea in 787, as authoritative in this fullest sense, while the Roman Catholic tradition continued convening and recognising further councils as ecumenical long after the Eastern and Western churches' communion had fractured, a divergence that is itself one of the clearest institutional markers of the schism's eventual completeness, examined in full in Chapter 19.

## The Petrine Claim and the Question of Roman Primacy

A final dimension of ecclesiastical authority requiring explanation before the historical narrative resumes is the specifically Roman claim to a unique primacy among the churches, since this claim — its scriptural basis, its patristic development, and its sharply differing reception in East and West — underlies a very substantial proportion of the institutional conflict examined across the remainder of this overview, from the Gregorian reform of Chapter 20 to the Schism of Chapter 19 to the Reformation's wholesale rejection of papal authority in Part VI. The scriptural foundation cited for this claim is principally Matthew 16:18–19, in which Jesus tells Peter, "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church... I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven" — a passage whose precise original meaning (whether "this rock" refers to Peter himself, to Peter's confession of faith immediately preceding the saying, or to Christ himself, readings that have been variously favoured by different exegetes and confessional traditions across the centuries) remains genuinely contested among biblical scholars independent of, even if frequently entangled with, the later confessional stakes riding on its interpretation.

What can be said with greater historical confidence is that Rome's claim to a distinctive, eventually supreme authority among the churches developed gradually across the patristic centuries, drawing on several converging strands: the Petrine text itself; the tradition, well attested from an early date though not from the very earliest possible date, that both Peter and Paul had been martyred in Rome, giving the Roman church a doubled apostolic founding pedigree no other church could claim; and Rome's continuing prestige as the empire's historic capital, a prestige the church's own developing administrative structure, examined further in Chapter 18's discussion of the eastward shift of imperial power to Constantinople, would increasingly need to reckon with as the political centre of gravity moved east even as Rome's distinctive ecclesiastical claim continued to develop independently of, and eventually in some tension with, that political shift. The Roman bishop's authority was, through the patristic period covered by this overview's early chapters, generally accorded real and widely acknowledged primacy of honour and frequently exercised real practical influence in adjudicating disputes referred to it from other regions, but the question of whether this primacy was merely one of honour among formally equal bishops (a position the Christian East would maintain and which underlies Eastern Orthodoxy's continuing rejection of papal supremacy as subsequently and increasingly assertively defined in the West) or amounted to a genuine, divinely instituted jurisdictional supremacy over the entire church, binding on Eastern as well as Western Christians, was never definitively settled across the patristic period this overview's first two Parts cover, and would become, as Chapters 19 and 20 will narrate, one of the central and ultimately unresolved fault lines on which the eventual division of Eastern and Western Christendom turned.

## Conclusion to Part II

With the doctrinal and institutional foundations now established across these four chapters — the formation of scripture and canon, the doctrines of God, Christ, and salvation, the sacramental life of baptism and Eucharist, the doctrines of sin, grace, and the afterlife, and finally the structures of episcopal, conciliar, and Roman authority through which the church has historically taught and governed — this overview returns to chronological historical narrative. The chapters that follow trace how these doctrinal and institutional foundations, far from settling into static and uncontested form once articulated, became instead the very terrain on which some of the most consequential and often most violently contested controversies of the patristic age were fought, beginning with the church's confrontation with Gnosticism and Marcion and its first serious attempt to define, against rival claimants, what authentic Christian orthodoxy actually was.
