# Chapter 15 — Global Spread, Phase One: Byzantium, Armenia, Ethiopia, and the Church of the East to China

## Christianity as a Multipolar Phenomenon

By the middle of the fifth century, and well before the Latin West had produced anything resembling a unified Christendom, Christianity was already a genuinely global and radically multipolar phenomenon — present in forms shaped by distinct theological traditions, linguistic cultures, and political contexts stretching from the Atlantic coast of Roman Gaul to the courts of the Tang dynasty in China, from the highlands of Ethiopia to the steppes of Central Asia. The habit of treating the Latin Western tradition as the main trunk of Christian history and all other traditions as peripheral branches is a distortion that the evidence does not support and that this chapter is specifically designed to correct — not by reversing the distortion and treating the Western tradition as peripheral, but by recovering the full geographical and institutional range of Christianity's first-millennium spread as the genuinely multipolar story it was.

This chapter covers the four major traditions of non-Western Christianity that had achieved substantial institutional form by approximately the seventh century: the Byzantine or Chalcedonian Eastern church, centred on Constantinople and covering the Greek-speaking world and much of the Balkans and Eastern Mediterranean; the Armenian Apostolic church, the world's oldest national Christian church, which had developed in political independence from both Roman and Persian imperial frameworks; the Ethiopian Orthodox church, whose roots in the ancient kingdom of Aksum made it one of the earliest and most distinctively African expressions of Christianity; and the Church of the East, the so-called "Nestorian" church, whose missionary energy carried Christianity eastward through Persia and Central Asia to India, the Mongol heartland, and eventually, in the seventh century, to China — a trajectory that made it, at its height, probably the most geographically extended Christian church in the world by area, even as its theological identity remained outside the bounds of both Chalcedonian and Oriental Orthodox communion.

## Byzantium: The Christian Empire of the East

Byzantine Christianity — the Christianity of the Eastern Roman Empire that survived the Western empire's fifth-century collapse and continued as a major political and cultural force until the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 — is sometimes treated as synonymous with Eastern Orthodoxy, but this identification, while broadly correct for later periods, somewhat flattens the complexity of Byzantine Christianity's own internal development across the first millennium. What can be said with reasonable precision is that the Christianity of the Eastern Roman Empire was shaped by a specific and durable conception of the relationship between church and state, conventionally described as *symphonia* — the harmony or consonance of imperial and ecclesiastical authority — in which the emperor occupied a position of uniquely exalted religious significance as God's viceroy on earth, responsible for the church's external welfare and the enforcement of doctrinal orthodoxy, while the patriarch of Constantinople and his episcopal colleagues retained doctrinal and sacramental authority, with the two realms understood to function in complementary rather than competing roles.

This *symphonia* was an ideology considerably more than a stable institutional reality, as the repeated conflicts between emperors and patriarchs, emperors and monks, and emperors and the Roman papacy across Byzantine history amply demonstrate; but it provided a normative framework for thinking about the church-state relationship that differed fundamentally from the trajectory the Latin West was taking, where the collapse of central imperial authority created a vacuum into which the papacy and the episcopate gradually expanded, producing the eventually confrontational model of papal versus imperial authority examined in Chapter 20. In Byzantium, no such vacuum opened: the emperor remained a real and powerful figure throughout, and the church operated within, rather than in competition with, imperial authority — a difference in structural context that goes a considerable way toward explaining why the Great Schism of 1054, examined in Chapter 19, was as much a clash of two fundamentally different models of how sacred and secular authority related to one another as a dispute about the specific theological point (*filioque*) on which it formally turned.

Byzantium's theological culture was characterised by an intensity of doctrinal engagement and popular investment in theological questions — a feature that struck Western visitors, then and since, as remarkable and sometimes bewildering — which itself reflected the structural reality that theological definition had been, since Constantine, an imperial as well as ecclesiastical matter, and that the outcomes of councils and the precise wording of creeds had immediate political and social consequences for the communities involved. The fierce popular investment in Christological formulae that produced the riots, lynchings, and street battles of the fifth and sixth centuries was not mere scholastic hair-splitting; it expressed a culture in which the correct understanding of God and Christ was understood to have direct and urgent implications for the empire's cosmic standing, its military fortunes, and its ultimate divine warrant. Byzantine Christianity also sustained a rich tradition of liturgical and mystical theology — the development of the Divine Liturgy attributed to John Chrysostom, the theological aesthetics of the great church of Hagia Sophia (completed by Justinian in 537), and the Hesychast tradition of contemplative prayer examined in its mature fourteenth-century form later in this overview — that constitutes one of its most distinctive and enduring contributions to the broader Christian inheritance.

## Armenia: The First Christian Nation

Armenia occupies a unique position in Christian history as the first state to adopt Christianity as its official religion, a claim predating Constantine's Edict of Milan by approximately a decade, and doing so moreover in a context of genuine political independence from the Roman imperial framework that makes the comparison with Constantine's adoption of Christianity a genuinely distinct rather than merely earlier version of the same story. The traditional dating places the conversion of the Armenian king Tiridates III by Gregory the Illuminator in 301 — Gregory, who had been imprisoned for years by Tiridates before curing him of a mental illness attributed in the tradition to divine intervention, subsequently became the first Catholicos (supreme head) of the Armenian church, a position that by the fifth century had become hereditary within Gregory's own family before transitioning to an elective model.

The Armenian church's development was from an early date marked by a determination to maintain its own identity against the competing pressures of both the Roman and Sassanid Persian empires between which Armenia was geographically and politically caught, and this determination produced distinctive institutional forms: the translation of the Bible into Armenian in the early fifth century by Mesrop Mashtots, who for this purpose created the Armenian alphabet (a cultural and ecclesiastical achievement of the first order, since it gave Armenian Christianity a scriptural and liturgical life in its own vernacular rather than in Greek or Syriac), and the Armenian church's rejection of the Council of Chalcedon in 451 — a rejection that aligned Armenia with the Oriental Orthodox tradition and against the imperial Chalcedonian settlement, a stance that had partly political dimensions (Chalcedonian orthodoxy was the imperial church's position, and maintaining theological distance from it also maintained political independence from Constantinople) alongside its genuine theological content.

The most searing episode in early Armenian Christian history is the Battle of Avarayr in 451 — fought, with striking symbolic coincidence, in the same year as the Council of Chalcedon — in which Armenian forces under Vartan Mamikonian resisted a Sassanid Persian attempt to forcibly reimpose Zoroastrianism on Armenia. The battle was a military defeat but an effective political and religious success: the Persians, finding Armenian resistance too costly, eventually conceded de facto religious autonomy. Avarayr is commemorated in the Armenian church as a martyrdom in the cause of Christian faith, with Vartan Mamikonian venerated as a saint, and the episode fixed Christianity as an element of Armenian national identity in a way that centuries of subsequent conquest, persecution, and diaspora — culminating in the Armenian Genocide of 1915, treated in its proper historical context later in this overview — would prove unable to extinguish. The Armenian church thus illustrates, with unusual clarity and unusual historical depth, a dynamic that will recur at several points across this overview: the fusion of Christian identity with ethnic and national identity in a people living under external political pressure, a fusion that both sustained Christianity through conditions that might otherwise have destroyed it and created theological and institutional forms shaped as much by the imperatives of cultural survival as by purely doctrinal considerations.

## Ethiopia: Christianity in the Kingdom of Aksum

Ethiopian Christianity is among the most ancient and most distinctive of all Christian traditions, and its origins in the kingdom of Aksum — a prosperous trading state in the northern Ethiopian highlands that had commercial connections with the Roman world, Arabia, and India, and that minted its own coinage and conducted its own foreign policy with a sophistication that challenges any assumption of sub-Saharan Africa as peripheral to the major currents of ancient world history — predate the Constantinian settlement and constitute an authentically African rather than Roman-mediated form of Christianisation. The traditional account dates the formal conversion of the Aksumite royal house to the fourth century, associated with the figure of Frumentius, a Christian from Tyre who had reached the Aksumite court as a young man, possibly as a shipwreck survivor, and who eventually became the first bishop of Aksum — a position he received from Athanasius of Alexandria, establishing the formal link between the Ethiopian church and the Alexandrian patriarchate that would persist for over fifteen centuries, until the Ethiopian Orthodox church gained its own indigenous patriarch in 1959.

The Ethiopian church's theological alignment with the non-Chalcedonian Oriental Orthodox tradition, consistent with its Alexandrian connection and its deep veneration for Cyril of Alexandria's Christological legacy, placed it outside Chalcedonian communion while remaining in close relationship with the Coptic church of Egypt. Its subsequent development was shaped by the arrival in the late fifth and early sixth centuries of the so-called Nine Saints — missionary monks, probably from Syria and Egypt, whose evangelisation of the Ethiopian interior and founding of monastic communities extended Christianity from the royal court into the broader population with a depth and durability that the royal conversion alone had not achieved. The Ethiopian church's canonical scriptures, as noted in Chapter 4's discussion of canon formation, are broader than those of any other Christian tradition, including books such as the Book of Enoch and Jubilees that other traditions had long excluded from their canons — a reflection of the Ethiopian church's access to a wider range of Jewish and early Christian texts in Ge'ez (classical Ethiopic) translation, and of the independent trajectory of its canonical development outside the Greek and Latin textual traditions that shaped Western and Byzantine canon formation. The Ethiopian church also retained practices — Saturday Sabbath observance, dietary laws closely resembling Jewish kashrut, circumcision — that gave it a distinctly Hebraic character absent from other Christian traditions, reflecting both its geographical proximity to Jewish communities in Arabia and the Horn of Africa and its own distinct engagement with the Hebrew scriptures as living liturgical and devotional texts rather than merely as typological prefigurations of the New Testament.

## The Church of the East: Christianity Beyond Rome

The most geographically extensive and institutionally ambitious expression of early Christian missionary energy was the Church of the East — the tradition that grew out of the Syriac-speaking Christianity of Edessa and Nisibis, centred institutionally in the Sassanid Persian empire rather than the Roman, and which carried the gospel east along the Silk Road routes into Central Asia, India, and ultimately China with a missionary reach that no other Christian tradition of the first millennium approached in geographic scope.

The Syriac-speaking churches had deep roots independent of and in some ways prior to the Latin and Greek traditions, drawing on an Aramaic cultural and linguistic world continuous with the language of Jesus himself, and the great school of Nisibis in Persian Mesopotamia became, after the Roman emperor Zeno's closure of its predecessor school at Edessa in 489 (a closure motivated by the school's association with the theological tradition of Theodore of Mopsuestia, whom post-Chalcedonian orthodoxy regarded with suspicion), one of the most intellectually productive centres of theological and scriptural scholarship in the ancient world, carrying forward the Antiochene exegetical tradition in forms that Chalcedonian and Latin Christianity largely lost access to. The formal institutional constitution of the Church of the East as a distinct hierarchy under its own Catholicos-Patriarch, based at Seleucia-Ctesiphon near modern Baghdad, gave it an organisational independence from both Roman imperial Christianity and its theological successors that proved in the long run to be both a source of remarkable missionary energy and a condition of extreme institutional vulnerability.

Operating within the Sassanid Persian empire, and subsequently under the Abbasid Caliphate after the Arab conquests of the seventh century, the Church of the East existed in a political environment that was neither hostile in any systematic, sustained way — the Sassanid emperors generally tolerated it, and the early Abbasid caliphs were in several cases actively interested in the church's intellectual tradition, particularly its role as transmitter of Greek philosophy and medicine — nor supportive in the way Christian Roman emperors had been supportive of Chalcedonian Christianity after Constantine. This produced a church that developed without the institutional entanglement with state power that shaped Latin and Byzantine Christianity, and that consequently relied more heavily on the vitality of its monastic and episcopal networks, its theological schools, and above all the energy of its missionary movement.

The eastward reach of this missionary movement is among the most remarkable phenomena in the entire history of Christianity, and deserves to be recovered from the relative obscurity to which Latin-centric historiography has assigned it. By the sixth century, Church of the East communities were established across Persia and Mesopotamia, in Arabia (where Christian communities existed in several regions before the rise of Islam, a fact of considerable relevance to the religious context of Muhammad's own milieu), and in India, where the Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala — a community whose foundation tradition attributes their origins to the apostle Thomas himself, a claim unverifiable but not chronologically impossible given first-century CE trade connections between the Mediterranean world and the Malabar coast — maintained a Christianity of substantial antiquity, with their liturgical practice conducted in Syriac and their episcopal connection running to the Catholicos of the Church of the East. The Kerala Christian communities would eventually be encountered by Portuguese missionaries in the sixteenth century in circumstances that generated the ecclesiastical conflicts and eventual denominational fragmentation examined in Chapter 34's treatment of the mission-and-empire encounter.

Further east still, the Church of the East's missionaries reached Central Asia by the sixth century, establishing communities among the Sogdian merchants who dominated the Silk Road trade and whose commercial networks provided both the practical infrastructure for missionary movement and a community of literate, cosmopolitan potential converts comfortable with religious plurality. The Xi'an stele, a carved stone monument discovered in 1625 in the Chinese city then known as Chang'an (modern Xi'an), the Tang dynasty capital, bears a bilingual inscription in Chinese and Syriac dated to 781 CE — the document that provides the clearest and most spectacular single piece of evidence for the Church of the East's Chinese presence. The inscription, in elegant Tang Chinese prose, describes the arrival of the missionary Alopen in Chang'an in 635, his reception by the Tang emperor Taizong, and the subsequent establishment of Christian monasteries in the Chinese capital, referring to Christianity as *Jingjiao*, the "Luminous Religion." Tang dynasty Christianity, which flourished under imperial tolerance across the seventh and early eighth centuries before declining under the more hostile atmosphere of the later Tang, represents the easternmost reach of Christianity's first-millennium expansion — a presence genuine enough to leave a monumental inscription but too thin, institutionally, to survive the political and religious changes of the later Tang period, and ultimately extinguished by the persecution of foreign religions under the emperor Wuzong in 845, which suppressed Buddhism, Christianity, and Zoroastrianism in a sweep whose motivations were as much fiscal and political as religious.

The Mongol period of the thirteenth century briefly revived the Church of the East's eastern fortunes in dramatic fashion: several of the major Mongol ruling families, including members of Genghis Khan's own extended family, had Christian wives drawn from the Kerait and Naiman tribes of Central Asia, both of which had been substantially Christianised in the Church of the East tradition, and the early Mongol court showed a remarkable degree of religious pluralism — or religious indifference, depending on one's reading — that allowed Christian clergy to operate alongside Buddhist monks, Muslim scholars, and shamanist practitioners at the Mongol capital of Karakorum. The hope, entertained by several thirteenth-century Latin missionaries including William of Rubruck and encouraged by the Mongol court's own apparent openness, that the Mongol empire might be converted to Christianity and become an ally against the Islamic world, proved illusory: the Mongol rulers of the Middle East converted to Islam rather than Christianity in the later thirteenth century, and the fourteenth-century collapse of the Mongol empire's pax Mongolica was followed by the campaigns of Timur (Tamerlane) in the 1380s and 1390s, which devastated the Church of the East's Central Asian and Mesopotamian heartlands with a thoroughness from which they never recovered, reducing what had been the world's most geographically extensive Christian church to a remnant community in the mountains of northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, and northwestern Iran — the Assyrian Christian communities that survive, in diminished and repeatedly persecuted form, to the present day, and whose modern history and ongoing vulnerability are touched on in Chapter 38's treatment of Christianity's global picture in the contemporary period.

## The Significance of This Diversity

The picture this chapter has traced — Byzantine Christianity's symphonic imperial theology, Armenian Christianity's fusion of faith and national identity forged under persistent external pressure, Ethiopian Christianity's distinctive Hebraic character and broad canon, and the Church of the East's extraordinary missionary reach across Eurasia — is not a collection of footnotes to a Latin story but a set of distinct and genuinely important Christian traditions, each making its own theological, liturgical, and institutional contributions to the total inheritance of the faith. Several consequences of this diversity deserve emphasis as this overview continues.

First, the category "Christianity" in the first millennium is genuinely plural in ways that the subsequent dominance of Latin Western Christendom in European historiography has systematically obscured: there was no single Christian world with a unified institutional structure, but several distinct Christian worlds with different relationships to one another, different theological emphases, different scriptural canons, different liturgical languages and forms, and different political contexts. Second, the theological differences between these traditions — above all the Chalcedonian/non-Chalcedonian divide examined in Chapter 14 — were real and institutionally consequential even where, as the twentieth-century ecumenical dialogues suggest, they may have been partly a product of terminological divergence rather than irreconcilable doctrinal disagreement. Third, and perhaps most importantly for the longer narrative of this overview: the geographical centre of gravity of global Christianity in the first millennium was not in Europe. The majority of the world's Christians in, say, the eighth century lived east and south of the territories usually treated as "Christendom" in standard Western historiographical usage — in Egypt, Syria, Mesopotamia, Persia, India, and Central Asia, as well as in Ethiopia and the surviving communities of North Africa. The subsequent shift of that centre of gravity first to Latin Europe and then, in the twentieth century and beyond, back toward the Global South and East, is one of the major structural stories of Christian history, and it cannot be understood without the foundation this chapter has attempted to lay.
