# Chapter 23 — Scholasticism: Aquinas and the Marriage of Faith and Reason

## The Institutional Revolution: The Rise of the Universities

Scholasticism — the method of theological and philosophical inquiry that dominated Western intellectual life from roughly the eleventh to the fifteenth centuries, and whose greatest achievement was the systematic integration of Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology — cannot be understood apart from the institutional context that produced it. The mediaeval university was not a continuation of the monastic school, still less an antique survival from the Roman world; it was a genuinely novel institution that emerged, with remarkable speed, across the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries in Paris, Bologna, Oxford, Cambridge, and a handful of other cities, in response to conditions — the recovery of classical texts, the growth of urban populations requiring educated clergy and administrators, the emergence of a market for advanced instruction that transcended local monastery or cathedral — that the monastic school's structure could not accommodate.

The cathedral schools, which had served as the primary sites of intellectual life in the earlier mediaeval period, began in the twelfth century to attract teachers of extraordinary ability and, in consequence, students from across Europe, creating in cities like Paris communities of scholars and learners too large and too intellectually ambitious to remain within traditional institutional forms. The university emerged from this concentration as a self-governing corporation (*universitas*) of masters and students, with its own statutes, its own jurisdiction over members (exemption from the ordinary municipal courts being one of the most practically important of these privileges), and its own degrees — the licence to teach (*licentia docendi*) that a successfully examined student received, valid in principle across all Christendom. This institutional form, with its formal curriculum, its disputational method, and its corporate independence, provided the structure within which the scholastic theological and philosophical programme was developed and within which its controversies were fought.

The curriculum of the mediaeval university, at Paris in particular (the dominant centre for theology), was built around two bodies of text: the Bible and the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard, a twelfth-century compilation of patristic opinions on theological questions organised into four books that became the standard framework around which theological teaching was organised, generating an enormous commentary literature across the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, of which Aquinas's own *Commentary on the Sentences* was among the most influential. The pedagogical method was the *quaestio disputata* — the disputed question — in which a proposition was stated, objections raised systematically against it, a determination offered by the presiding master, and the objections then answered in turn, a method of structured dialectical inquiry that trained students in the precision of distinction and the handling of apparent contradiction that the scholastic synthesis required, and that at its best produced genuine advances in conceptual clarity while at its worst generated the kind of sterile, terminologically elaborate controversy that gave "scholasticism" its later, pejorative connotations.

## The Recovery of Aristotle and Its Consequences

The intellectual catalyst for the scholastic synthesis was the recovery, across the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, of the bulk of Aristotle's works in Latin translation — a recovery mediated substantially through Arabic philosophical culture, since it was through the translations made from Arabic versions, many of them in Toledo and other centres of translation in Muslim Spain, that Aristotelian texts on logic, metaphysics, natural philosophy, ethics, and politics, long unavailable in the Latin West, became accessible to Western scholars alongside the Arabic philosophical commentaries of Averroes and Avicenna that had themselves been developed in sophisticated engagement with the Aristotelian corpus. The sheer intellectual scale of what became available was extraordinary: a complete and systematic philosophy of nature, metaphysics, psychology, ethics, and politics, developed with a rigour and comprehensiveness that the Platonic and Neoplatonic inheritance available to earlier mediaeval thinkers had not matched, and presenting, at several points, a picture of the world's operations that was in apparent tension with Christian theological commitments.

The tensions were real and were recognised immediately: Aristotle's physics implied the eternity of the world, which contradicted the Christian doctrine of creation from nothing at a beginning in time; his account of the intellect — as interpreted by Averroes in the doctrine of a single, shared "material intellect" for all humanity — seemed to eliminate individual immortality; his ethics was this-worldly and naturalistic in a manner that sat awkwardly with Christian asceticism and the theology of grace; and his metaphysics, if pursued consistently, seemed to leave little room for divine providence or miracles as interventions in a world governed by necessary natural causes. These tensions generated an initial ecclesiastical reaction of considerable suspicion: the University of Paris in 1210 and again in 1215 prohibited the teaching of Aristotle's natural philosophy and metaphysics, prohibitions that proved impossible to enforce in the long run but that expressed a genuine concern about where unconstrained Aristotelianism might lead.

The intellectually aggressive response to this concern — rather than simply prohibiting Aristotle or accepting the tensions as irresolvable — was the project that defines the great thirteenth-century scholastic achievement: demonstrating that Aristotle's philosophy, correctly understood and where necessary corrected or supplemented by Christian theology, was not the enemy of faith but its most powerful available intellectual ally, capable of providing a systematic, rationally defensible account of the natural world and of human nature within which Christian theology's specific claims about grace, revelation, and salvation could be coherently situated rather than merely asserted against philosophical scepticism. This project was essayed by several thinkers — Albert the Great, Thomas's teacher, made the first comprehensive attempt at a synthesis across the full range of Aristotelian disciplines — but it was brought to its fullest and most enduring expression by Thomas Aquinas.

## Thomas Aquinas: The Synthesis

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) was born into a minor noble family in the Kingdom of Sicily, received his early education at the Benedictine monastery of Monte Cassino, and — against his family's considerable resistance, since the new mendicant Dominican order was regarded as socially beneath the dignity of a family with aristocratic connections — joined the Dominicans and eventually made his way to Paris, where he studied under Albert the Great and spent most of his subsequent career between Paris and various Italian Dominican houses, producing across roughly twenty years of extraordinary intellectual productivity a corpus of writing whose range, precision, and systematic ambition has few equivalents in the history of Western thought. He died in 1274, aged around forty-eight, leaving his greatest work, the *Summa Theologiae*, unfinished — the last section on the sacraments breaks off mid-discussion of penance, after which his secretaries recorded that he said he could write no more, since everything he had written seemed to him "like straw" compared to what he had seen — and was canonised in 1323.

The *Summa Theologiae* (its full title, often truncated to *Summa Theologica* in common usage, though Aquinas's own preferred form was *Theologiae*) is structured as an enormous series of *quaestiones*, each addressing a specific theological or philosophical topic in the disputed-question format, with objections, a determination, and responses to objections. It covers, in three Parts with a supplementary appendix, the nature of God and the Trinity, the creation, angels, and humanity, the moral life and the virtues, the Incarnation, the sacraments, and the last things — effectively a complete map of the entire domain of Christian theological reflection, arranged with a systematic coherence that earlier theological writing, however brilliant in individual moments, had not achieved at comparable scale.

The philosophical foundations of Aquinas's synthesis rest on a set of conceptual distinctions — most of them Aristotelian in origin but developed and modified in his own hands — that are worth introducing in accessible form, since the subsequent chapters of this overview, particularly those dealing with the Reformation's assault on scholasticism, presuppose some familiarity with what was being attacked.

The most fundamental is the distinction between *nature* and *grace*: the natural order, accessible in principle to unaided human reason, within which Aristotelian philosophy operates legitimately and with genuine authority; and the supernatural order, accessible only through divine revelation, which theology addresses and which faith, not reason alone, receives. For Aquinas, these two orders are not in competition but in a hierarchical complementarity: grace does not destroy nature but perfects and elevates it (*gratia naturam non tollit sed perficit*), and reason does not undermine faith but, properly employed, can demonstrate the reasonableness of faith's preconditions (the existence of God, the soul's immortality) and clarify and systematise the content of revealed doctrine even where it cannot reach that content through natural inquiry alone. This distinction allowed Aquinas to take Aristotelian philosophy entirely seriously within its proper domain — natural science, ethics, metaphysics as far as natural reason can reach — without either surrendering the specific claims of Christian revelation to philosophical critique or retreating into a fideism that simply refused engagement.

The *Five Ways* — Thomas's five arguments for God's existence in the opening questions of the *Summa Theologiae* — are probably his most widely known philosophical contribution, and their logical structure is worth brief summary: each begins from an observed feature of the natural world (motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection, the directedness of natural processes toward ends) and argues that the series of natural explanations this feature requires cannot regress infinitely but must terminate in a first cause, itself uncaused, which is what everyone calls God. The arguments are not originally Aquinas's — they draw on Aristotle's unmoved mover and on earlier Islamic and Jewish philosophical versions of the cosmological argument — and their validity has been disputed by philosophers from Hume and Kant onward, but within the Aristotelian metaphysical framework in which they operate they have a genuine rigour that dismissive treatments sometimes underestimate, and they established a pattern of engagement with philosophical scepticism about God's existence — meeting the sceptic on philosophical terrain rather than simply appealing to scriptural authority — that proved immensely influential for the subsequent tradition of natural theology.

In moral philosophy, Aquinas's synthesis of Aristotelian virtue ethics with Christian moral theology produced the natural law framework that has remained the dominant Catholic moral philosophical tradition to the present day: the claim that moral truth is not merely the content of divine commands (a position called divine command theory, which makes morality contingent on God's will in ways Aquinas regarded as theologically and philosophically problematic) but inscribed in the rational structure of human nature itself, accessible in principle to natural reason, such that the basic moral law can be known without revelation even while revelation supplements and perfects what reason can reach. The *Summa*'s extended treatment of the virtues — the four cardinal virtues of prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance, inherited from classical philosophy and integrated with the three theological virtues of faith, hope, and charity that Paul's letters supply — constitutes one of the most detailed and systematic accounts of the moral life in the Western tradition, and was drawn on extensively by the Council of Trent and subsequent Catholic moral theology in ways that make Aquinas's framework the de facto philosophical underpinning of the Catholic moral tradition in a way that Augustinian theology, by contrast, is the underpinning of the Protestant one — a division whose consequences run through the Reformation controversies in Part VI and beyond.

## The Condemnations of 1277 and the Limits of Synthesis

The synthesis Aquinas achieved did not go uncontested in his own lifetime or immediately after his death. The tension between Aristotelian philosophy's implications and Christian theology was felt with particular acuity in the Arts Faculty of Paris, where a group of masters — associated particularly with Siger of Brabant and sometimes labelled "Latin Averroists" by their critics, though the label is disputed — pursued a rigorously Aristotelian philosophy that drew consequences (most controversially, the eternity of the world and the unity of the intellect) that appeared to conflict directly with Christian teaching, while defending this philosophical programme on the grounds that it represented the conclusions of natural reason operating independently of faith, even where those conclusions could not be accepted as theologically true. The idea that something could be philosophically demonstrable and theologically false simultaneously — sometimes glossed as a "double truth" doctrine, though the Averroists themselves did not generally use this phrase — was the most provocative implication of this position, and it generated a reaction from the theological establishment severe enough to require significant political management.

The Bishop of Paris, Stephen Tempier, responding to pressure from the papacy and the theological faculty, issued in 1277 a condemnation of 219 propositions — a remarkably sweeping document that targeted both the radical Aristotelians of the Arts Faculty and, in ways that became increasingly clear on careful reading, several specific positions of Aquinas himself, whose synthesis some of Tempier's advisers regarded as having conceded too much to Aristotelian philosophy in ways that compromised specifically Christian theological commitments. The condemnations of 1277 — followed by an Oxford condemnation of further propositions — created a complex and somewhat paradoxical afterlife: they did not extinguish the scholastic programme, and Aquinas was canonised and his positions officially rehabilitated within a generation; but they forced a degree of intellectual caution and qualification onto the scholastic project that accelerated the development, in the fourteenth century, of positions — above all William of Ockham's nominalism and its separation of the domains of reason and faith — that ultimately dismantled the synthesis from within rather than from without, producing the intellectual situation that the Reformation would inherit and exploit.

The longer trajectory of scholasticism's crisis — the nominalist challenge, the via moderna versus via antiqua controversy, the dissolution of the high mediaeval synthesis — belongs properly to the Avignon period and the pre-Reformation chapters in Part VI, which trace how the intellectual confidence of the thirteenth century gave way to the institutional and theological fractures of the fourteenth and fifteenth. What this chapter has established is the nature and scale of what the synthesis achieved: a genuine integration of faith and reason, nature and grace, philosophy and theology that constituted, at its best, one of the most ambitious and coherent intellectual programmes in the history of Western thought, and whose subsequent dismantling was not the simple triumph of reason over superstition that some Enlightenment accounts presented it as, but a far more complex and internally driven process whose consequences included both the Reformation's theological revolution and the eventual emergence of the scientific and philosophical traditions of early modernity.
