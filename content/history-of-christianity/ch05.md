# Chapter 5 — Core Doctrine I: God, Trinity, Incarnation, and Salvation

## Monotheism Under Pressure: The Problem the Doctrine of the Trinity Solves

Christianity inherited from Judaism an uncompromising monotheism — the conviction, structurally fundamental to the entire tradition, that there is only one God, creator of all things, with no rival or lesser divine beings genuinely sharing in that status. The earliest Christian texts simultaneously and without apparent embarrassment apply language of remarkably high status to Jesus: Paul, writing within roughly two decades of the crucifixion, can speak of Christ as existing "in the form of God" prior to his incarnation (Philippians 2) and as the one through whom all things were created (1 Corinthians 8, Colossians 1), while John's Gospel opens by identifying Jesus with the divine *Logos* who "was God" and "was with God" in the beginning. The Holy Spirit, similarly, is spoken of in the New Testament not merely as an impersonal divine force but in terms — sent by the Father, bearing witness, interceding, capable of being grieved or lied to — that imply some manner of distinct agency. The doctrine of the Trinity exists because the earliest church found itself committed, by its own foundational texts and devotional practice (worship of and prayer to Christ alongside the Father is attested startlingly early, and is itself one of the more historically significant and historically puzzling data points about the earliest post-Easter community), to both halves of an apparent contradiction: there is only one God, and yet Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are each spoken of in ways that seem to require full divine status. The doctrine of the Trinity is best understood not as a piece of speculative philosophical embroidery added to a simpler original faith, but as the church's attempt, worked out with real difficulty and real controversy across some three centuries, to hold both halves of this commitment together without surrendering either — without lapsing into a tritheism of three separate gods, and without collapsing the real distinctions between Father, Son, and Spirit into a single undifferentiated divine unit wearing three different masks.

## The Vocabulary of Trinitarian Definition

The technical vocabulary in which the doctrine eventually came to be stated emerged gradually and was itself a major site of controversy, treated narratively in Part III; this chapter introduces the vocabulary in its settled, post-controversy form so that the historical narrative of how it became settled can be followed without constant definitional interruption. The classical formula, achieved across the fourth century and associated above all with the Cappadocian Fathers — Basil of Caesarea, his brother Gregory of Nyssa, and their friend Gregory of Nazianzus, writing in the Greek East in the decades following the Council of Nicaea — holds that God is one *ousia* (Greek: substance, essence, or being — the technical term for what makes God one) existing eternally as three *hypostaseis* (Greek: literally "that which stands under," conventionally translated "persons," though with the important caution that the English word "person," with its modern connotations of separate individual centres of consciousness in the way three human persons are separate, considerably overstates the distinctness the term was originally meant to convey, and "person" should be understood here in something closer to its older sense of a distinct mode of subsisting or presenting, rather than three discrete selves). Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are thus understood as one God in three persons: each fully and equally divine, sharing a single divine essence without division or degree, yet really and eternally distinct from one another — the Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Spirit — distinguished not by differing degrees of divinity but by their relations of origin within the Godhead (the Father eternally begets the Son; the Spirit eternally proceeds, a term examined further below, from the Father, and, in a later and historically explosive addition examined in Chapter 19, from the Son as well).

The Latin-speaking West, working substantially through Tertullian's earlier formulation (Tertullian, writing in Latin around 200 and treated in Chapter 3 for his apologetic work, is also credited with coining much of the Latin Trinitarian vocabulary that would become standard) and later through Augustine's extensive fifth-century treatise *De Trinitate*, arrived at a substantially compatible position using the Latin terms *una substantia, tres personae* ("one substance, three persons") — terminology that maps onto the Greek formula closely enough that the two traditions could recognise themselves as expressing the same underlying doctrine, though, as Chapter 19 will examine, not without some genuine difficulty in translation between the Greek *hypostasis* and the Latin *persona*, a difficulty that contributed to real, if initially resolvable, confusion during the fourth-century controversies themselves. The single most theologically charged term in the entire vocabulary, *homoousios* (Greek: "of the same substance" or "consubstantial"), used to describe the Son's relationship to the Father, is the word around which the entire Arian controversy narrated in Chapter 10 would turn, and its significance is better explained in that narrative context than asserted here in isolation; it is enough, for now, to note that it became, after Nicaea, the church's settled technical term for affirming that the Son shares the Father's own divine essence fully and without qualification, rather than being a distinct, created, or lesser being who merely resembles or derives from the Father.

## The Incarnation: God Become Human

Closely bound to Trinitarian doctrine, though conceptually distinguishable from it, is the doctrine of the Incarnation — the claim that the eternal Son, the second person of the Trinity, became fully and genuinely human in Jesus of Nazareth without ceasing to be fully and genuinely divine. The classical statement of this doctrine, achieved at the Council of Chalcedon in 451 after the bitter controversies narrated in Part III's later chapters, holds that Christ exists as one person (again using the technical *hypostasis*/*persona* vocabulary established above) in two natures, divine and human, united "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation" — a formula deliberately constructed, as its fourfold negative structure suggests, to rule out a range of specific alternative positions that different theologians and church factions had proposed and that the wider church had come to regard as inadequate, rather than to offer a fully positive, philosophically transparent explanation of how exactly two complete natures, one infinite and one finite, can coexist in a single subject. The two natures are not blended into a hybrid third thing (ruling out what would later be associated with a Eutychian-style position, examined in Chapter 14), nor is the union merely a moral or sympathetic association between two essentially separate subjects loosely cooperating (ruling out what the council judged, with disputed fairness to Nestorius's own actual position, to be the error of Nestorianism, examined in Chapter 13); rather, the one person of the eternal Son is understood to possess, simultaneously and without diminishment of either, both a complete divine nature, by which he is consubstantial with the Father, and a complete human nature, body and rational soul, by which he is consubstantial with humanity.

The theological stakes of getting this right, rather than treating it as an abstruse technicality, were understood by the participants in these controversies, however politically entangled their disputes also were, to bear directly on salvation itself, captured in the patristic axiom, associated above all with Gregory of Nazianzus in his polemic against the Apollinarian position (the claim that Christ possessed a human body but not a complete human rational soul, the divine Logos replacing it), that "what is not assumed is not healed" — the logic being that if Christ's humanity was somehow incomplete or partial, then the corresponding part of human nature he had not fully assumed would remain, on this soteriological logic, unredeemed. A saviour who was not genuinely and fully human could not, on this view, genuinely heal and redeem full humanity from within; a saviour who was not genuinely and fully divine could not, on the corresponding logic explored further below, accomplish a redemption of truly cosmic and not merely exemplary significance. The doctrine of the Incarnation, in other words, was held to matter not as an isolated metaphysical curiosity but because the entire logic of how salvation was understood to work depended on getting the relationship between Christ's divinity and humanity correctly stated.

## Soteriology: How Does Christ's Death and Resurrection Save?

Unlike the doctrines of the Trinity and the Incarnation, which the church did eventually define with conciliar precision and binding technical vocabulary, the question of precisely how Christ's death and resurrection accomplish human salvation — the branch of theology called soteriology — was never resolved into a single official, mandatory formula in anything like the same way, and this should be stated plainly rather than smoothed over: orthodox Christianity has historically tolerated, and continues to tolerate, a real plurality of soteriological models, often held in combination rather than as exclusive alternatives, and a reader should not expect the kind of single settled answer the Trinity and Incarnation chapters above were able to supply.

The earliest and most pervasive model, sometimes labelled *Christus Victor* in modern scholarship following the influential 1931 study of that title by the Swedish Lutheran theologian Gustaf Aulén, understands Christ's death and resurrection primarily as a victorious cosmic confrontation with the powers of sin, death, and the devil, which had held humanity in bondage — a model with strong patristic roots, particularly in the Greek East, sometimes expressed through the striking and theologically daring image of a "ransom" paid to the devil, who held humanity captive through sin, but who, on the version of this image developed by figures including Gregory of Nyssa, is tricked by the ruse of Christ's hidden divinity concealed within his human nature (sometimes called the "bait on the hook" image), overreaching his legitimate claim by attempting to hold the sinless Christ and thereby forfeiting his hold over humanity entirely. A second major model, satisfaction theory, received its most influential systematic statement considerably later, in Anselm of Canterbury's *Cur Deus Homo* ("Why God Became Man"), written around 1098 and treated more fully in its proper chronological context in Part V: Anselm, working from the feudal honour-culture of his own eleventh-century context, reframed sin as an offence against God's infinite honour, requiring a satisfaction proportionate to the offence — a satisfaction no finite human sinner could supply, but which the God-man Christ, being both infinitely capable as God and rightly representative of humanity as man, could and did supply through his voluntary death. The Protestant Reformers, treated in Part VI, would later develop a related but distinct model, penal substitution, recasting Anselm's framework of offended honour in the more explicitly legal and punitive terms of offended divine justice and law: Christ, on this model, bears the punishment that sinful humanity's transgression of divine law justly deserved, satisfying the demands of divine justice in humanity's place. A further, older and recurrently influential strand, moral influence theory, associated particularly with Peter Abelard in the twelfth century and again treated more fully in Part V, locates the saving power of Christ's death primarily in its capacity to reveal and elicit love — the cross as the supreme demonstration of divine love, capable of transforming the believer's heart and will, rather than as a transaction of ransom, victory, or satisfaction accomplished independently of the believer's own response. These models are not, it bears repeating, mutually exclusive options between which orthodox Christianity has had to choose a single winner; they have coexisted, been combined, and shifted in relative emphasis across different periods, regions, and confessional traditions, and a degree of irreducible theological pluralism on precisely how the cross saves, in contrast to the conciliar precision achieved on who Christ is, is a genuine and durable feature of the tradition rather than an oversight awaiting future resolution.

With the doctrine of God, the person of Christ, and the principal models of how his work saves now established, the following chapter turns to the sacramental life through which the church has historically understood believers to be incorporated into and sustained within that salvation — beginning with baptism and the Eucharist, the two sacraments universally recognised across the whole of the Christian tradition.
