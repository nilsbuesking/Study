# Chapter 32 — Witch Hunts: Theology, Society, and Early Modern Persecution

## The Historiographical Problem

The history of the early modern witch hunts is one of the most distorted in all of Christian historiography, subject to systematic exaggeration in the popular tradition and, in reaction, to a revisionist scholarship that has substantially corrected the numbers and the mechanisms while generating its own interpretive debates. Any honest account must navigate between two distortions: the older popular and some feminist historiographical tradition that estimated millions of victims — figures as high as nine million circulated in the twentieth century — and presented the witch hunts as a systematic campaign by the institutional church against women, popular religion, and pre-Christian spirituality; and the opposite tendency, in reacting against these exaggerations, to understate the genuine horror of what occurred and to dissolve the phenomenon into so many local variations that its structural coherence becomes invisible. The position taken here is that careful modern scholarship, working from the surviving trial records, has established a picture that is simultaneously more modest in scale and more complex in causation than either tradition has acknowledged, and that intellectual honesty requires stating both the correction and the complexity without either minimising the suffering of real people or inflating it for rhetorical effect.

The most reliable modern estimates, based on systematic archival research across the European trial records, place the total number of executions for witchcraft across the whole of Europe between approximately 1400 and 1782 (when the last legal execution for witchcraft in Europe occurred, in Switzerland) at somewhere between forty thousand and sixty thousand — a figure enormously smaller than the millions of popular tradition, but representing a very large number of real human deaths for a crime that did not exist. Of those tried but not executed, estimates suggest perhaps two to three times the execution figure were tried and acquitted or received lesser penalties, giving a total of perhaps a hundred and fifty thousand prosecutions overall. These are the figures current scholarship supports; they are subject to ongoing revision as more regional archives are examined, and they vary considerably by region, but the order of magnitude is not seriously contested by specialists.

## The Theological Framework: What Made Witch Belief Possible

The belief in witchcraft as a genuine, demonic threat requiring judicial response was not a survival of primitive superstition foreign to educated Christian culture but a position endorsed, developed, and propagated by some of the most sophisticated theological and legal thinkers of the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, and understanding why requires attending to the specific theological framework within which it made sense.

Christian theology had always acknowledged the existence of the devil and of demonic beings capable of influencing the material world, and the tradition had always maintained some distinction between the legitimate religious practice of prayer and sacrament on the one hand and the illegitimate invocation of demonic powers on the other. What crystallised into the specific witch-belief of the early modern period — the fully elaborated concept of the witches' sabbath, the demonic pact, the flight through the air, the sexual congress with the devil, and the organised conspiracy of witches against Christian society — was not an ancient folk belief but a relatively recent and substantially learned construction, developed primarily in the fifteenth century through the intersection of theological demonology, inquisitorial procedure, and the specific social anxieties of late mediaeval European communities.

The foundational text of witch-hunt culture was the *Malleus Maleficarum* ("Hammer of the Witches"), published in 1487 by the Dominican inquisitors Heinrich Kramer (Institor) and, in the title page attribution whose accuracy is disputed by modern scholars, Jakob Sprenger. The *Malleus* synthesised existing theological and demonological material into a comprehensive prosecution manual — explaining the theological basis for witch belief (including the notorious assertion that witches were predominantly women because of female intellectual weakness and carnal lust, a claim rooted in misogynistic readings of scriptural and patristic texts), describing the specific crimes witches committed, and providing detailed guidance for inquisitorial procedure in witch cases. It was printed in numerous editions and widely distributed, its authority lent by a papal bull of Innocent VIII (1484) that the *Malleus* reproduced, though the bull was less specifically directed at witchcraft than the *Malleus*'s use of it implied.

The *Malleus* was influential but not uniformly authoritative: several Catholic authorities, including the theologians of the University of Cologne who initially refused to endorse it, expressed reservations about its specific claims and procedures, and the work's actual impact on specific regional prosecutions was uneven. What it represents most accurately is not a blueprint that was followed everywhere but the crystallisation of a set of beliefs — the diabolic pact, the organised conspiracy, the demonic sabbath — that, once widely enough distributed, provided the interpretive framework within which individual accusations of maleficium (harmful magic) could be elevated from local disputes about crop failure, illness, or neighbourly malice into participation in a vast satanic conspiracy requiring systematic judicial response.

## The Mechanics of Persecution: Courts, Confessions, and Chain Reactions

Understanding how witch trials actually worked, as opposed to how the *Malleus* and later popular tradition imagined they worked, requires attention to the specific judicial mechanisms through which accusations became executions — and this attention reveals a picture considerably more varied and more structurally complex than the image of church inquisitors systematically burning women.

One of the most important and most frequently misunderstood findings of modern witch-hunt scholarship is the relative roles of ecclesiastical and secular courts in witch prosecutions. The majority of witch trials across Europe were conducted not by church courts or inquisitors but by secular criminal courts — royal, territorial, civic — operating under secular criminal law rather than canon law. The great majority of executions were carried out by secular authorities rather than by ecclesiastical ones, and in several regions the church courts were actually more sceptical of witch accusations and more resistant to the chain-reaction dynamics of mass prosecution than their secular counterparts. The Roman Inquisition, in particular, was characteristically cautious about witchcraft prosecutions, insisting on evidentiary standards that secular courts frequently abandoned and refusing to treat the fantastical elements of sabbath confession — flight through the air, transformation into animals, intercourse with the devil — as physically real events requiring criminal sanction. The Spanish Inquisition, similarly, was considerably less credulous about witch confessions than the secular justice of the same period and produced far fewer executions than the popular image of the Inquisition as the primary vehicle of witch persecution would suggest.

This is not to exonerate ecclesiastical culture from responsibility for the witch hunts — the theological framework that made witch belief possible was thoroughly ecclesiastical, and the specific demonological elaboration that made mass prosecutions conceivable was developed largely by clerical intellectuals — but it does mean that the image of the institutional church systematically pursuing witches through its courts misrepresents the actual institutional mechanisms of the persecution, and that any account that reduces the witch hunts to "the church killing women" requires substantial qualification.

The second important structural finding is the mechanism of chain-reaction prosecution. The most devastating witch panics — episodes in which dozens or hundreds of people were executed in a compressed period — typically developed through the dynamic of tortured confession naming accomplices: an accused witch, subjected to torture or its threat, confessed to attending the sabbath and, required to name fellow participants, named neighbours, relatives, and acquaintances who were then arrested, tortured, and required in turn to name further accomplices. This chain-reaction dynamic, once initiated, could consume entire communities before it was interrupted — either by running out of accusable people, by the resistance of family members of the accused whose social standing made prosecution more costly, or, most effectively, by the intervention of sceptical judicial or administrative authorities who recognised the self-generating character of the process and halted it.

The best-documented cases of judicial scepticism interrupting otherwise devastating prosecutions — the Dutch jurist Johann Weyer's sustained critique of the witch trials in his *De Praestigiis Daemonum* (1563), the intervention of the Jesuit Friedrich Spee in the Rhineland prosecutions of the 1620s and 1630s (described in his *Cautio Criminalis* of 1631, one of the most courageous works of legal criticism produced in the period), and the eventual English scepticism represented by figures like Reginald Scot — illustrate that the persecution was not experienced as self-evidently legitimate by all contemporaries, and that those who recognised its self-amplifying judicial mechanism and challenged it deserve historical credit that the popular image of unanimous witch-burning fanaticism obscures.

## Geography and Confession: Who Persecuted Most, and Why

The geographical distribution of witch prosecutions across early modern Europe shows patterns that challenge any simple confessional explanation of the phenomenon. The regions of most intensive prosecution were not uniformly Catholic or uniformly Protestant but were concentrated in the territories of the Holy Roman Empire — particularly the southwestern German territories, the Swiss Confederation, the Prince-Bishoprics of the Rhineland, and parts of France — regardless of their confessional allegiance. Some of the most devastating local panics occurred in Prince-Bishoprics like Bamberg and Würzburg (Catholic) and in territories like Electoral Saxony (Lutheran); some of the largest single episodes of mass execution were conducted under secular rather than ecclesiastical authority.

The confessional character of witch prosecution was real but more nuanced than the simple Catholic-versus-Protestant framing suggests. Lutheran territories generally prosecuted witches at comparable rates to Catholic ones, since Luther himself believed in witchcraft with considerable fervour and the Lutheran theological tradition offered no principled challenge to the basic reality of diabolic agency in the world. Reformed (Calvinist) territories showed somewhat greater scepticism in some regions, though the Salem witch trials of 1692 — conducted in Puritan Massachusetts, one of the most intensively Reformed communities in the Atlantic world — illustrate that Reformed theology was no guarantee of immunity from witch-prosecution dynamics. The English pattern was relatively restrained by continental European standards — England executed perhaps a thousand people for witchcraft across the entire period, a figure both smaller than the continental panics and larger than some apologist accounts suggest — partly because English common law procedure was less reliant on torture and therefore less prone to the chain-reaction dynamic of tortured confession generating further accusations.

The social profile of the accused, across the European witch trials as a whole, was predominantly female — approximately 75 to 80 percent of those tried were women, a proportion significant enough to require explanation even if it falls far short of the "woman's holocaust" framing of some feminist historiography. The explanations that have been offered are multiple and contentious: the specific misogyny of the *Malleus* and the demonological tradition that identified women as more susceptible to diabolic temptation; the specific social vulnerability of older, widowed, or socially marginal women who had fewer resources to resist accusation or mount a defence; the specific anxieties of communities in which women's roles as healers, midwives, and managers of the domestic economy gave them a form of social power that could be reframed as maleficium when things went wrong; and the specific dynamics of neighbourhood conflict in village communities in which women were more likely than men to be identified as the source of the small-scale harm — sick livestock, spoiled butter, failing crops, infant deaths — that formed the most common substrate of witchcraft accusation. None of these explanations individually accounts for the totality of the pattern, and several need to be held in combination; what can be said with confidence is that the witch hunts were neither simply a war against women as such nor a gender-neutral phenomenon, and that the gendered character of the persecution requires acknowledgement without being elevated into the sole explanatory principle.

## Possession, Exorcism, and the Demonological Precondition

The persecution mechanisms examined in this chapter were made possible by a specific theological framework whose foundations belong properly to the doctrinal tradition examined in Chapter 7 — the tradition of devil-belief and demonology whose development from the patristic period through the mediaeval inflation of the devil's power and independence constituted the conceptual precondition without which the specific witch-hunt demonology of the *Malleus Maleficarum* would have had no purchase on the theological imagination of early modern Europe.

The New Testament's own accounts of demonic possession — Jesus's multiple exorcisms, which are among the most historically secure elements of the gospel tradition (satisfying both multiple attestation and the criterion of embarrassment, since no later apologetic would have invented a miracle-working technique so easily associated with the magical practices of the surrounding culture) — established exorcism as a significant dimension of Christian ministry from the earliest period. The church absorbed this tradition into its institutional life through the development of formal exorcistic rites embedded in the baptismal liturgy — the renunciation of Satan as a standard component of pre-baptismal preparation — and through the minor order of exorcist within the developing clerical hierarchy, whose function was the protection of the community against demonic influence. By the early mediaeval period, formal rites of major exorcism for those believed to be possessed — clearly distinguished in the liturgical manuals from the minor exorcisms of the baptismal rite — had developed into a specialised clerical competence whose practice required episcopal authorisation and whose procedure involved extended prayer, fasting, and the commanded departure of the named demon in Christ's name.

The witch-hunt demonology of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries drew on this tradition while substantially extending and distorting it. Where the earlier exorcism tradition had understood demonic possession as something that happened to victims — an affliction requiring pastoral and liturgical response — the witch-hunt framework introduced the crucial additional element of the voluntary diabolic pact: the witch was not a victim of demonic power but a consenting partner in it, having freely chosen to submit to the devil's authority in exchange for supernatural abilities. This shift from possession-as-affliction to possession-as-contract transformed the theological and juridical character of the situation entirely: where the possessed person was an object of pastoral care, the witch-as-contractor was a criminal agent whose voluntary choice to serve the devil constituted a capital offence against both God and the community. The chain-reaction dynamic of tortured confession examined above depended on this contractual framework — it made sense to ask an accused witch to name her fellow sabbath-attendees because the sabbath was understood as a real, contractual gathering of voluntary devil-worshippers rather than a fantasised collective experience of possession.

The modern Catholic church has retained a formal rite of major exorcism — revised most recently in 1999 — but surrounded it with explicit procedural requirements that reflect the tradition's own internal correction of its early modern excesses: the 1999 Rite of Exorcism explicitly requires that natural and psychiatric explanations be excluded before a formal major exorcism is undertaken, placing the rite firmly within a framework that acknowledges the reality of natural causation for most apparent possession phenomena. The contrast between this cautious modern procedure and the confident demonology of the *Malleus Maleficarum* illustrates how substantially the tradition's own self-understanding of the devil's practical operations has changed across five centuries, even while the theological category of personal demonic agency has been retained.

## The Decline of the Witch Trials

The decline and eventual cessation of witch prosecution across Europe between roughly 1650 and 1780 was gradual, regionally uneven, and motivated by a combination of factors that included but was not limited to Enlightenment scepticism about the supernatural. The earliest and most effective challenges came from legal reformers who recognised the epistemological problems with witch trial procedure — particularly the unreliability of tortured confession and the impossibility of providing adequate evidence for the fantastic crimes alleged — rather than from a principled atheism or a theological revision of belief in the devil's existence. Even jurists who retained orthodox Christian belief in demonic agency could and did argue that the specific judicial standards required for capital conviction were impossible to meet in witchcraft cases, since the alleged crimes were by definition unwitnessable in the normal sense and the primary evidence was therefore confession obtained under torture whose inherent unreliability was increasingly difficult to ignore.

The Enlightenment contributed a more broadly sceptical intellectual environment that made the specific demonological elaborations of sabbath flight, shape-shifting, and demonic congress increasingly implausible to educated opinion, but the decline of witch prosecution preceded the Enlightenment in several regions and was achieved on procedural grounds that did not require any fundamental revision of Christian cosmology. The cessation of witch trials was therefore not straightforwardly a victory of reason over superstition in the Voltairean sense, though that narrative was readily available and widely used; it was a more complex process in which legal reform, scepticism about specific judicial mechanisms, and the changing intellectual environment interacted in different proportions across different regional and confessional contexts.

The victims of the witch trials were real people — predominantly, though not exclusively, women; predominantly, though not exclusively, elderly or socially marginal; predominantly, though not exclusively, accused by neighbours in the context of existing local conflicts — whose deaths for a non-existent crime constitute a genuine historical atrocity even at the corrected scale of forty to sixty thousand. The honest historiographical obligation is to narrate this atrocity with precision rather than inflation, and to understand its mechanisms rather than simply to condemn its perpetrators — not in order to excuse what was done, but because understanding how communities generate self-amplifying persecution dynamics, how judicial procedures can produce their own evidence, and how theological frameworks can make violence against the innocent not merely permissible but mandatory, is knowledge with implications that extend well beyond the early modern period.
