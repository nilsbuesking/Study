# Annex C, part two — Christian Holidays in England, Ireland, and Germany

## The Fixed Feasts: The Christmas Cycle and Other Celebrations

## Advent / Adventszeit

**When:** Beginning on the Sunday nearest 30 November (St Andrew's Day), the fourth Sunday before Christmas. Runs from late November or early December to Christmas Eve.

**Background:** Advent (from Latin *adventus*, "coming" or "arrival") is the season of preparation and anticipation for Christmas, beginning the liturgical new year in the Western church. It is a season of moderate penitential character — liturgical colour purple, reduced festivity, emphasis on waiting and preparation — though considerably less severe than Lent, and in practice the commercial Christmas season has effectively colonised Advent in most Western societies, often inverting its character of restrained preparation into one of premature celebration. The liturgical tradition of Advent preserves a double orientation: preparation for the commemoration of Christ's first coming at Christmas, and anticipation of Christ's final coming at the end of history, with the readings of the Advent Sundays moving from eschatological urgency in the early weeks to the more immediate figures of John the Baptist and the Virgin Mary as Christmas approaches. The Advent wreath — a circular arrangement of greenery with four candles, one lit on each of the four Sundays of Advent — is one of the most universally observed Advent customs across Catholic, Anglican, and Lutheran households in all three countries, originating in German Lutheran practice in the nineteenth century and spreading globally. In Germany, Advent is also the season of the *Adventskalender* (Advent calendar, now commercialised worldwide but originating as a specifically German devotional custom) and the *Weihnachtsmarkt* (Christmas market), the outdoor markets of mulled wine, spiced biscuits, and crafts that are now a defining feature of German winter culture and have been widely imitated across England and Ireland.

## St Nicholas Day / Nikolaustag

**When:** 6 December. Not a bank holiday in any of the three countries, but widely observed in Germany.

**Background:** The feast of St Nicholas, bishop of Myra (died c. 343) and patron of children among many other causes (his entry in Annex A of this overview gives the biographical details), is one of the most widely observed saints' days in Germany, where the evening of 5 December is the occasion for children to leave shoes or boots outside their doors to be filled overnight with sweets and small gifts by the Nikolaus — a figure depicted in the bishop's red vestments with a mitre and staff, accompanied in the Catholic tradition by his sinister companion Knecht Ruprecht (Servant Rupert) or the Krampus, a demonic figure who threatens naughty children with punishment. The custom migrated to the Netherlands as Sinterklaas and thence, via Dutch settlers in America, into the secular Santa Claus tradition whose main gift-giving moment shifted to Christmas Eve or Christmas Day in the Anglo-American context. In England and Ireland, St Nicholas Day is not widely observed as a distinct celebration, the gift-giving tradition having been absorbed into Christmas; in Germany it remains an important precursor event in the Advent season, distinct from the Christmas gift-giving that follows.

## Christmas Eve / Heiliger Abend (Weihnachtsabend)

**When:** 24 December. Not a bank holiday in its own right in England or Ireland, though the preceding period is subject to early closing; an increasingly observed half-day in Germany, where many businesses close early and public life effectively stops by evening.

**Background:** Christmas Eve is, liturgically, the vigil of Christmas — the occasion for the first celebration of the Christmas Mass, which in Catholic and Anglican tradition is held at midnight (the Midnight Mass, *Mitternachtsmesse*, or *Christmette* in German tradition) and which commemorates the birth of Jesus in Bethlehem as narrated in Luke 2 and Matthew 1. The midnight timing reflects the tradition that the birth occurred at midnight, and the Midnight Mass is one of the most widely attended of the year even in communities where Sunday attendance has significantly declined — a pattern observable across all three countries. In Germany, *Heiliger Abend* ("Holy Evening") is the primary focus of the domestic Christmas celebration: the Christmas tree is decorated (or revealed, if decorated in secret), gifts are exchanged in the evening, and the family attends the evening or midnight church service. The Christmas tree (*Weihnachtsbaum*) is itself a German contribution to global Christmas culture, originating in German Protestant households in the sixteenth century and spreading to England via the German-born Prince Albert, consort of Queen Victoria, whose 1848 illustration of his family around the tree is widely credited with popularising the custom in England. In Ireland and England the domestic gift-giving moment is Christmas morning rather than Christmas Eve, reflecting the different cultural emphasis.

## Christmas Day / Weihnachten / Erster Weihnachtstag

**When:** 25 December. A bank holiday in England, Ireland, and Germany.

**Background:** Christmas (from Old English *Cristes Mæsse*, Christ's Mass) commemorates the birth of Jesus Christ, the Incarnation of the eternal Son of God in human flesh — the theological doctrine examined at length in Chapter 5. The date of 25 December was fixed for the Western church by the early fourth century, though the precise process of its selection remains debated: the older theory that it was chosen to Christianise the Roman festival of Sol Invictus (celebrated on 25 December as the date of the winter solstice in the Julian calendar) has been challenged by an alternative theory that it was calculated from 25 March (the traditional date of the Annunciation and the spring equinox) by adding nine months of pregnancy — in either case, the resulting date had become so thoroughly established by the high mediaeval period that its origins were largely forgotten. The Eastern Orthodox churches that follow the Julian calendar celebrate Christmas on 7 January (Julian 25 December, thirteen days behind the Gregorian). The nativity narratives of Luke (the manger, the shepherds, the angels, no room at the inn) and Matthew (the Magi, the star, the flight to Egypt) are the two primary scriptural sources, notable for the significant details each includes that the other omits — they are not fully harmonisable without editorial work, though the crib scene of popular tradition blends both. In England, Christmas Day is the primary gift-giving and family gathering occasion, with Christmas lunch (turkey, a custom originating in the sixteenth century) and the King's Christmas broadcast (a tradition established by George V in 1932) among the most widely observed secular customs. In Germany, *Erster Weihnachtstag* (First Christmas Day) follows the gift-giving of Christmas Eve with a more solemn Christmas Day church attendance and family gathering; *Zweiter Weihnachtstag* (26 December, St Stephen's Day) is also a public holiday.

## St Stephen's Day / Second Day of Christmas / Zweiter Weihnachtstag / Boxing Day

**When:** 26 December. A bank holiday in England (as Boxing Day), Ireland (as St Stephen's Day), and Germany (as Zweiter Weihnachtstag).

**Background:** The day after Christmas is observed across the three countries as a public holiday, though with different names and different primary associations. In the liturgical calendar it is the feast of St Stephen, the first Christian martyr, whose stoning is described in Acts 6–7 and whose death immediately after Christmas places martyrdom's witness in direct juxtaposition with the birth it witnesses to — a liturgical arrangement of considerable theological intentionality. In Ireland, St Stephen's Day preserves the liturgical name and is also associated with the traditional Wren Day (*Lá an Dreoilín*), a custom of symbolic wren-hunting (now with fake or dead wrens, the live practice having ceased) and processions by groups of musicians and mummers known as Wrenboys — one of the more distinctively Irish folk survivals of the Christmas season. In England, Boxing Day takes its name from the tradition of opening the church poor box and distributing its contents on this day, and from the custom of tradespeople and servants receiving a Christmas box (gift or gratuity) from employers and customers. In Germany, Zweiter Weihnachtstag extends the Christmas celebration with continued family gatherings, concerts (the Christmas oratorio repertoire, including Bach's *Weihnachtsoratorium*, is performed extensively across the period from Christmas Eve through 6 January), and a general atmosphere of the twelve-day Christmas season that the older tradition maintained between Christmas and Epiphany.

## Holy Innocents / Feast of the Holy Innocents / Fest der unschuldigen Kinder

**When:** 28 December. Not a bank holiday in any of the three countries; observed liturgically.

**Background:** The feast commemorates the children of Bethlehem killed by Herod's order, as narrated in Matthew 2:16–18, in his attempt to eliminate the infant Jesus by destroying all male children under two years old in the Bethlehem region. The historicity of the massacre is debated by scholars — it is not mentioned by Josephus, who otherwise records Herod's considerable violence — but its theological significance in the Christmas season is considerable: the feast situates innocent suffering and political violence within the Christmas narrative from its very beginning, refusing the sentimentalisation of a Christmas stripped of its darker dimensions. The feast is also known in English tradition as Childermas (Children's Mass), and in several folk traditions the day was associated with bad luck — a survival of its character as a day of mourning rather than festivity within the Christmas octave.

## Epiphany / Dreikönigstag / Heilige Drei Könige / Twelfth Night

**When:** 6 January. A bank holiday in the predominantly Catholic German Länder (Bavaria, Baden-Württemberg, Saxony-Anhalt); not a bank holiday in England or Ireland, though the Church of England observes the feast liturgically and it marks the end of the Twelve Days of Christmas.

**Background:** Epiphany (from Greek *epiphaneia*, "manifestation" or "appearance") commemorates the manifestation of Jesus to the Gentiles, represented in the Western tradition by the visit of the Magi — the "wise men from the East" whose number (three, though not specified in Matthew's text) and royal identity (not specified in the text either, but derived from the gifts and the Psalm 72 royal imagery) have been elaborated in tradition into the Three Kings of the German *Dreikönigstag*. Matthew 2's bare account of Magi (Greek: *magoi*, probably Zoroastrian astrologers from Persia or Mesopotamia) following a star to Bethlehem has generated one of the most richly productive traditions in Christian art, with the gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh interpreted as symbolic of kingship, divinity, and mortality (anticipating the burial) respectively. The Eastern church also associates Epiphany with the baptism of Jesus, and in some traditions the feast encompasses both manifestations. In Germany, *Dreikönigstag* is associated with the custom of *Sternsingen* — children dressed as the Three Kings going door to door singing carols and collecting for charity, leaving the blessing inscription C+M+B (traditionally interpreted as the initials of the legendary names *Caspar, Melchior, Balthasar*, though also as *Christus Mansionem Benedicat*, "Christ bless this house") above doorways in chalk. This custom, organised through the Catholic church's Sternsinger programme, raises significant sums for development aid annually and remains one of the most widely observed folk-religious practices in Catholic Germany. In England, Twelfth Night (the eve of Epiphany) was historically the occasion for the final Christmas celebrations and the removal of Christmas decorations — the tradition that removing decorations before or after Twelfth Night brings bad luck being a survival of the feast's importance as the formal close of the Christmas season.

## Candlemas / Darstellung des Herrn / Mariä Lichtmess

**When:** 2 February, forty days after Christmas (following the Mosaic law's purification period of forty days after the birth of a male child).

**Background:** Candlemas commemorates the Presentation of the infant Jesus in the Temple, as narrated in Luke 2:22–40, when Mary and Joseph bring him to Jerusalem for the rite of purification and dedication prescribed in Mosaic law, and the elderly Simeon takes the child and sings the *Nunc Dimittis* — "Lord, now let your servant depart in peace... for my eyes have seen your salvation... a light for revelation to the Gentiles and the glory of your people Israel." The candle symbolism that gives the feast its English name derives from Simeon's image of Christ as light: the blessing and procession of candles on this day is one of the more ancient liturgical customs in the Western calendar. Candlemas also traditionally marks the end of the Christmas season in the older observance — decorations removed if they were still present after Epiphany, the crib put away. In Germany, *Mariä Lichtmess* ("Mary's Candlemass") has a long history as a quarter-day and the traditional date for the end of winter farm contracts and the hiring of servants — a folk significance entirely separate from its liturgical character. The German proverb *Am Lichtmesstag der Winter enden mag, doch kann er auch noch vierzig Tage dauern* ("At Candlemas winter may end, but it can also last another forty days") reflects the day's character as a meteorological hinge — the same folk belief that underpins the North American Groundhog Day on the same date.

## Assumption of Mary / Mariä Himmelfahrt

**When:** 15 August. A bank holiday in Ireland and the predominantly Catholic German Länder; not a bank holiday in England.

**Background:** The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary — the doctrine, defined as a dogma by Pope Pius XII in 1950, that Mary was taken body and soul into heaven at the end of her earthly life — is celebrated on 15 August as one of the principal Marian feasts of the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox calendars (where it is known as the Dormition). The feast's origins long predate the 1950 dogmatic definition: it was observed in the Eastern church from the sixth century onward and reached the Western calendar no later than the seventh century, becoming one of the most widely celebrated feasts of the mediaeval calendar. In Germany, Mariä Himmelfahrt is associated with the blessing of herbs and wildflowers (*Kräuterweihe*) — bunches of up to seventy-seven different herbs and flowers brought to church for blessing, a custom attested since the mediaeval period and particularly strong in Bavaria and Austria, where the blessed herbs were kept in homes for protection throughout the year. The custom is one of the more picturesque survivals of the mediaeval sanctification of the agricultural and natural year through the liturgical calendar. In Ireland the feast is a holy day of obligation with relatively high Mass attendance. In England it is observed liturgically in Catholic and High Anglican churches but without public holiday status.

## All Saints' Day / Allerheiligen / All Hallows

**When:** 1 November. A bank holiday in Ireland and the predominantly Catholic German Länder; not a bank holiday in England.

**Background:** All Saints' Day (in older English usage, All Hallows — *hallow* being an Old English word for saint) is the feast commemorating all the saints of the Christian church, both those formally canonised and the vast company of holy men and women whose names are known only to God. The feast's origins lie in the early church's practice of commemorating all martyrs on a single day, which Gregory IV extended to a universal feast in 835. It is inseparable from All Souls' Day (2 November, Allerseelen) which follows it — the latter commemorating all the faithful departed, not only the canonised saints. In Catholic countries and communities the two days together constitute the primary occasion for visiting and tending the graves of family members, with cemeteries illuminated by candles and lamps throughout the nights of 1 and 2 November — a practice particularly striking and beautiful in Catholic parts of Germany and throughout Ireland, where the tradition of grave-visiting and candle-lighting remains strong. The evening before All Saints' Day — All Hallows' Eve or Hallowe'en — has pre-Christian roots in the Celtic festival of Samhain, when the boundary between the living and the dead was believed to be permeable, and has been thoroughly secularised in the contemporary Anglo-American culture that has exported its commercial form globally. In Germany, Allerheiligen is a solemn public holiday in the Catholic Länder, with the grave-visiting and candle-lighting tradition particularly prominent.

## All Souls' Day / Allerseelen

**When:** 2 November. Not a bank holiday in England; observed as a holy day in Ireland; not a public holiday in Germany, though following directly after the Allerheiligen holiday.

**Background:** All Souls' Day is the commemoration of all the faithful departed — all Christians who have died and are understood, in Catholic and some Anglican theology, to be undergoing the process of purification in purgatory (the doctrine examined in Chapter 7) before entry into the full vision of God. The feast was instituted by the Benedictine abbey of Cluny in 998 and spread rapidly, reflecting the central importance of prayer for the dead in mediaeval Catholic piety and the doctrine of purgatory that gave such prayer its theological rationale. The Requiem Mass — the Mass for the Dead, whose Latin text (*Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine* — "Grant them eternal rest, O Lord") gave the musical form its name — is the characteristic liturgical observance of the day, and the great Requiem settings of the musical tradition (Mozart, Verdi, Brahms, Fauré, Britten) are performances as likely to be heard in the concert hall as in the church. Protestant traditions, having rejected the doctrine of purgatory at the Reformation, have no direct equivalent, though several Protestant denominations observe a commemoration of the departed in November, and the Church of England's calendar includes a commemoration of the faithful departed on or near 2 November. In Germany the *Totensonntag* (Sunday of the Dead), observed on the last Sunday before Advent in the Lutheran tradition, performs a similar commemorative function to All Souls' Day in the Catholic calendar.

## Christ the King / Christkönigsfest

**When:** The last Sunday before Advent, the final Sunday of the liturgical year. Falls between 20 and 26 November.

**Background:** The feast of Christ the King was established by Pope Pius XI in 1925 as a response to the rising tide of secular nationalism and the erosion of the church's public authority across post-war Europe, asserting Christ's sovereignty over all earthly rulers and powers as the counter-claim to the political ideologies of the age. Its timing at the end of the liturgical year — the Sunday before Advent begins the new cycle — gives it an eschatological character, looking forward to the final, universal establishment of Christ's kingdom at the end of history. The feast is observed in Catholic and many Anglican and Protestant churches across all three countries, though it carries particular weight in Catholic practice. The Lutheran *Totensonntag* (see above) falls on the same Sunday in the German Lutheran calendar, giving the day a different but related end-of-year commemorative character in Protestant tradition.

## A Note on Denominational Variation

The holidays in this annex are those observed across the Catholic and mainstream Protestant (Anglican/Lutheran) traditions that between them account for the great majority of Christian practice in England, Ireland, and Germany. Several significant variations in observance are worth noting. The Reformed (Calvinist) tradition, which has shaped some Scottish and Swiss practice and influenced elements of English Nonconformity, historically observed far fewer special days, regarding the Sunday alone as the divinely instituted holy day and treating the additional feasts of the liturgical calendar as human additions without scriptural mandate — an approach that has softened considerably in most Reformed communities over the past century. Free Church and evangelical communities in England vary considerably in their observance; Baptist, Methodist, and many Pentecostal congregations observe Christmas, Easter, and Pentecost as central celebrations while treating the more specifically Catholic feasts (Candlemas, Corpus Christi, Assumption, All Saints) as outside their tradition. Orthodox Christians in all three countries — a significant community in Germany in particular, through the Greek, Russian, Serbian, and Romanian diaspora communities — observe the same feast cycle but date Easter and Christmas according to the Julian calendar, placing them up to thirteen days later in some years.
