# Chapter 38 — Decolonisation and the New Centre of Gravity: Global South Christianity and the Rise of Pentecostalism

## The Demographic Revolution

One of the most consequential and least adequately appreciated developments in the entire history of Christianity is the radical shift in its demographic centre of gravity that occurred across the twentieth century and has accelerated into the twenty-first. In 1900, approximately eighty percent of the world's Christians lived in Europe and North America; by 2050, on current trajectories, approximately seventy percent will live in Africa, Latin America, and Asia. This is not a marginal statistical variation but a structural transformation of the global church — a reversal of the long association between Christianity and Western civilisation that has implications for the faith's theology, its politics, its social teaching, and its institutional forms that the Western churches, still largely accustomed to regarding themselves as the mainstream from which others deviate, have been slow to absorb.

This demographic shift was driven by two distinct but related processes: the explosive growth of Christianity in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia following the collapse of the colonial framework that had associated Christianity with European power, and the parallel explosion of Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity that has been the most rapidly growing religious movement in the world across the past century. Understanding each of these processes separately, and their interaction, is necessary for grasping the character of contemporary global Christianity.

## African Christianity After Colonialism

The relationship between the collapse of European colonialism and the growth of African Christianity is more complex than the simple narrative of missionary Christianity giving way to indigenous alternatives might suggest. In many parts of sub-Saharan Africa, Christianity grew faster after decolonisation than it had during the colonial period — a counterintuitive pattern that reflects several distinct dynamics. The missionary education systems that colonial Christianity had established produced the leadership of the independence movements themselves: Kwame Nkrumah, Julius Nyerere, Kenneth Kaunda, and many other founders of African independent states had received mission school education, and carried into their political programmes a synthesis of Christian social thought, African cultural values, and socialist political economy that was a distinctly post-colonial and post-missionary product. The association of Christianity with European colonialism was real and damaging, but it coexisted with the recognition that the mission schools had provided the tools — literacy, education, a universal moral vocabulary — that the independence movements had used.

The more significant development for the character of African Christianity was the emergence, from the late nineteenth century but accelerating dramatically in the twentieth, of the African Independent Churches (AICs) — a vast and varied family of Christian communities that had separated from the mission-established churches and developed distinctive forms of African Christianity that drew more fully on African cultural, spiritual, and social resources than the mission churches' European models had permitted. The AICs encompass an enormous range: from the Zionist churches of southern Africa, with their emphasis on healing, prophecy, and Spirit possession within a framework of Christian identity, to the Aladura ("praying people") churches of Nigeria, to the Kimbanguist church of the Congo — founded in the 1920s by Simon Kimbangu, a Baptist catechist whose healing ministry generated a movement of such scale that the Belgian colonial authorities sentenced him to death (commuted to life imprisonment, of which he served thirty years), a martyrdom that gave the movement its founding narrative and its distinctive theology of African suffering and divine vindication.

The AICs represent, in the assessment of scholars including John Mbiti and Andrew Walls, the most authentically African expression of Christianity in the modern period — not a pale imitation of European Christianity or a syncretic compromise with traditional religion, but a genuine translation of the Christian gospel into African cultural idioms and African spiritual concerns that has produced forms of Christian life with their own theological integrity. The African cultural concerns most consistently reflected in AIC Christianity include: the centrality of healing and protection from spiritual forces as dimensions of salvation rather than peripheral concerns; the integration of communal rather than purely individual categories of sin, redemption, and restoration; the importance of ancestors and the ongoing relationship between the living and the dead as a framework for understanding the communion of saints; and a cosmology in which the spiritual and the material worlds interpenetrate continuously rather than being separated by the divide that Western Christianity, shaped by Enlightenment categories, tends to assume. These features have made AIC Christianity simultaneously more culturally embedded in its African contexts and more theologically challenging to the Western categories that have dominated Christian theology since the Reformation.

## Latin American Christianity: Liberation and Pentecost

Latin American Christianity in the twentieth century developed along two sharply divergent trajectories that together constitute the most theologically productive and the most politically consequential story in the hemisphere's Christian history.

The first trajectory was liberation theology — the theological movement that emerged from the Latin American Catholic church in the 1960s and 1970s, associated above all with the Peruvian theologian Gustavo Gutiérrez (whose *Teología de la Liberación*, published in 1971, gave the movement its name and its founding systematic statement), the Brazilian Paulo Freire, the Uruguayan Juan Luis Segundo, and the martyred Archbishop of El Salvador Óscar Romero, whose assassination at the altar in March 1980 — shot by a right-wing death squad while celebrating Mass, on the day after he had publicly called on Salvadoran soldiers to disobey orders to kill their fellow citizens — made him the movement's most powerful symbol. Liberation theology's central methodological move was the "preferential option for the poor" — the claim, grounded in biblical exegesis (the Exodus narrative, the prophetic tradition, the Beatitudes, and the parable of the Last Judgement in Matthew 25) that God's purposes in history were specifically identified with the liberation of the poor and oppressed, and that authentic Christian theology had therefore to begin from the perspective of the poor rather than from the perspective of the culturally dominant — and the related claim that genuine understanding of the gospel required not merely theoretical reflection but *praxis*, participation in the historical struggle for liberation.

Liberation theology provoked intense controversy from two directions simultaneously. The political right, in the specific Latin American context of Cold War anti-communism, associated it with Marxist revolutionary movements whose specific programme it did not straightforwardly endorse but whose social analysis — the structural character of poverty, the class interests embedded in existing social arrangements — it drew on; the resulting association of liberation theology with communist subversion was exploited by the military dictatorships of the 1970s and 1980s to justify the persecution and murder of clergy and lay Catholics associated with the movement, making Oscar Romero one of several hundred clergy killed across the region in this period. The Vatican under John Paul II and Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger at the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith issued two instructions (1984 and 1986) that affirmed the option for the poor as a legitimate theological category while warning against the appropriation of Marxist social analysis and the reduction of salvation to historical-political liberation — criticisms with genuine theological substance, directed however at the movement's most extreme formulations rather than its mainstream expression, and accompanied by administrative measures against specific liberation theologians that the movement experienced as suppression rather than refinement.

The second and numerically far more significant trajectory was Pentecostal growth. The Pentecostal movement — originating in the United States in the early twentieth century, particularly associated with the Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles in 1906, in which William Seymour led a multi-racial congregation in experiences of Spirit baptism characterised by speaking in tongues (*glossolalia*), healing, prophecy, and ecstatic worship — spread to Latin America from an early date and has grown with extraordinary speed across the twentieth century, to the point where Pentecostal and charismatic Christians now constitute the majority of Protestants in most Latin American countries and represent perhaps twenty-five percent of Brazil's total population. The reasons for this growth are multiple and contested — poverty and the social networks of mutual support that Pentecostal communities provide, the accessibility of Pentecostal worship to people without formal education or liturgical training, the emphasis on direct experience of the Holy Spirit rather than mediated institutional religion, the healing ministry's relevance to populations with limited access to formal healthcare, and the specific emotional and communal resources Pentecostalism offers to people experiencing the social disruption of rapid urbanisation — but their combination has produced a religious transformation whose scale puts it among the major sociological events of the twentieth century.

## Pentecostalism as a Global Phenomenon

The Pentecostal and charismatic movements — distinguished broadly by their emphasis on the immediate experience of the Holy Spirit through spiritual gifts including tongues, healing, prophecy, and deliverance from evil spirits — defy simple characterisation as a single theological tradition because of their extraordinary internal diversity and their capacity to take root in radically different cultural contexts while maintaining recognisable family resemblances. In Africa, Pentecostalism has interacted with the AIC tradition's emphasis on healing and spiritual power in ways that produced the continent's most rapidly growing churches. In South Korea, David Yonggi Cho's Yoido Full Gospel Church became the largest single Christian congregation in the world, with an estimated eight hundred thousand members at its peak, and South Korean Christianity as a whole has become one of the world's most energetic missionary-sending communities. In China, the extraordinary growth of Christianity across the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries — estimates of China's Christian population range widely, from perhaps thirty to a hundred million, reflecting both genuine uncertainty and the difficulty of measuring a largely unregistered religious community — has been driven primarily by unregistered house churches with strong charismatic and Pentecostal characteristics rather than by the officially registered Three-Self Patriotic Movement churches that operate within state parameters.

The theological characteristics of Global South Pentecostalism are not identical to those of the North American Pentecostal tradition, and the differences matter. The "prosperity gospel" — the teaching, associated particularly with several North American Pentecostal television evangelists and exported with considerable success to Africa and Latin America, that faith expressed through financial giving to the ministry will produce material prosperity for the giver — represents the most theologically controversial feature of the exported Pentecostal tradition, drawing sharp criticism from mainstream theologians including within the Pentecostal tradition itself for its instrumentalisation of faith and its implicit theology of a God whose blessing is reliably correlated with financial generosity. The persistence of prosperity gospel teaching in significant segments of African and Latin American Pentecostalism reflects the genuine appeal of a theology that takes material poverty seriously as a spiritual and not merely a social problem, however inadequate the specific diagnosis and prescription.

The political diversity of Global South Pentecostalism resists simple categorisation: it has produced both the liberation-theology-adjacent activism of some Brazilian Pentecostal communities and the stridently conservative social politics of others; it has generated African church leaders whose opposition to LGBTQ rights has become a significant factor in Anglican Communion internal conflicts, and others whose primary emphasis is on healing, community building, and social service delivery in contexts where the state provides neither. The attempt by Western progressive Christians to read Global South Christianity simply as a conservative ally of Western Christian conservatism, and the attempt by Western Christian conservatives to cite Global South Christianity as vindicating their own social-political positions, both distort a reality that is considerably more complex, more contextually driven, and less derivable from Western political categories than either reading implies.

## The Church of the East: Final Thread

The recurring Church of the East thread reaches its final beat here, the destination of a story carried forward from Chapters 15, 34, and 37. The community that once sent missionaries from Persia to China, that produced the Xi'an stele and the great school of Nisibis, that Timur's campaigns reduced from a world-spanning church to a mountain remnant, that the Seyfo of 1914–1918 halved in population, and that the Islamic State's 2014–2015 campaign effectively expelled from the Nineveh Plains, now survives primarily in diaspora communities in Sweden, Germany, Australia, and the United States, with a small residual presence in Iraq, Syria, Iran, and India's Saint Thomas Christian communities. The Assyrian Church of the East — distinguished from the Chaldean Catholic Church, the Syriac Orthodox Church, and the various other descendants of the Church of the East's ancient traditions — maintains its patriarchate in Chicago. Its liturgical language remains a form of Aramaic, the language closest to what Jesus himself spoke. That a community reading the gospel in the language of its first proclamation should find itself clinging to existence in the American Midwest, having been driven from the lands its ancestors Christianised in the first centuries of the faith, is a historical irony of extraordinary poignancy and a rebuke to any account of Christian history that tells the story only from its Western end.

## The World Christianity Paradigm

The demographic revolution described in this chapter has generated, within academic theology and missiology, a shift in scholarly framework whose implications are still working themselves out. The "World Christianity" paradigm — associated particularly with scholars including Andrew Walls, Lamin Sanneh, and Philip Jenkins — insists that the Christianity now being practised by the majority of the world's Christians is not a derivative of Western Christianity, a mission-field product awaiting maturation into the forms the Western tradition has established, but a set of genuinely distinct and genuinely Christian traditions whose diversity is not a problem to be managed but a richness to be celebrated as consistent with the gospel's own capacity for cultural translation. Walls's concept of the "translation principle" — the claim that the gospel's essence is translatable into every human culture in a way that the religion of Islam, with its insistence on Arabic as the sacred language, or Judaism, with its ethnically specific covenant structure, is not — provides one framework for understanding why Christianity has proved so culturally adaptable across two millennia, while the specific theological implications of that adaptability for the tradition's own self-understanding remain a major site of contemporary theological work.

What the demographic revolution means for the future of Christianity is a question whose answer belongs, properly speaking, outside the scope of this overview. What this chapter has attempted to establish is that the Christianity of the twenty-first century is not the Christianity of the fifteenth century's European Christendom, not the Christianity of the Reformation's confessional settlements, not even primarily the Christianity of the twentieth century's European and North American establishments whose decline — in church attendance, in cultural authority, in the lived faith of successive generations — has generated the secularisation narrative that dominates European and North American accounts of Christianity's contemporary situation. The majority of the world's Christians are African, Asian, or Latin American; their Christianity is overwhelmingly Pentecostal or charismatic in its experiential character; and their engagement with the theological, ethical, and political questions of the twenty-first century will increasingly shape what Christianity as a global tradition thinks, teaches, and does — whether or not the declining Western churches have yet recognised this fact.
