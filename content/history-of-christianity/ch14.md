# Part IV — Christendom Forms, East and West Diverge (c. 451–1054)

# Chapter 14 — Chalcedon and the Making of the Miaphysite Churches

## The Problem Ephesus Left Unsolved

The Council of Ephesus in 431 had settled, with whatever procedural irregularity, the question of whether Christ was one subject or two: he was one, and any position that amounted in practice to positing two subjects — a divine person and a human person in close but ultimately external association — was ruled out of bounds. What it had not settled, and what the two decades following Ephesus made increasingly and painfully clear, was the question of how exactly the two natures, divine and human, related to one another within that one subject. Cyril of Alexandria's victory at Ephesus had been won under the formula *mia physis* — one nature — and his supporters in the decades after his death in 444 pressed this formula in directions that generated a new and in some ways more fundamental difficulty than the one Ephesus had addressed: if there is genuinely only one nature in Christ after the Incarnation, what has happened to his complete humanity? Has it been absorbed into, overwhelmed by, or transformed into something other than the genuine, complete human nature the soteriological logic of "what is not assumed is not healed" requires?

The figure who brought this latent tension to an explicit crisis was Eutyches, an elderly archimandrite (head of a monastic community) in Constantinople who had been a firm supporter of Cyril's position at and after Ephesus and who, pressed in a synodal hearing in Constantinople in 448 to define his Christological position with precision, declared that he would not confess two natures in Christ after the Incarnation — only one. When asked to clarify whether he affirmed that Christ's humanity was *homoousios* (consubstantial) with ordinary humanity, as the Chalcedonian formula would later require, Eutyches declined, on the grounds that Christ's flesh was not the same as ordinary human flesh in the relevant sense, having been deified by the union with divinity. The position his interlocutors drew from this — rightly or not, since Eutyches was not a sophisticated theologian and his own understanding of his position may have been less precise than the technical vocabulary being pressed upon him — was that Eutyches held the natures to be genuinely fused or mixed in the Incarnation, with the human being absorbed into or transformed by the divine into a single hybrid nature that was neither simply divine nor simply human but something between the two, a position later labelled monophysitism (from Greek *mono*, one, and *physis*, nature) by its opponents, though the term is contested by the traditions that found themselves on the wrong side of Chalcedon's definition, for reasons examined below.

## Leo's Tome and the Western Position

The Eutychian controversy drew in a figure whose contribution to Chalcedon would prove decisive: Leo I, bishop of Rome from 440 to 461, a man of considerable theological ability and even greater institutional self-confidence whose interventions in the affair established, more clearly than any previous episode, both the Roman see's claim to doctrinal authority across the whole church and the specific Christological content around which the Chalcedonian definition would coalesce. Summoned to pronounce on the Eutychian dispute, Leo produced in 449 a detailed theological letter to the patriarch of Constantinople known as the *Tome of Leo* — a document that, in the subsequent tradition, came to be regarded as among the most precise and authoritative pre-conciliar statements of orthodox Christology, and whose influence on Chalcedon's eventual formula was direct and substantial.

Leo's position, expressed in characteristically lucid Latin prose, insisted on the complete integrity and genuine distinctness of both natures in Christ after the Incarnation: each nature retains its own proper character and operations, such that what belongs to the divine nature — omnipotence, eternity, impassibility — remains genuinely divine and is not mixed with or transferred to the human, and what belongs to the human nature — vulnerability, growth, hunger, suffering, death — is genuinely and completely human and is not absorbed into or overwhelmed by the divine. Both natures, Leo insisted, act in a genuine *communio* (communion or fellowship), with each contributing what is proper to it to the one person's single activity: "each form performs in communion with the other what is proper to it, the Word performing what belongs to the Word, and the flesh carrying out what belongs to the flesh" — a formulation that resonated strongly with the Antiochene emphasis on the integrity of both natures while insisting, against any Nestorian reading, that the subject in and through whom both natures act is a single person, not two associated subjects.

Leo's *Tome* arrived in the East too late for the council that Theodosius II convened at Ephesus in August 449 — a gathering that bears the same geographical name as the 431 council but is emphatically not the same event, and which the subsequent tradition, following Leo's own contemptuous designation, has called the *Latrocinium* or Robber Synod. Dioscorus of Alexandria, Cyril's successor and heir to his institutional aggression if not to his theological sophistication, presided over a gathering that rehabilitated Eutyches, deposed his opponents including Flavian of Constantinople (who died shortly afterward from injuries sustained in the proceedings, in circumstances that implicate physical violence by Dioscorus's monastic supporters, though the exact details are contested), refused to read Leo's *Tome*, and produced a definition so emphatically in the *mia physis* direction as to exclude any possibility of reconciliation with the Antiochene position. Leo refused to recognise the council's authority and pressed for a new, genuinely ecumenical gathering; the political circumstances that allowed one to be convened arrived swiftly and from an unexpected direction when Theodosius II died in a riding accident in July 450 and was succeeded by Marcian, a soldier whose wife Pulcheria — Theodosius's sister, a woman of formidable intelligence and genuine theological opinions who had been effectively excluded from power during her brother's reign — now exercised co-imperial authority and shared both Leo's Christological sympathies and his desire for a properly conducted settlement.

## The Council of Chalcedon, 451

The council that met at Chalcedon, across the Bosphorus from Constantinople, in October 451 was the largest and most representative gathering the church had yet assembled, with somewhere between 350 and 600 bishops in attendance — the sources vary, but all agree on a number considerably exceeding any previous council. The imperial and papal presence was felt differently but forcefully: Marcian and Pulcheria attended in person at the opening session, an imperial sponsorship even more visible than Constantine's at Nicaea, while Leo was represented by legates whose instructions were to secure the acceptance of the *Tome* and to avoid reopening questions that Rome considered settled.

The proceedings began with a thorough and damning review of the Robber Synod of 449, which the assembled bishops collectively repudiated — Dioscorus was deposed and exiled — and moved toward the production of a new doctrinal definition through a process that combined genuine theological deliberation with considerable imperial management of the outcome. The key conceptual breakthrough — attributed in the sources to the combined influence of Leo's *Tome* and a careful rereading of Cyril's own more precise letters, particularly his letter to John of Antioch in 433 that had effected a partial earlier reconciliation between the Alexandrian and Antiochene positions — was the recognition that the apparent contradiction between Cyril's *mia physis* formula and the Antiochene insistence on two natures was partly, though not entirely, a product of the same terminological instability flagged in Chapter 13: Cyril, it could be argued, used *physis* where later, more precise usage would employ *hypostasis* (person, concrete individual subsistence), so that his "one *physis*" amounted to what the Chalcedonian formula would express as "one *hypostasis*" — one person, one concrete subject — while the "two natures" the Antiochene tradition insisted on were not two persons or subjects but two complete sets of properties, divine and human, that that one person possessed without confusion or mixture.

The Chalcedonian definition that emerged from this process is worth quoting in substance, since it is the most precisely crafted piece of doctrinal language produced by any of the first four ecumenical councils and was explicitly constructed, as its fourfold negative structure reveals, to rule out specific identified errors from each direction rather than to offer a positive philosophical account of how the union works: the council defined that Christ is "one and the same Son, our Lord Jesus Christ," "complete in Godhead and complete in manhood, truly God and truly man," consubstantial with the Father in divinity and consubstantial with humanity in humanity, "made known in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation" — the first two negatives (*asynchytōs*, *atreptōs*: without confusion, without change) ruling out the Eutychian/monophysite position of a fusion or transformation of the natures, and the last two (*adiairetōs*, *achōristōs*: without division, without separation) ruling out the Nestorian position of two effectively separate subjects — "the difference of the natures being in no way removed because of the union, but rather the property of each nature being preserved, and concurring into one person (*prosōpon*) and one subsistence (*hypostasis*), not parted or divided into two persons."

The formula is, as noted in Chapter 5's introduction to this doctrine, deliberately negative in structure — it tells you what the union is not rather than fully explaining what it is — and this is not theological timidity but appropriate intellectual modesty before a mystery that the council's framers understood their philosophical vocabulary to be insufficient to exhaust. Four negatives is a tighter constraint than any positive definition could have supplied, and the terms were chosen with sufficient precision that the resulting formula could be, and was, accepted by both the heirs of the Antiochene tradition and significant numbers of bishops who had previously supported Cyril's Alexandrian position — while being rejected, decisively and permanently, by others.

## The Miaphysite Rejection and the Permanent Schism

The rejection of Chalcedon by a significant portion of Eastern Christianity — concentrated in Egypt, Syria, Armenia, and Ethiopia — was not, as the older polemical tradition sometimes presented it, straightforwardly a matter of theological error being defeated by orthodox truth, and the communities that rejected Chalcedon have consistently and with considerable theological warrant objected to being described as "monophysites" in the sense that label was intended by their Chalcedonian opponents. The term *monophysite* — one-nature — implies a position on which the human nature is genuinely absorbed into or destroyed by the divine in the union, producing a single nature that is no longer fully human; but the major non-Chalcedonian traditions — the Coptic church of Egypt, the Syriac Orthodox church, the Armenian Apostolic church, and the Ethiopian Orthodox church — consistently maintained, and maintain to this day, that their position is not monophysitism in this sense but what they prefer to call *miaphysitism* (from Greek *mia*, the feminine form of "one" used by Cyril in his formula, rather than *mono*, carrying implications of singularity), affirming one united nature of the incarnate Christ that is fully both divine and fully human without confusion, mixture, or absorption.

The terminological distinction between *mia physis* and *monophysis* captures, in compressed form, a real theological disagreement about whether the Chalcedonian formula's "two natures" language implies a separation between divinity and humanity in Christ that compromises the genuineness and completeness of the union — a concern the non-Chalcedonian traditions regarded as theologically serious rather than merely obstinate, rooted in their reading of Cyril's *mia physis* formula as expressing precisely the kind of genuine, complete, and inseparable union of divine and humanity that the Alexandrian soteriological logic requires. Twentieth-century ecumenical dialogue between Chalcedonian and Oriental Orthodox churches has produced a series of Christological agreements — the most significant between the Roman Catholic Church and several Oriental Orthodox churches, and between the Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox families — explicitly acknowledging that the two traditions, despite their different terminological frameworks, may be affirming substantially the same Christological reality and that the anathemas pronounced at Chalcedon and after may have been based in part on mutual terminological misunderstanding rather than genuine doctrinal incompatibility. These agreements have not yet produced full communion, and some theologians on both sides dispute whether they have correctly identified the depth of remaining differences, but they constitute a significant revision of the older assumption that the non-Chalcedonian churches are simply and straightforwardly in Christological error.

The immediate aftermath of Chalcedon was not peaceful resolution but intensified conflict. In Egypt, the Chalcedonian definition was experienced as an imperial imposition that violated the theological heritage of Cyril and Athanasius, and the attempt to install Chalcedonian patriarchs in Alexandria against the resistance of the Coptic majority produced decades of intermittent violence, exile, and competing episcopal claimants. The emperor Zeno's attempt to find a compromise formula in his *Henotikon* of 482 — which tried to affirm Cyril's authority without explicitly endorsing or condemning Chalcedon — pleased neither side and opened a schism with Rome (the so-called Acacian schism, 484–519) that itself illustrated the increasingly divergent institutional trajectories of the Eastern and Western churches. The Justinianic settlement of the sixth century, which reasserted Chalcedonian orthodoxy with imperial force, definitively fixed the non-Chalcedonian communities outside the imperial church's boundaries, even as Justinian's own theological sympathies inclined toward accommodating his non-Chalcedonian wife Theodora and the theological position she vigorously promoted — a domestic theological negotiation conducted at the highest level of imperial power with consequences for communities scattered from Egypt to the Persian frontier.

The long-term institutional consequence of the Chalcedonian schism was thus the permanent division of Eastern Christianity into distinct families of churches whose different relationships to the 451 definition would structure their separate historical trajectories across the following fifteen centuries — the Chalcedonian churches (Eastern Orthodoxy and, after 1054, Roman Catholicism) on one side, the Oriental Orthodox family (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, and later Eritrean and Malankara) on the other, and, further east still, the Church of the East carrying a tradition shaped by the pre-Ephesine Antiochene theology into Persia and beyond the Roman world's reach entirely — a global diversification of Christianity whose further story Chapter 15 now takes up.
