# Chapter 11 — Donatism, North Africa, and the Politics of Purity

## North Africa and the Church Before the Schism

Roman North Africa — the provinces of Africa Proconsularis, Numidia, and Mauretania, stretching broadly across modern Tunisia, eastern Algeria, and Morocco — was by the early fourth century one of the most densely Christianised regions in the entire Roman world and the most intellectually productive province of the Latin-speaking church. It had given Christianity Tertullian, the first major Latin theological author; Cyprian of Carthage, the third-century bishop whose writings on church unity and the sacraments would prove foundational for Western ecclesiology; and would subsequently produce Augustine of Hippo, arguably the single most influential theologian in the Western tradition. It was also a deeply unequal and socially fractured society in ways that need to be understood as the material context of the schism rather than mere background colour, since the Donatist controversy cannot be honestly read as a purely ecclesiastical dispute about sacramental theology that happened to occur in North Africa, rather than a dispute whose theological content was inextricably entangled with the social, economic, and ethnic tensions of the specific society in which it erupted.

North African society in this period was marked by sharp stratification between a Romanised urban elite — concentrated in the coastal cities, speaking Latin, connected to the wider imperial administrative and commercial networks, and generally identified with the more accommodating strand of North African Christian tradition — and a rural and semi-rural population in the Numidian hinterland, speaking the indigenous Berber languages (Punic survived in some areas into the patristic period), economically marginal or dependent, and in significant numbers both more fervently Christian in its attachment and more socially alienated from the Roman imperial order that the church's urban leadership was embedded in and increasingly accommodating of. This is not a simple mapping — there were poor urban Christians and wealthy rural ones, and the schism's social geography was more complex than any single class-conflict narrative can capture — but the correlation between the social and the ecclesiastical division is real enough that any account that ignores it in favour of a purely theological narrative misrepresents what the controversy actually was.

## The Traditores and the Succession Dispute

The immediate trigger of the Donatist schism was the final, most severe phase of Diocletian's Great Persecution, begun in 303 and discussed in Chapter 3. Among the measures Diocletian's edict demanded was the surrender of Christian scriptures and liturgical books for destruction — an act of capitulation whose perpetrators acquired, in North African Christian usage, the label *traditores*, "those who hand over" (the word from which the English "traitor" derives), a designation that carried the specific charge not merely of weakness under pressure but of a fundamental betrayal of the faith and of the community. The question of what to do with clergy who had surrendered books — and more broadly with all those who had complied with persecution demands in various degrees — was, as Chapter 3 noted in flagging the Decian *lapsi* controversy as a precursor, one the church had faced before, and one on which North African tradition was already sharply divided: Cyprian's mid-third-century resolution of the Decian lapse question had established the principle that the lapsed could be readmitted to communion through a process of penance, but had also emphasised the bishop's indispensable mediating role in that process in ways that created a strong institutional interest in the question of episcopal legitimacy.

The specific crisis that precipitated the schism involved the election of Caecilianus as bishop of Carthage in 311 or 312, conducted in haste and by a small group of bishops, one of whom — Felix of Aptunga, who performed the consecration — was alleged by his opponents to have been a *traditor* during the persecution. A substantial group of North African bishops, led initially by the Numidian primate and drawing support heavily from the Numidian episcopal bench, refused to recognise Caecilianus's consecration as valid on the grounds that a bishop who had committed the act of *traditio* could not validly ordain, and elected a rival bishop of Carthage — first Majorinus, and on his death shortly afterward Donatus, the figure from whom the movement takes its name and who led it with remarkable effectiveness for some four decades. The theological principle underlying this refusal was a specific and rigorously maintained ecclesiology: the sacramental validity of ordination, and by extension of any sacrament administered by an ordained minister, depended on the moral and spiritual worthiness of the minister, such that an ordination performed by a *traditor* was simply no ordination at all, and the bishop so "ordained" no true bishop, regardless of the external form of the rite having been properly observed.

This position — that sacramental efficacy depends on the minister's personal holiness (*ex opere operantis*, from the quality of the one performing the work, to use the later scholastic terminology introduced in Chapter 6, as against the *ex opere operato* principle the wider catholic tradition would uphold) — was not an eccentric novelty in North African Christianity; it had deep roots in the tradition associated with Cyprian himself, who had insisted, against the Roman bishop Stephen, that baptism administered by schismatic or heretical ministers was no true baptism and required repetition on admission to the Catholic church. The Donatists understood themselves as the heirs of this Cyprianic tradition, and their claim that they, not the Caecilianist party, constituted the true church of the martyrs and confessors — the church that had held firm when the *traditores* had surrendered — was a claim with genuine moral force in a community that had elevated the martyr's witness to the faith as the highest expression of Christian identity and that had vivid and recent memories of persecution.

## Constantine and the Failure of Imperial Settlement

The schism's timing was acutely unfortunate for Constantine, who had just emerged from his victory at the Milvian Bridge and was actively cultivating the North African church as part of his broader religious policy. His initial response was to refer the question of Caecilianus's legitimacy to a series of ecclesiastical hearings — first a council at Rome under Pope Miltiades in 313, then a larger council at Arles in 314 — both of which found in favour of Caecilianus and against the Donatist position. The Donatists, refusing to accept these verdicts and appealing repeatedly to Constantine himself, prompted an increasingly frustrated emperor to attempt direct imperial resolution, which he delivered in 316 by condemning the Donatist position and ordering the confiscation of Donatist church properties and the exile of Donatist clergy. This coercive approach proved entirely counterproductive: rather than suppressing the schism, it inflamed it, associating the Caecilianist "Catholic" party with imperial power and persecution in ways that powerfully reinforced the Donatist narrative of themselves as the true church of the martyrs now facing a new wave of persecution, this time from an ostensibly Christian emperor acting through his ecclesiastical allies. Constantine abandoned the coercive approach in 321 and adopted a policy of toleration toward the Donatists, leaving the schism unresolved — it would remain the dominant form of North African Christianity for much of the fourth century and the defining context of Augustine's own episcopal career, as Chapter 12's examination of Augustine will further develop.

## The Circumcellions: Class, Violence, and the Limits of Ecclesiastical Control

The social dimension of the Donatist controversy that most directly challenges any purely theological reading of it is the phenomenon of the Circumcellions (*Circumcelliones*, literally "those who move around the farm buildings"), a movement of itinerant, predominantly rural militants whose relationship to organised Donatism was complex, contested, and deeply embarrassing to Donatist leadership even as it drew on the same reservoirs of social alienation and religious intensity that sustained the wider schism. The Circumcellions appear in the sources — predominantly hostile sources, the Catholic writers who condemned them, which is a limitation that needs to be acknowledged even while recognising that their activities are attested too consistently to be dismissed as pure Catholic fabrication — as bands of wandering ascetics and religious agitators, associated with the cult of Donatist martyrs and the shrines at which they were venerated, who combined extreme ascetic practices (some accounts describe deliberate self-mortification and a cult of martyrdom that extended to seeking death by provoking violent confrontations) with periodic outbursts of violent social action: attacking Catholic clergy, destroying basilicas, liberating slaves, cancelling debt records held by landlords, and compelling creditors to run behind their own vehicles while their debtors rode inside — an inversion of the social order so pointed and specific that it is impossible to read it as the expression of purely religious enthusiasm unconnected to the actual social conditions of the Numidian agricultural economy in which the Circumcellions operated.

The Circumcellions represent one of the more striking early instances of a recurrent pattern in Christian history: the point at which religious grievance and social grievance become effectively indistinguishable in a movement whose participants likely could not themselves have separated them, and whose combination produced a volatility that organisational leadership found simultaneously useful — the Circumcellions constituted an intimidating resource for Donatist communities under Catholic or imperial pressure — and impossible to manage without risking association with violence that even committed Donatist bishops deplored or claimed to deplore. The Donatist leadership's relationship to the Circumcellions oscillated between denunciation and tacit exploitation, a dynamic that tells its own story about the extent to which the schism's theological content was saturated with social pressures that the theological vocabulary only partially articulated.

## Augustine and the Turn to Coercion

The schism endured, in its organised ecclesiastical form, into the early fifth century, by which time Augustine of Hippo — bishop of the North African coastal town of Hippo Regius from 395, examined more fully in Chapter 12 for his theology of grace and his controversy with Pelagius — had become its most formidable Catholic opponent and, ultimately, the intellectual architect of the coercive response that would define the Catholic church's approach to dissent for much of the subsequent millennium. Augustine's engagement with Donatism was prolonged, multi-fronted, and intellectually serious: he wrote extensively against Donatist theology, engaged in formal public debates with Donatist bishops, and developed the most sustained and philosophically rigorous Catholic response to the Donatist ecclesiology, centring on the principle that the church's sacramental efficacy and visible unity were independent of the holiness of its individual members and ministers, since Christ, not the human minister, was the true agent of the sacraments — a principle that both addressed the Donatist theological argument directly and had the practical advantage of making the church as an institution considerably more robust against the unavoidable reality of human ministerial sinfulness.

What makes Augustine's position on Donatism a matter of longer historical consequence than the specific North African controversy is his eventual, and initially reluctant, endorsement of imperial coercion as a legitimate tool for returning schismatics to Catholic unity. Augustine had initially resisted the use of force, preferring persuasion; his change of position, articulated in the aftermath of successful coercive measures in the early 400s that did produce significant numbers of Donatist returns to Catholic communion, represents one of the most consequential and morally troubling intellectual developments in the entire patristic period. His argument, developed most fully in his letters and deployed with considerable rhetorical force, drew on the parable of the banquet in Luke 14, in which the host instructs his servants to "compel them to come in" (*compelle intrare*) — a text that Augustine, against what most modern readers would regard as its obvious meaning as a narrative figure for urgent divine invitation, pressed into service as a scriptural warrant for using state coercion to bring schismatics back to the church, arguing that genuine love for those in error could legitimately extend to the use of force to prevent their damnation, as a physician might forcibly restrain a patient from a self-destructive act. The argument has been rightly criticised as a rationalisation for what was ultimately a capitulation to the pragmatic appeal of a method that worked, at least in the short term and by the crude metric of bodies in Catholic churches; what cannot be denied is that Augustine's intellectual authority gave it a legitimacy and a subsequent influence — in the justification of the Inquisitions, the Albigensian Crusade, and the wide spectrum of later Catholic coercive practice against heresy — vastly disproportionate to the specific and limited circumstances of North African schism in which it was first advanced. That longer history, and the moral reckoning it requires, is taken up at the appropriate points across Parts V and VI of this overview.

The Donatist schism itself was formally ended, after a major conference at Carthage in 411 at which Catholic and Donatist bishops confronted one another before an imperial commissioner who found in the Catholics' favour, by a series of imperial edicts that stripped Donatist communities of legal standing and subjected their clergy to penalties. The organised ecclesiastical structure of Donatism did not long survive these measures in effective form, though Donatist sentiment persisted in some North African communities considerably longer — into the Vandal period and, in attenuated traces, perhaps beyond. The Arab conquests of the seventh century, which extinguished North African Christianity as an institutional presence with a thoroughness unparalleled in any other major Christian region, mean that we cannot know what longer trajectory either the Catholic or the residual Donatist tradition might have followed; North African Christianity, which had been the most theologically fertile province of the Latin church, simply ceased to exist as an organised body, leaving its intellectual legacy — above all Augustine's — to be carried forward by the churches of the European West that had absorbed and transmitted it.
