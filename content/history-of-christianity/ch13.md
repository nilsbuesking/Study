# Chapter 13 — Nestorius, Cyril, and the Christological Wars: The Road to Ephesus

## Two Schools and Their Different Instincts

The Christological controversies of the fifth century — of which the Nestorian dispute and its resolution at Ephesus in 431 constitute the first major episode, with Chalcedon in 451 providing what the wider church judged the definitive settlement, examined in Chapter 14 — were not simply personal quarrels between ambitious bishops, though the personal and the political were thoroughly entangled with the theological throughout. They were also the expression of a genuine and deep divergence in theological instinct between two great schools of Christian thought, the Alexandrian and the Antiochene, whose different starting points in approaching the mystery of Christ generated different emphases that the technical vocabulary available in the early fifth century was not yet adequate to reconcile without significant loss on one side or the other.

The Alexandrian theological tradition, rooted in the great city of Alexandria and the intellectual culture it had sustained from Origen onward through Athanasius and the Cappadocians, approached Christology from above — beginning with the divine Logos, the eternal second person of the Trinity, and asking how this divine subject came to be present in human form. Its characteristic emphasis was on the unity of Christ: the one divine Logos genuinely and fully became human, such that the subject of all Christ's acts, divine and human, is the same divine person — it is the Logos who was born, who suffered, who died, even though divinity as such cannot suffer or die. This "communication of attributes" (*communicatio idiomatum*), by which what is properly true of Christ's human nature can be predicated of his divine person and vice versa, was central to Alexandrian Christology, and it generated the single most explosive trigger of the fifth-century controversy: the title given to the Virgin Mary as *Theotokos* — in Greek, "God-bearer" or "Mother of God" — which expressed, in Alexandrian logic, the straightforward consequence that if the one born of Mary is the divine Logos, then Mary is, in the precise technical sense, the mother of God, not merely the mother of a human being in whom God subsequently dwelt.

The Antiochene theological tradition, centred on Antioch in Syria and associated above all with the exegetical school founded by Diodore of Tarsus and developed by Theodore of Mopsuestia, approached Christology from below — beginning with the full humanity of Jesus of Nazareth and asking how the divine presence related to this complete human being. Its characteristic emphasis was on the completeness and integrity of Christ's human nature: if Christ's humanity was in any way absorbed into, modified by, or rendered incomplete by the union with divinity, then the salvific logic of "what is not assumed is not healed" (Gregory of Nazianzus's formula, encountered in Chapter 5) was compromised — Christ would not have been a genuine human being subject to the same conditions of human experience, temptation, growth, and suffering that the human beings he came to save are subject to, and his solidarity with humanity would be illusory. The Antiochene instinct was therefore to insist on the genuine completeness and integrity of both natures in Christ, to read the Gospel narratives with close attention to the human experiences they record, and to be wary of any formulation that seemed to blur or overwhelm Christ's human nature by collapsing it into the divine.

These two instincts were not, at the level of eventual Chalcedonian definition, simply contradictory — the Chalcedonian formula of Chapter 14 would attempt, with considerable sophistication, to hold both the Alexandrian unity and the Antiochene duality — but in the early fifth century, before that vocabulary had been developed, they generated real and not merely verbal conflict when pushed to their logical endpoints, and the personal and institutional contexts in which they clashed ensured that the conflict, when it came, was as vicious as any in the church's history.

## Nestorius: The Man and the Controversy

Nestorius, a monk from Antioch of considerable reputation as a preacher, was appointed bishop of Constantinople in 428 by the emperor Theodosius II — a choice that elevated an Antiochene theological sensibility into the see that, since the Council of Constantinople in 381, had been formally recognised as holding the second position of honour in the church after Rome, a consequence of Constantinople's status as the imperial capital that Alexandria, the oldest and most prestigious Eastern see, had never fully accepted and deeply resented. The rivalry between Constantinople and Alexandria thus forms an immediate institutional backdrop to everything that followed.

The controversy ignited almost immediately after Nestorius's arrival in Constantinople, when one of his presbyters preached against the title *Theotokos* for Mary, and Nestorius himself then publicly associated himself with this position, insisting that Mary should be called *Anthropotokos* (mother of a man) or, at most, *Christotokos* (mother of Christ), but not *Theotokos*, since — on the reasoning his Antiochene instincts supplied — the divine Logos had no mother, being eternal and uncreated, and attributing birth to God was a category confusion that blurred the distinction between Christ's divine and human natures. The title *Theotokos*, Nestorius argued, led directly toward the error of thinking divinity had been born and had suffered, which was precisely the kind of confusion between the natures that Antiochene Christology existed to prevent.

This position, whatever its theological justification, was immediately experienced by large segments of Constantinople's population not as a careful theological refinement but as an attack on a title and a form of Marian devotion already deeply embedded in popular piety, generating significant lay unrest in the capital. More consequentially, it provided Cyril of Alexandria — who had been bishop of Alexandria since 412 and who had already demonstrated in his earlier career a political ruthlessness and institutional aggression that made him one of the most formidable, and most disturbing, figures in the entire patristic period — with both a genuine theological grievance and a perfect political opportunity.

## Cyril of Alexandria: Theology and Power

Any honest account of the Nestorian controversy requires confronting Cyril with some directness, since the hagiographical tradition has not infrequently elided or softened a record that the contemporary evidence does not support softening. Before the Nestorian controversy reached its climax, Cyril had already presided over the violent expulsion of the Jewish community from Alexandria in 415, an episode in which synagogues were seized and a Jewish population that had been present in the city for centuries was driven out with the participation of a Christian mob, in circumstances that implicate Cyril's authority even if the precise degree of his direct instigation remains debated; and over the murder of Hypatia, the Neoplatonist philosopher and mathematician, by a Christian mob in the same year, torn apart in the street in an act of sectarian violence whose connection to Cyril's immediate authority is again disputed but whose occurrence within his city and his period of dominance he neither condemned nor effectively prevented. These events belong to the longer history of Christian antisemitism and of Christianity's relationship to the classical intellectual tradition, treated at appropriate points later in this overview; they are mentioned here because a reader who encounters Cyril only as a defender of Alexandrian Christological orthodoxy against Nestorian error has an incomplete and misleadingly favourable picture of the man whose institutional and political conduct, no less than his genuine theological seriousness, shaped the outcome at Ephesus.

Cyril's theological objection to Nestorius was, on its own terms, coherent and substantive: if the *Theotokos* title was denied, then the divine Logos and the human Jesus were, in effect, two distinct subjects merely associated with one another — a divine person alongside a human person, connected by will, purpose, and grace but not genuinely unified in a single subject, such that the *communicatio idiomatum* Alexandrian Christology required was impossible, and the claim that it was truly God who was born, suffered, and died for humanity's salvation was, in the last analysis, false. Nestorius's Christ, on Cyril's reading, was not God incarnate but a God-indwelt man — a supremely close and unique divine-human association, but not the genuine personal union of divine and human in a single subject that the soteriological stakes required. Cyril expressed his positive position through the formula *mia physis tou theou logou sesarkōmenē* — "one nature (*physis*) of the incarnate God the Logos" — a formulation he attributed to Athanasius but which is now generally recognised as deriving from Apollinarius (the fourth-century bishop condemned for denying Christ a complete human rational soul, encountered in Chapter 5), a misattribution that would have significant consequences for the reception of Cyril's Christological language in the controversies following Ephesus.

The vocabulary problem at the heart of this controversy deserves emphasis: the Greek terms *physis* (nature) and *hypostasis* (person, or concrete individual subsistence) were used with insufficient precision and consistency across different theological schools in the early fifth century, such that what Cyril meant by asserting "one *physis*" and what Antiochene theologians meant by asserting "two *physeis*" were not straightforwardly contradictory in the way they appeared to be, since the terms themselves were not yet carrying the technical, stabilised meanings they would receive at and after Chalcedon. This genuine terminological confusion was exploited by both sides — sometimes in good faith, sometimes less so — throughout the controversy, and accounting for it is essential to understanding why a dispute that later theological clarification would reveal as partly, though not entirely, verbal proved so intractable and so violent in practice.

## The Council of Ephesus, 431: Procedure as Weapon

Theodosius II convened a council at Ephesus in June 431 to resolve the dispute, with bishops summoned from across the empire. The proceedings that followed constitute one of the more scandalous episodes in the history of conciliar Christianity — not because of the theological outcome, which the subsequent tradition broadly endorsed, but because of the procedural conduct by which it was achieved.

Cyril arrived with a large Egyptian contingent and, crucially, with Celestine I of Rome's prior support for his position already secured — support Cyril had solicited through letters to Rome carefully framing the dispute in terms most likely to alarm a Roman bishop committed to both Nicene orthodoxy and Roman authority. The Syrian bishops supporting Nestorius were delayed in their travel and had not yet arrived when Cyril convened the council's opening session on 22 June 431, despite explicit requests for delay and an instruction from the imperial commissioner to wait. In a single day, without the Syrian contingent present, Cyril's council — with himself presiding — heard the case, condemned Nestorius, and deposed him, with Nestorius and the imperial commissioner both protesting the procedure's illegality in terms that were not obviously wrong as a matter of conciliar process.

When the Syrian bishops arrived days later under their leader John of Antioch, they convened their own counter-council, condemned Cyril in turn, and the result was two simultaneously sitting councils each claiming conciliar authority, each having condemned the other's leading figure, and an imperial court trying to manage the resulting chaos. Theodosius initially confirmed both depositions — of both Nestorius and Cyril — before political pressure, in which Cyril's agents at the court in Constantinople, operating with what the sources describe in terms that modern readers would readily recognise as systematic bribery (lavish gifts of gold, silver, and luxury goods to court officials, chamberlains, and the emperor's own sisters, documented in embarrassing detail in Cyril's own letters), gradually secured a reversal. Nestorius was exiled to the Egyptian desert, where he died around 451, leaving behind an account of his own position in an autobiographical apologia recovered in 1889 and known as the *Book of Heraclides*, which presents his own self-understanding and which has convinced some modern scholars that his actual theological position was considerably closer to Chalcedonian orthodoxy than his opponents' characterisation of it suggested — a possibility that makes the entire episode still more troubling in retrospect. Cyril was restored to Alexandria and entered a period of intense literary production, including the twelve anathemas against Nestorius that constituted his most precise Christological statement, before his death in 444.

## The Theological Residue of Ephesus

What Ephesus settled, and what it left unresolved, requires careful distinction, since the council is sometimes presented as having fully resolved the Christological question when it had in fact left the most technically difficult part of it — precisely how the two complete natures of divinity and humanity coexisted in Christ — untouched. What Ephesus did definitively establish, and what the subsequent tradition accepted as binding, was the *Theotokos* title for Mary, the condemnation of the specific Nestorian position as articulated in the controversy's polemical heat, and the unacceptability of any Christological position that amounted to positing two subjects or persons in Christ rather than one. What it did not settle was the precise relationship between those two natures in the one person — whether they were genuinely and completely distinct or in some way mixed, modified, or fused in the union — and it was precisely this residual question that would generate the second and more theologically complex episode of the Christological wars, the controversy over Eutyches and the eventual Council of Chalcedon in 451, to which the following chapter turns.

The longer institutional consequence of Ephesus was the permanent schism between what would become the Chalcedonian churches and the traditions — the Church of the East (the so-called "Nestorian" church, though the label misrepresents their own theological self-understanding) and to a lesser degree the Oriental Orthodox — that found the Ephesine and subsequent Chalcedonian settlements unacceptable, a schism that would take those traditions eastward, away from the Roman imperial world and into Persia, Central Asia, and eventually China, and southward into Ethiopia and the Egyptian and Syriac heartlands. That story belongs in full to Chapters 14 and 15; what this chapter establishes is that the route to that permanent division ran through a council whose procedure was manipulated by one of the most politically ruthless figures in the patristic church's history, in the service of a Christological position that was theologically serious and in its broad conclusions correct by the later Chalcedonian standard — a combination that defies both simple celebration and simple condemnation, and that exemplifies once again why the theological and the institutional dimensions of these controversies cannot be honestly separated from one another.
