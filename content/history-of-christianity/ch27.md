# Chapter 27 — Luther, the Ninety-Five Theses, and the Theological Break

## The Man and His Formation

Martin Luther (1483–1546) was the son of a Saxon mining entrepreneur who had invested considerable resources in his son's legal education, and who was proportionally dismayed when the young Luther, caught in a thunderstorm near Stotternheim in July 1505 and terrified by a lightning strike, vowed to Saint Anne that if he survived he would become a monk — a vow he kept, entering the Augustinian Eremite monastery at Erfurt within weeks. The biographical detail is not anecdotal: the thunderstorm vow, the entry into the most intellectually serious of the German Augustinian communities, and the subsequent years of monastic formation within a tradition whose foundational theological texts were Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings, provided Luther with both the existential crisis and the intellectual resources from which his theological breakthrough would eventually come.

Luther's personal struggle in the monastery — the *Anfechtungen*, the spiritual assaults of doubt, self-accusation, and the terror of a just God whose demands the sinful human being could never satisfy, that he describes with unusual psychological candour in his later writings — was not, as some psychological reductions of the Reformation have proposed, merely a personality disorder requiring therapeutic rather than theological resolution. It was, rather, the existential experience of taking the late mediaeval system of penance, contrition, and merit-accumulation entirely seriously and finding it insufficient: the harder Luther worked at the prescribed spiritual disciplines, the more acutely he experienced the gap between what God's justice demanded and what a fallen human will could supply. His confessor and superior, Johann von Staupitz, directed him toward the Augustinian theology of grace and toward an academic career as a biblical theologian — a direction that proved, against Staupitz's own expectations, to lead Luther not back into the existing system but through it and out the other side.

Luther completed his doctorate in theology at Wittenberg in 1512 and began lecturing on the biblical texts — the Psalms, then Paul's letters to the Romans and Galatians — that would be the immediate sites of his theological breakthrough. The precise dating of the "tower experience" (*Turmerlebnis*) in which he describes having suddenly understood the phrase "the righteousness of God" in Romans 1:17 — a phrase he had previously experienced as a threatening expression of God's punitive justice, and which he suddenly understood as describing the gift of righteousness that God freely bestows on the sinner through faith rather than the standard by which God judges and condemns — has been extensively debated by scholars, with dates ranging from 1513 to 1519 proposed, and the account Luther gives in a late autobiographical preface written in 1545, looking back over thirty years, needs to be read as a retrospective theological construction as much as a literal historical record. What is beyond dispute is that by the time of the indulgence controversy in 1517, Luther's theology had already undergone a substantial transformation from the late mediaeval Augustinian framework he had been trained in, even if the full implications of that transformation only became clear to him — and to his opponents — across the following years of controversy.

## The Indulgence Controversy and the Ninety-Five Theses

The immediate trigger of the Reformation was neither Luther's most fundamental theological discovery nor his most theologically radical position, but the specific and local issue of indulgence preaching — a trigger that makes full sense only against the background of Chapter 7's explanation of purgatory and indulgence theology and Chapter 24's identification of indulgence preaching as the specific proximate cause of the Reformation's opening rupture.

The specific indulgence campaign that provoked Luther was the preaching of Johann Tetzel, a Dominican friar operating in the territories adjacent to Electoral Saxony (Elector Frederick the Wise had prohibited the indulgence in Saxony itself, partly from territorial rivalry with the Archbishop of Mainz who was the campaign's primary beneficiary) on behalf of a combined papal-episcopal enterprise: Pope Leo X. needed funds for the rebuilding of Saint Peter's Basilica in Rome, and Archbishop Albert of Brandenburg needed to recover the enormous sum he had borrowed from the Fugger banking house to pay for the multiple benefices he illegally held simultaneously. The arrangement — in which the indulgence proceeds were split between papal building funds and episcopal debt repayment, with the Fuggers present at the collection points to receive their share directly — was not a secret, and its financial character was transparent enough to generate the resentment that Luther's challenge would crystallise.

The Ninety-Five Theses that Luther reportedly posted on the door of the Wittenberg Castle Church on 31 October 1517 — the posting itself may be a later legendary elaboration; what is certain is that he sent the theses to Archbishop Albert and to his own bishop on that date — were not, as their subsequent fame might suggest, a comprehensive statement of Lutheran theology. They were a series of propositions for academic disputation, in the established scholastic format, focused primarily on the specific theology of indulgences: questioning whether the pope had the authority claimed, challenging the popular understanding of indulgences as guarantees of release from purgatory, and raising the pastoral question of whether encouraging the purchase of indulgences was compatible with genuine Christian contrition. Several theses expressed loyal, even deferential, attitudes toward the papacy itself, and Luther's covering letter to Archbishop Albert, while firm on the theological substance, was not the tone of a man who had decided to break with Rome.

What transformed a set of academic propositions into the opening shot of the Reformation was primarily the printing press — the mechanism identified in Chapter 26 as the structural change that distinguished Luther's context from Wycliffe's and Hus's. Within weeks, the theses had been printed without Luther's authorisation and were circulating across the German-speaking world; within months, Luther's subsequent writings and the controversy they generated had created a public debate operating at a speed and scale the ecclesiastical machinery had no precedent for managing. The institutional church's response — the papal theological commission's initial assessment, the attempted resolution through the Augustinian order's own channels, the intervention of the papal legate Cajetan at the Augsburg meeting of 1518, and finally the Leipzig Debate of 1519 at which Johann Eck drew Luther into explicitly defending Hus and denying the authority of councils — was a sequence of escalations that each pushed Luther further from the possibility of institutional resolution while simultaneously raising the political stakes for the princes who would have to implement any coercive response.

## The Theological Core: Justification by Faith Alone

The theological substance of Luther's breakthrough needs careful explanation, because "justification by faith alone" — the phrase in which the Reformation's central insight is conventionally summarised — carries genuine philosophical and theological content that is easy to flatten into a simple anti-works-righteousness slogan without capturing what Luther actually discovered and why it was experienced, by himself and by many of his readers, as a liberation of extraordinary force.

Justification, in the Pauline and Augustinian theological tradition Luther was working within, refers to the act by which God declares a sinner righteous — the divine verdict that stands at the beginning of salvation rather than its end, the forensic or declaratory act by which the sinner is received into a right relationship with God. The mediaeval Western synthesis, as examined in Chapter 23, had developed an elaborate framework in which justification involved a real interior transformation of the soul through the infusion of grace, in which the justified sinner then cooperated with that grace through meritorious acts, and in which the entire penitential system — confession, contrition, satisfaction, the accumulation of merit through good works and sacramental participation — provided the practical machinery through which the Christian moved, gradually and cumulatively, toward the final justification of the Last Judgement.

Luther's reading of Paul, particularly the letter to the Romans and the letter to the Galatians, convinced him that this entire framework was a fundamental misreading of what Paul was actually teaching — that Paul's insistence on justification by faith rather than by "works of the law" was not a refinement within the existing system but a direct challenge to the underlying logic of merit-accumulation on which it depended. The righteousness by which a sinner stands justified before God is not, on Luther's reading, an interior quality that the sinner gradually acquires through the transformative action of grace and then presents before God's tribunal; it is an *alien* righteousness — the righteousness of Christ himself, which God imputes (credits, reckons, declares) to the believing sinner through faith, not as a reward for anything the sinner has done or become but as a sheer act of divine grace received through trust. The Latin formula *simul iustus et peccator* — simultaneously righteous and a sinner — captures the paradox: the believer is genuinely righteous before God, declared so by divine verdict, while remaining genuinely sinful in their actual moral and spiritual condition, with no pretence that the divine declaration corresponds to any achieved interior transformation that makes the sinner intrinsically deserving.

The existential liberation this discovery represented for Luther, given the specific character of his earlier spiritual crisis, is not difficult to understand: the terrifying question of whether one had done enough, confessed with sufficient contrition, accumulated sufficient merit to satisfy God's justice, was dissolved by the recognition that the answer to that question was, taken in itself, always negative — one could never do enough, confess enough, or merit enough — but that this was not the question God was asking. God's question, on Luther's reading of Paul, was not "have you done enough?" but "do you trust in Christ?" — a question whose answer was not a matter of anxious self-scrutiny and moral inventory but of the simple, receiving act of faith that Luther came to call *fiducia*, personal trust rather than merely intellectual assent to doctrinal propositions.

The implications of this theological position for the entire institutional structure of the late mediaeval church were enormous, even if they took time to unfold fully. If justification was by faith alone rather than by faith operating through the institutional sacramental system, then the entire apparatus of penance, indulgences, purgatory, masses for the dead, and the clerical monopoly on the channels of grace was not merely corrupt in its specific abuses but was based on a fundamental theological error — was, in effect, an elaborate mechanism for generating spiritual anxiety in order to profit from its relief, rather than proclaiming the genuine gospel of God's free grace received through faith. The connection between the theological insight and the assault on the church's institutional and fiscal interests was not accidental: the logic of justification by faith alone, followed consistently, dissolved the theological foundations on which those interests rested.

## Sola Scriptura and the Bondage of the Will

Two further theological principles completed the Lutheran programme. The first was *sola scriptura* — scripture alone as the supreme and self-sufficient norm of Christian faith and life, against the Catholic position that scripture and tradition together constituted the church's authoritative foundation and that the church's magisterium was the authoritative interpreter of both. Luther's appeal to scripture against papal and conciliar authority — pressed most dramatically at the Diet of Worms in April 1521, where he refused to recant unless convinced by scripture or clear reason and declared that his conscience was captive to the Word of God — had both a positive and a negative dimension: positively, the conviction that scripture was sufficiently clear in its essential content that any literate believer could read it and grasp the gospel it proclaimed; negatively, the rejection of any human authority — papal, conciliar, or patristic — as capable of overriding scripture's plain sense. The programme of biblical translation that Luther undertook during his confinement at the Wartburg in 1521–1522 — his German New Testament, completed in eleven weeks with a linguistic precision and vernacular vitality that made it one of the foundational texts of modern German literature, was followed by the complete Bible in 1534 — was the practical expression of this principle: a scripture in the language of the people, available for individual reading and interpretation, was the institutional consequence of *sola scriptura* as a theological commitment.

The second principle was the bondage of the will — Luther's insistence, worked out most fully in his *De Servo Arbitrio* ("On the Bondage of the Will," 1525), written in direct response to Erasmus's defence of free will in *De Libero Arbitrio*, that the fallen human will lacked any capacity to choose or incline toward God and salvation without the prior, unmerited, and irresistible action of divine grace. This position, drawing directly on Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings and going beyond even the moderate Augustinianism of the Council of Orange (529), had implications that Luther himself regarded as among the most important and most uncomfortable he had worked out: if the will was bound and grace was irresistible, then the question of who was saved and who was not was determined entirely by God's free election rather than by any human response — a doctrine of predestination that Luther endorsed in its strongest form in the *De Servo Arbitrio* and that Calvin would systematise, with more explicit rigour and less existential discomfort than Luther brought to it, in the following generation. Luther himself later pulled back somewhat from the most extreme formulations of the *De Servo Arbitrio*, emphasising the gospel proclamation over the metaphysics of divine sovereignty; Calvin did not pull back, and the differences between Lutheran and Reformed theology on this point, examined in Chapter 29, proved lasting and institutionally consequential.

## The Diet of Worms and the Point of No Return

The Diet of Worms of April 1521 was the moment at which the Reformation crossed from theological controversy into permanent institutional rupture, and its precise sequence of events and Luther's precise words at its climax deserve attention, since both have been subject to mythologisation in the subsequent Protestant tradition that needs calibrating against the historical record.

The emperor Charles V, presiding over the Diet and obligated by the promises that had secured Luther's safe conduct to hear him before acting, confronted Luther with a simple demand: would he recant his writings? Luther's response — which in the most famous version includes the phrase "here I stand, I can do no other," words almost certainly not spoken at the Diet itself but added in the printed versions that circulated immediately afterward, though the theological substance they express is entirely consistent with what Luther did say — was a refusal to recant unless convinced by scripture or evident reason, since popes and councils had erred and could err again, and his conscience was captive to the Word of God. The Edict of Worms, issued by Charles V after Luther had departed under the safe conduct he had been promised, declared Luther a heretic and an outlaw, subject to arrest and delivery to the imperial authorities.

The Edict was not enforced. Elector Frederick the Wise of Saxony had Luther abducted — by arrangement — on the road from Worms and confined in the Wartburg castle, where he was safe from imperial jurisdiction while the Elector maintained plausible deniability about his whereabouts. The political protection that Chapter 26 identified as the structural difference between Luther's situation and that of his predecessors was immediately and decisively operative: the Edict of Worms was the end of any possibility of Luther's being tried, condemned, and burned as Hus had been, and the beginning of the period in which the Reformation established itself in a critical mass of German territories from which it could not be dislodged.

## Luther and the Jews: The Threading Commitment

Luther's relationship to Jews and Judaism requires explicit treatment here, picking up the thread planted in Chapter 24. His relationship to Jews and Judaism underwent the trajectory described there in outline, which the present chapter fills in with more specificity.

His early writing *Daß Jesus Christus ein geborener Jude sei* ("That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew," 1523) expressed, in the context of his early evangelical confidence, the hope that Jews who had rejected the "papal paganism" of the old church would now be open to genuine Christian proclamation — a position whose apparent generosity was, as Chapter 24 noted, instrumental and conditional rather than representing any respect for Judaism on its own terms or any revision of the supersessionist framework Luther had inherited. When the expected conversions did not materialise, Luther's writing shifted across the following two decades toward a hostility that surpassed most mediaeval precedent in its specific practical recommendations.

His late work *Von den Juden und ihren Lügen* ("On the Jews and Their Lies," 1543), written three years before his death and in an atmosphere of general embitterment that also produced extreme attacks on the papacy and on fellow Protestants who disagreed with him on the Eucharist, recommended a programme of violence against Jewish communities that reads, in the light of subsequent history, with unbearable clarity: synagogues to be burned, Jewish homes destroyed, prayer books and Talmudic writings confiscated, rabbis forbidden to teach on pain of death, safe conduct on roads removed, money confiscated, and the Jews set to forced agricultural labour — and if none of this was effective, expulsion. The work was explicit enough to embarrass some of Luther's own contemporaries: Andreas Osiander, Philip Melanchthon's associate, distanced himself from it, and several Protestant princes declined to act on its recommendations. But it circulated, was cited, was reprinted in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries by German nationalist and eventually National Socialist publishers who found in it precisely the theological legitimation for anti-Jewish violence they were seeking, and was quoted approvingly by Julius Streicher at the Nuremberg trials in partial defence of his own role in Nazi antisemitic propaganda.

Luther's late antisemitism cannot be quarantined from his theology as an unfortunate personal failing unrelated to his reforming programme. The connection is structural: the same supersessionist reading of Paul and the Hebrew scriptures that produced his theology of justification — in which the Jewish law represented the old covenant superseded by the gospel, and Jewish adherence to that law represented the paradigm of the works-righteousness the gospel abolished — generated, when the Jews he encountered declined to validate this reading by converting, the rage of a man whose theological framework had no conceptual space for a Judaism that might be legitimate on its own terms and not merely as a Christological prefiguration awaiting fulfilment. The theological and the antisemitic were not separable in Luther; the church traditions that inherited and developed his theology were therefore also, with whatever modifications, inheriting a framework within which the relationship to Judaism remained theologically problematic in ways that the formal repudiation of Luther's antisemitism cannot entirely resolve without also engaging with the supersessionist theological structure from which it grew.

The full reckoning with this history — including the German Protestant church's conduct under National Socialism — belongs to Chapter 37. What this chapter establishes is that the trajectory from the hopeful early Luther to the murderous late Luther is not an anomaly but a structure, rooted in the same theological soil that produced everything else that made Luther consequential.
