# Chapter 21 — The Crusades: Origins, Aims, and the Sack of Constantinople

## What Were the Crusades?

The Crusades resist simple definition, and the definitional question is not merely academic — different answers to it produce substantially different historical narratives, moral assessments, and understandings of what the movement's significance actually was. In the narrowest sense, a crusade was a papally authorised military expedition to the Holy Land, conducted by warriors who took a vow, received the cross as a visible sign of their commitment, and were granted an indulgence — remission of the temporal penalties of sin — in return for their participation. In a broader sense, the term has been extended by historians to cover a wider range of papally authorised military enterprises conducted against various enemies of the church: Muslims in the Iberian peninsula and North Africa (the *Reconquista*), pagan peoples in the Baltic (the Northern Crusades), political opponents of the papacy in Italy, and heretics within Christendom itself (the Albigensian Crusade, examined in Chapter 22). How widely one draws the definitional net affects the overall assessment of the movement considerably, and scholars disagree about where the boundaries properly lie. This chapter concentrates primarily on the crusades to the Holy Land — eight major expeditions conventionally numbered across the period 1095–1291 — while noting the movement's broader dimensions where they illuminate its essential character.

The Crusades also resist simple moral verdict, and this too needs stating at the outset. The movement has been both romanticised — in the nineteenth-century medieval revival that produced an entire iconographic tradition of chivalric crusading heroism entirely remote from the actual conditions of crusading warfare — and, in reaction, subjected to a blanket condemnation that, while understandable as a corrective, risks a different distortion, projecting modern categories of colonial violence and religious coercion onto actors operating within a medieval conceptual world whose internal logic, however morally objectionable in its consequences, cannot be adequately understood if simply reduced to cynicism or barbarism. The position taken here is that the participants believed what they argued — the theological framework of holy war, crusading indulgence, and the defence of the holy places was genuinely motivating rather than a mere cover for material interests — and that the material interests were simultaneously real, varied across different participants, and in some cases decisive in shaping outcomes that the theological framework alone cannot explain. Holding both of these simultaneously is the necessary condition of an honest account.

## The Background: Holy War, Pilgrimage, and the Devotional World

The Crusades did not emerge from nothing in 1095. Several distinct strands of development across the preceding century and a half converged to make Pope Urban II's appeal at Clermont possible and effective, and understanding those strands is necessary for understanding why the appeal resonated as it did.

The first was the theological development of the concept of holy war — fighting not merely in a just cause by secular standards but at God's direct command, in the service of his purposes, with spiritual merit attaching to the fighting itself rather than merely the cause it served. The earlier Christian tradition had largely followed Augustine's framework of the *bellum iustum* (just war), in which war might be morally permissible under specified conditions but carried no spiritual merit and was permitted only as a regrettable necessity, never as a positive good. The shift from this Augustinian framework toward a theology in which fighting for God's cause was itself meritorious — in which the miles Christi (soldier of Christ) metaphor, previously applied to the monk's spiritual combat, was applied to the literal fighter — was a gradual development of the ninth, tenth, and eleventh centuries, driven partly by the church's need to accommodate and redirect the endemic violence of an aristocratic warrior culture it could not simply prohibit, and partly by the specific circumstances of Christian military engagement with Islam in Spain and southern Italy. By the time of Urban II's pontificate, the conceptual ingredients of crusade theology were largely in place: it remained for a specific occasion to crystallise them into a coherent call to action.

The second strand was pilgrimage — the practice, already ancient and deeply embedded in Christian devotional life by the eleventh century, of travelling to holy sites to venerate the relics and sacred geography associated with Christ, the Virgin, and the saints, and receiving the spiritual benefit the physical presence at such sites was understood to convey. Jerusalem, the site of the Crucifixion, Resurrection, and Ascension, occupied a unique position in this pilgrimage geography as the *caput mundi* of Christian sacred space — not merely a very important shrine but the place where the central events of salvation history had occurred and where, in the eschatological expectation that ran through much of mediaeval Christian thought, the final drama of history would ultimately be played out. Pilgrimage to Jerusalem had been possible, if arduous, under the Arab caliphates that had controlled the city since the seventh century, with Christian access generally tolerated under conditions that varied by regime and period. The disruption of this access, real or perceived, by the Seljuk Turks following their victory at Manzikert in 1071 and their subsequent control of much of Anatolia and Syria was one of the immediate provocations that the Byzantine emperor Alexius I Komnenos cited when he appealed to the West for military assistance in 1095 — an appeal that Urban II transformed, with considerable rhetorical skill, into something Alexius had not requested and would not always find welcome: a full-scale armed pilgrimage whose ultimate objective was not merely Byzantine territorial recovery but the liberation and permanent Christian control of Jerusalem.

The third strand was the cult of saints examined in Chapter 3 and its specifically Jerusalemite dimension: the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, enclosing the traditional sites of Calvary and the empty tomb, was the supreme shrine of Christendom, and the emotional and devotional investment of Western Christians in the holy places — kept vivid by the constant circulation of pilgrims' accounts, of relics, and of the liturgical commemoration of the holy city — gave Urban's appeal an imaginative and affective resonance that extended well beyond the warrior aristocracy he was primarily addressing. The popular, non-knightly response to the crusade call — the so-called People's Crusade of Peter the Hermit, which preceded the main expedition and ended catastrophically in Anatolia — illustrated both the breadth and the uncontrollable character of this popular religious enthusiasm.

## Urban II at Clermont, 1095: The Appeal and Its Ambiguities

Pope Urban II. delivered his appeal for a crusade at the Council of Clermont in November 1095, addressing an audience of clergy and laity in a speech of which no verbatim record survives — the several accounts that do survive were written after the event by authors who knew how it had ended and whose reconstructions reflect that knowledge — but whose essential content is recoverable from the convergent testimony of multiple sources and whose immediate effect — an enormous, apparently spontaneous popular response, with crowds shouting *Deus lo volt* ("God wills it") and pressing forward to receive the cross — is not seriously in doubt.

The appeal Urban made was simultaneously a spiritual summons and a political act of the first order, and disentangling its several strands is important for understanding what kind of event Clermont actually was. Spiritually, Urban offered a remarkable theological package: the crusade was presented as an act of penance — the warrior's violence, habitually a source of sin requiring priestly absolution, was here transformed into the means of penance itself, with the suffering and hardship of the journey and campaign substituting for the traditional penitential disciplines of fasting, prayer, and almsgiving. The indulgence Urban offered was not, in its original formulation, quite the developed system of quantified remission of purgatorial punishment that later mediaeval indulgence theology would elaborate (and that Chapter 7's discussion of purgatory has already prepared the reader to understand), but a remission of the penances enjoined for sins already confessed — a distinction that subsequent popular understanding largely collapsed, with significant consequences for the indulgence theology that would trigger the Reformation four centuries later.

Politically, Urban's appeal served the Gregorian reform programme in several respects simultaneously: it projected papal authority into a new domain by making the pope the authoriser and director of a major military enterprise; it offered the violent aristocratic culture of the Latin West a channel for its aggression that redirected it outward rather than against the church's own interests and properties; and it expressed and reinforced the claim, central to the Gregorian papacy, that the pope's authority extended to the direction of Christian society as a whole, including its military dimensions, in a way that the emperors and kings who had traditionally claimed that role were now being invited to acknowledge. That these political dimensions were real and consciously pursued does not reduce Urban's appeal to cynical calculation — he appears to have believed sincerely in the crusade's spiritual value and the justice of its cause — but they need to be held alongside the spiritual framework rather than subordinated to it.

## The First Crusade and Jerusalem's Fall: 1096–1099

The First Crusade, departing in several waves from 1096 and converging on Constantinople before passing through Anatolia and the Levant, achieved its stated objective with a speed and completeness that surprised participants and observers on all sides: Jerusalem fell to the crusading forces in July 1099 after a siege of five weeks. The account of what followed belongs to the catalogue of atrocities that an honest narrative must integrate rather than soften: the crusaders' entry into the city was accompanied by a massacre of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants of extraordinary scale and savagery, described in terms of blood running to the horses' bridles in the streets by the crusaders' own chroniclers, who reported it not as a scandal but as a sign of divine favour, the city purged of its pollution by the sword. The Jewish community of Jerusalem, who had taken refuge in their synagogue, were burned alive inside it. These events are attested in multiple crusader sources and cannot be minimised as hostile propaganda; they represent the opening act of Christian rule in Jerusalem, and the theological framework within which the participants understood them — as righteous violence in God's service against the enemies of his holy city — is itself part of the historical record.

The crusader states established in the aftermath — the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Tripoli, the Principality of Antioch, the County of Edessa — constituted a genuinely novel political experiment: Latin Christian polities in a predominantly Muslim and Orthodox Christian environment, sustained by a constant trickle of Western settlers and pilgrims and by the military orders that the crusading movement generated, most importantly the Knights Hospitaller and the Knights Templar, whose institutional combination of monasticism and warfare represented a new synthesis of the two vocations that Bernard of Clairvaux — the most influential churchman of the twelfth century, whose presence in this chapter is overdue — provided with its most important theological justification.

## Bernard of Clairvaux: The Conscience of the Twelfth Century

Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153) was, in terms of personal influence on the religious life of his century, arguably the most important single figure in twelfth-century Western Christianity, and his appearance here in the crusading context captures only one dimension of a career that ranged across monastic reform, mystical theology, ecclesiastical politics, the condemnation of heresy, and the development of Marian devotion with an energy and authority that no contemporary could match and few have approached since. He entered the Cistercian monastery of Cîteaux in 1112 — a reformed Benedictine community founded in 1098 in explicit reaction against Cluniac elaboration, returning to a stricter, more austere interpretation of Benedict's Rule, with plain churches stripped of ornament, manual labour restored as a genuine rather than nominal monastic obligation, and a constitutionalism that gave the network of Cistercian daughter houses a regulated relationship to the mother house at Cîteaux far more systematic than the Cluniac model — and founded the daughter house of Clairvaux in Burgundy in 1115, which under his abbacy became one of the most influential monasteries in Europe and the seedbed of a Cistercian expansion that would produce over three hundred houses by his death.

Bernard's mystical theology, developed in his sermons on the Song of Songs and in shorter treatises, represented the most articulate expression in the twelfth century of what might be called the affective tradition in Western spirituality — an approach to the knowledge of God through love, desire, and the transformation of the will rather than through the rational analysis and systematic argumentation that scholastic theology, then developing in the cathedral schools that would become the universities, was beginning to deploy. His famous confrontation with Peter Abelard — the brilliant, provocative master whose application of dialectical method to theological questions Bernard regarded as dangerously rationalist and whose specific theological positions on the Trinity and on atonement he subjected to a campaign of ecclesiastical condemnation that secured Abelard's condemnation at the Council of Sens in 1140 — represented a genuine clash of intellectual visions for what Christian theology was and should be, not merely a personal quarrel: Bernard's victory at Sens was a significant, if not permanent, check on the direction of early scholastic theology, even as the scholastic programme that Abelard represented ultimately prevailed in the following century.

Bernard's justification of the Templars, in his tract *De laude novae militiae* ("In Praise of the New Knighthood"), developed the theological argument for the warrior-monk with characteristic rhetorical force: the Templar who kills in God's service is not a homicide but a *malicide*, a killer of evil, and his death in battle is a martyrdom that wins eternal life — a theological argument that squared the circle of Christian pacifist instincts and crusading violence with a logic whose internal coherence was matched only by the violence of its practical implications. It was the same Bernard who preached the Second Crusade with consuming personal energy in 1146–1147, travelling through France and Germany and generating enormous popular enthusiasm for a campaign whose catastrophic failure before Damascus in 1148 left him personally devastated and theologically perplexed — his attempted explanation of the defeat as divine punishment for crusader sins was less convincing than his original preaching had been persuasive, and the Second Crusade's failure cast a shadow over crusading theology from which it never fully recovered.

## Saladin, the Loss of Jerusalem, and the Later Crusades

The recapture of Jerusalem by Saladin in 1187 — following his decisive defeat of the crusader armies at the Battle of the Horns of Hattin, which effectively destroyed the military capacity of the Kingdom of Jerusalem at a stroke — triggered the Third Crusade (1189–1192), the most famous and most effectively conducted of all the crusading expeditions, led by the Emperor Frederick Barbarossa (who drowned crossing a river in Anatolia), Philip II of France, and Richard I of England, whose military abilities made him the crusade's effective leader and whose negotiations with Saladin secured a treaty that restored Christian access to Jerusalem for pilgrims without restoring territorial control of the city. The Third Crusade's outcome — militarily impressive, strategically incomplete, and politically productive of the territorial conflict between Richard and Philip that shaped the subsequent decade of European politics — illustrated the fundamental tension within the crusading enterprise between its spiritual objective (the permanent Christian control of the holy places) and the political interests and limited resources of the secular rulers on whose participation the enterprise depended.

The subsequent major crusades illustrated different but equally fundamental difficulties. The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) was diverted, through a sequence of events whose precise degree of premeditation on the part of Venice's leadership remains disputed by historians but whose commercial logic is not in doubt, from its intended Egyptian destination to Constantinople, whose Latin Emperor Alexius IV Angelus the crusaders had agreed to restore in exchange for payment of their Venetian transport debts and a promise of union with Rome. When Alexius's assassination by Byzantine opponents of the Latin alliance made payment impossible, the crusading force sacked Constantinople in April 1204 in an episode of devastation whose systematic character — the looting of the Hagia Sophia and the city's churches, the rape and murder of civilians, the burning of entire quarters of the city, the installation of a Latin patriarch and a Latin emperor — distinguished it from the accidental violence of uncontrolled soldiery. Crusaders who had sworn to fight Muslims desecrated the greatest church in Christendom, slaughtered fellow Christians, and divided the Byzantine empire among themselves, with Venice securing the most strategically valuable territories. Pope Innocent III, who had explicitly forbidden the attack on Constantinople, was initially appalled — his letters describe the events in terms of scandal and sin — before accommodating himself to the outcome as an unexpected but perhaps providential opportunity for the reunion of Eastern and Western Christianity under Roman authority, an accommodation whose moral cost he perhaps underweighted and whose political benefit proved entirely illusory, since the Latin Empire lasted less than sixty years before Byzantine restoration under the Palaeologan dynasty in 1261, leaving behind only the deepened hatred between Eastern and Western Christianity that Chapter 19 identified as the event that made the Schism definitively irreparable.

The Fifth Crusade (1217–1221), the Sixth (Frederick II's treaty crusade of 1228–1229, which regained Jerusalem through diplomacy rather than warfare and was condemned by the papacy for negotiating with the enemy rather than fighting them), and the Seventh and Eighth Crusades of Louis IX of France (1248–1254 and 1270), in which the French king died of disease before Tunis, belong to a pattern of diminishing returns: each expedition generated genuine popular and aristocratic enthusiasm, each failed to achieve its stated objectives, and each contributed to the institutional exhaustion of the crusading ideal that made the loss of Acre in 1291 — the last major crusader stronghold in the Holy Land — not the occasion for a new wave of crusading enthusiasm but the beginning of a long reckoning with the movement's failure.

## The Anti-Jewish Violence of the Crusades

No honest account of the crusading movement can omit what the chroniclers' own accounts document with uncomfortable clarity: the crusades were, from the very beginning, also occasions of organised anti-Jewish violence within Europe itself, preceding the departure of armies for the Holy Land and constituting one of the most devastating episodes in the long history of Christian antisemitism examined in full in Chapter 24. The logic was explicit and was articulated by its perpetrators: if Christians were to fight the enemies of God across the sea, why should they leave unpunished the enemies of God living among them? The Rhineland massacres of 1096, in which the Jewish communities of Speyer, Worms, and Mainz were systematically attacked and killed — with estimates of the dead running into the thousands, though the exact figures are uncertain — by bands of crusaders before they had even left European soil, were the most devastating pre-crusade violence, but similar eruptions accompanied subsequent expeditions as well. The Rhineland Jewish communities that were destroyed in 1096 had been among the most prosperous and intellectually distinguished in Europe; their destruction marked a permanent turning point in the history of Jewish life in northern Europe, with the traumatic memory preserved in Hebrew chronicles of great literary and historical power that constitute an indispensable counter-narrative to the Latin crusading literature's self-celebration. The connection between crusading religious enthusiasm and anti-Jewish violence is not incidental or contingent but structural, rooted in the same theological framework of holy war and religious purification that animated the crusade itself — and it needs to be named as such rather than treated as a separate and unfortunate by-product of an otherwise spiritually creditable enterprise.

## The Crusades in Historical Perspective

The crusading movement's ultimate failure to achieve its territorial objectives, and the enormous human cost at which its temporary achievements were purchased, have made it one of the most contested episodes in the historiography of medieval Christianity. The older, largely positive assessment — crusading as the heroic expression of a united Christian civilisation defending itself against Islamic aggression, tinged with romantic chivalric nostalgia — has been replaced, in academic historiography, by a considerably more sceptical and institutionally attentive account that foregrounds the movement's entanglement with aristocratic land-hunger, Italian commercial interest, papal political ambition, and the internal violence that crusading enthusiasm generated against Jews, heretics, and Eastern Christians as well as Muslims. Neither the romantic nor the purely cynical reading does justice to the evidence: the participants believed, with varying degrees of sincerity and varying admixtures of material interest, in the theological framework that gave their enterprise meaning, and that framework needs to be understood historically rather than simply condemned from outside. What cannot be maintained, against the evidence, is a reading that treats the violence against Jews, the sack of Constantinople, and the Albigensian Crusade as peripheral to the movement's essential character rather than as expressions of the same logic of sanctified violence that its more straightforwardly celebrated military achievements embodied.
