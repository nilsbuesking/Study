# Chapter 26 — Heresy Before Luther: Wycliffe, Hus, and the Limits of Dissent

## Why These Movements Matter

The careers of John Wycliffe and Jan Hus are sometimes treated in general histories as mere anticipations of the Reformation — forerunners whose significance is exhausted by their resemblance to what came after them, precursors whose role is to point forward to Luther. This framing is understandable but ultimately misleading, since it obscures what was specific and significant about each movement in its own context, and since it tends to produce a teleological narrative in which the Reformation's eventual success casts a retrospective inevitability over everything that preceded it. The more historically accurate and more illuminating framing is to ask why Wycliffe's and Hus's programmes, each of which achieved real and substantial popular impact, were ultimately contained or destroyed by the existing institutional order, while Luther's — in some respects less theologically radical than Wycliffe's, and in many respects directly indebted to Hus — succeeded in producing a permanent institutional rupture. The answer to that question tells us as much about the specific political and institutional conditions of the sixteenth century as it does about any inherent superiority in Luther's theological programme.

## John Wycliffe: Scripture, Dominion, and the Attack on the Church

John Wycliffe (c. 1328–1384) was an Oxford philosopher and theologian of considerable academic distinction — his early career in metaphysics placed him among the serious professional philosophers of his generation, and his engagement with the nominalist tradition descending from Ockham gave his later theological positions a rigorous conceptual foundation that distinguished them from popular anti-clericalism — who developed, from the late 1370s onward, an increasingly radical programme of ecclesiastical reform that combined an assault on clerical wealth and power with a doctrine of scriptural authority that anticipated the Reformation's *sola scriptura* principle with considerable precision.

Wycliffe's political theology centred on his doctrine of "dominion" (*dominium*) — a concept he developed from the Augustinian and Franciscan traditions in a direction that proved theologically explosive. The argument, developed in his *De Dominio Divino* and *De Civili Dominio*, held that all legitimate authority — political or ecclesiastical — was held in a condition of stewardship from God, contingent on the holder's being in a state of grace: a lord in mortal sin held no true dominion, and his inferiors were theoretically released from their obligation to obey him. The implications for an institutional church whose clergy were visibly, systematically, and by Wycliffe's account comprehensively failing in their spiritual obligations were radical: a sinful pope held no true authority; a sinful clergy had forfeited its right to the endowments on which its institutional existence depended; and secular rulers, as God's stewards in the temporal sphere, had not merely the right but the obligation to strip a corrupt and unworthy church of its property and reform it by force.

This argument gave secular rulers — and specifically the English crown and nobility, to whom Wycliffe's programme proved attractive for reasons that were not entirely spiritual — a theological justification for doing what they had often wanted to do on purely material grounds: seize church property. Wycliffe's early protection from ecclesiastical prosecution owed much to the patronage of John of Gaunt, who found his programme politically convenient during the period of conflict over clerical taxation and papal provisions (the papal practice of appointing, and collecting revenues from, English benefices) that shaped English ecclesiastical politics in the 1370s. The connection between the theological and the political was entirely explicit and mutually reinforcing: Wycliffe's theology served English political interests, and English political protection enabled Wycliffe's theology.

Wycliffe's scriptural theology was equally radical and equally consequential for the Reformation tradition he fed into. Against the Catholic position that scripture and tradition together constituted the source of Christian authority, and that the church's magisterium was the authoritative interpreter of both, Wycliffe insisted that scripture alone was the supreme and self-sufficient rule of faith — that everything necessary for salvation was contained in scripture, that nothing not found in scripture was binding on the Christian conscience, and that the church's traditions, ceremonies, and institutional claims were valid only insofar as they could be demonstrated from scripture to be warranted. He promoted the translation of the Bible into English, and the Wycliffite Bible — completed in the 1380s and 1390s by his associates, since Wycliffe's own direct contribution to the translation work is uncertain — was the first complete English Bible, a cultural achievement of enormous significance whose political implications were fully understood by its opponents: a scripture available in the vernacular to any literate layperson was a scripture whose interpretation was no longer controllable by a Latin-educated clerical monopoly.

Wycliffe's sacramental theology pushed further still, into territory that even some of his supporters found difficult to follow: his denial of transubstantiation, the doctrine that the bread and wine of the Eucharist are converted in their underlying substance into the body and blood of Christ, on the grounds that the Aristotelian philosophical framework on which transubstantiation depended was neither scripturally warranted nor philosophically coherent (substance cannot be destroyed, he argued, within an Aristotelian framework consistently applied), struck at what the mediaeval church regarded as the central mystery of Christian life in a way that his criticisms of clerical wealth, however radical in their implications, did not. The Oxford condemnation of his Eucharistic views in 1381 marked the point at which his programme exceeded what even reform-sympathetic institutional figures could protect, and his political protection became progressively less reliable as the conservative reaction against the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 — in which Wycliffe's ideas were associated, not entirely fairly but not entirely inaccurately, with the intellectual climate of radical social questioning — made his patrons more cautious about their association with him.

Wycliffe died in 1384 of natural causes, escaping the fate that the Council of Constance would subsequently determine he deserved: his bones were exhumed in 1428, burned, and the ashes cast into the River Swift — a posthumous execution whose symbolic thoroughness reflects both the seriousness with which the institutional church regarded his legacy and the limits of what it could practically do against an Oxford don who had died peacefully in his parish. His followers in England — the Lollards, who maintained Wycliffite positions on scripture, the Eucharist, and the corruption of the clergy through the fifteenth century, surviving underground persecution to emerge and intersect with early Lutheran ideas in the 1520s — represent the most direct continuation of his programme on English soil, and constitute an important context for the specific speed with which Reformation ideas spread in England examined in Chapter 30.

## Jan Hus: Bohemia and the Politicisation of Reform

Jan Hus (c. 1369–1415) was a theologian and preacher at the University of Prague whose engagement with Wycliffe's writings — Bohemian students had brought manuscripts of Wycliffe's philosophical and theological works back from Oxford in the late fourteenth century, and Hus absorbed them with a thoroughness that left the intellectual dependence unmistakable — gave his reforming programme a conceptual foundation more rigorous than the popular anti-clericalism that surrounded him in Prague's volatile atmosphere of the early fifteenth century. His career cannot be understood apart from the specifically Bohemian political and national context in which it developed, since the reform movement he led became entangled, in ways that proved both politically empowering and ultimately fatal, with the Czech national consciousness that was simultaneously developing in tension with the German-speaking establishment of the university and the church in Bohemia.

Hus's theological positions were, in their core content, considerably more moderate than Wycliffe's: he did not deny transubstantiation, accepted the authority of scripture as mediated through the church's tradition, and focused his reforming energy primarily on the moral and pastoral failures of the clergy — simony, absenteeism, the inadequacy of preaching — rather than on systematic doctrinal revision. What made him dangerous to the institutional church was less his specific theological positions, several of which were shared by critics who suffered no persecution, than the combination of his popular platform (the Bethlehem Chapel in Prague, where he preached in Czech to enormous audiences), his willingness to press his appeal to the authority of scripture against ecclesiastical authority when he judged the two to conflict, and the political protection afforded him by the Bohemian nobility and ultimately by the Bohemian king Wenceslas IV, who found the reforming movement's pressure on ecclesiastical wealth and German ecclesiastical domination politically useful.

The specific controversy that drew Hus into open conflict with Rome was the indulgence affair of 1412, in which Pope John XXIII — the same John XXIII who would shortly be deposed at Constance — authorised the sale of indulgences to fund his military campaign against the King of Naples, and Hus publicly condemned the practice as both corrupt and without scriptural warrant. This condemnation put him on precisely the same ground — the invalidity of indulgences not grounded in scripture — that Luther would occupy a century later, and the parallel is not coincidental: Luther himself acknowledged Hus as a predecessor and was shocked, reading Hus's writings while preparing for the Leipzig Debate of 1519, to discover how thoroughly he shared positions he had thought were his own innovations.

Hus's fate at the Council of Constance, where he was burned in July 1415 despite the Emperor Sigismund's safe conduct, has been examined in Chapter 25 for its significance as evidence of the conciliar movement's limits; its significance here is different. The burning of Hus was not the end of the Hussite movement but its radicalisation: Bohemia erupted in the Hussite Wars (1419–1434), in which Bohemian armies, motivated by the theological convictions of the reform movement and by a burning sense of national injustice at the martyrdom of their greatest preacher, repeatedly defeated the crusading armies sent against them by the emperor and the papacy — the only sustained case in the mediaeval period in which a heretical movement successfully resisted military suppression through armed force. The Hussites' demands — summarised in the Four Articles of Prague (1420), which called for freedom of preaching, communion in both kinds for the laity (the *sub utraque specie* demand that gave the moderate Hussite party the name Utraquists), the end of clerical property and secular power, and the punishment of mortal sin regardless of the sinner's clerical status — constituted a genuinely revolutionary programme of church reform, and the Utraquist settlement eventually agreed at the Council of Basel in 1436, which conceded communion in both kinds to the Bohemian laity, was the only case before the Reformation in which the mediaeval church made a formal doctrinal concession to popular heretical pressure through negotiation rather than either extermination or absorption.

## Why the Limits Held — And Why They Would Not Hold for Luther

The question of why Wycliffism and Hussitism, despite their real impact, did not produce the permanent institutional rupture that Luther's movement achieved is worth addressing directly, since the answer is neither that Luther was theologically superior to his predecessors nor that the truth of his positions simply became undeniable in the sixteenth century — both teleological explanations that the evidence does not support.

Several structural conditions distinguished Luther's context from that of his predecessors. The printing press, absent in Wycliffe's and Hus's lifetimes and only beginning to make its impact felt in the generation before Luther, transformed the dynamics of theological controversy in ways that were not merely quantitative but qualitative: a pamphlet produced in Wittenberg could circulate across the German-speaking world within weeks, generating a public debate too widely distributed for any single institutional authority to suppress by the seizure of manuscripts and the silencing of individual preachers. Wycliffe's manuscripts were copyable and were copied; Hus's sermons were heard by the thousands who crowded the Bethlehem Chapel; but neither operated in a media environment remotely comparable to the one Luther inhabited from 1517 onward, and the difference in the speed and geographic reach of their respective controversies reflects this structural change as much as any difference in the content of what they taught.

The political context also differed in crucial ways. Wycliffe's English protection depended on the goodwill of a small group of aristocratic patrons whose interest in his programme was partly instrumental and who withdrew that protection when its political cost rose; Hus's Bohemian protection was real and important but was ultimately overborne by the combination of imperial and papal pressure that the Council of Constance represented. Luther, by contrast, operated in a Holy Roman Empire whose constitutional structure — a confederation of territories under princes with real sovereignty and real incentives to assert independence from both imperial and papal authority — provided structural protection that neither of his predecessors had access to. The Elector of Saxony's protection of Luther after the Diet of Worms in 1521 was not an individual act of personal patronage comparable to John of Gaunt's protection of Wycliffe; it was the exercise of territorial sovereignty by a prince who had both political and theological reasons for maintaining it, within a constitutional framework that the emperor could contest but not straightforwardly override. The Reformation succeeded partly because it occurred in a political environment where the structural conditions for its survival, once it had established itself in a critical mass of territories, were considerably more robust than anything Wycliffe or Hus had been able to rely on.

This does not reduce the Reformation to a political accident or Luther's theology to a mere convenient instrument of princely power — the theological content was genuine, the popular response was real, and the intellectual programme had a momentum of its own that the political protection enabled rather than created. But it does mean that the question "why did the Reformation succeed where earlier reform movements failed?" cannot be answered by theology alone, and that the structural and contingent conditions of the early sixteenth century need to be part of the answer. It is to those conditions — and to the specific theological content of Luther's breakthrough — that the following chapter turns.
