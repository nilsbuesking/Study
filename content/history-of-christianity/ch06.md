# Chapter 6 — Core Doctrine II: Sacraments, the Eucharist, and Transubstantiation

## What a Sacrament Is

A sacrament, in the classical definition associated with Augustine and refined by mediaeval scholastic theology treated more fully in Part V, is a visible or outward sign of an invisible or inward grace — a physical, perceptible act or element (water, bread, wine, oil, the laying on of hands) through which the church has historically understood God to actually communicate, rather than merely symbolically represent, the spiritual reality the sign signifies. This distinction between a sacrament and a mere symbol is theologically load-bearing throughout this chapter and the controversies that later attach to it: a flag is a symbol of a nation, conveying meaning by convention and association without itself being or containing the nation it represents; a sacrament, on the mainstream historic Christian understanding before the Reformation reopened the question in the specific and various ways Part VI will narrate, was held to actually convey, by virtue of the action being rightly performed (a principle given the technical Latin label *ex opere operato*, "from the work performed," meaning the sacrament's efficacy depends on the divinely instituted action itself rather than on the spiritual merit or worthiness of the minister performing it — a doctrine with its own controversial history, examined in Chapter 11's treatment of the Donatist crisis), the grace it signified.

The number of sacraments recognised across the tradition has not been uniform, and this variation matters for understanding later confessional division. The mediaeval Western church, settling a question that had remained somewhat fluid in the patristic period, arrived at a standard list of seven — baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance (confession and absolution), anointing of the sick (historically also called extreme unction), holy orders (ordination), and matrimony — a sevenfold enumeration given authoritative conciliar definition at the Council of Florence in 1439 and reaffirmed against Protestant challenge at Trent, treated in Part VI. Eastern Orthodoxy recognises substantially the same seven "mysteries" (the more usual Eastern term, *mysterion*, carrying connotations somewhat different from the Latin *sacramentum*'s original legal and military overtones of a binding oath) without historically insisting on the number seven as a fixed dogmatic boundary in quite the same conciliar way the medieval West did. The Protestant Reformers, examined in their proper context in Part VI, would narrow this number sharply, recognising as sacraments in the full sense only those they could demonstrate were directly instituted by Christ himself in scripture — baptism and the Eucharist alone, on the Lutheran and Reformed reckoning, with the remaining five reclassified as valuable but non-sacramental rites or, in the case of penance, retained by Luther himself in qualified form before being more thoroughly set aside by later Reformed theology. This chapter, consistent with its stated purpose of explaining doctrine before controversy, treats the two universally shared sacraments, baptism and the Eucharist, in some detail, before turning to the Eucharistic question that would become the single most theologically and politically explosive point of Reformation-era division.

## Baptism: Incorporation and Cleansing

Baptism, the rite of ritual washing in water performed, in the dominant Trinitarian formula attested already in Matthew's Gospel, "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit," has been understood across the tradition as the rite of initiation into the Christian community, effecting, in language drawn substantially from Paul's own theology of the rite in Romans 6, a dying and rising with Christ — a washing away of the guilt of original sin (a doctrine examined more fully in Chapter 7) and an incorporation into the body of Christ that is the church. The mode of baptism — full immersion, partial immersion, or affusion (pouring water over the head) — and the question of infant versus adult ("believer's") baptism have been historically variable and, in the case of the latter question, a genuine and consequential point of division: the mainstream patristic and mediaeval church baptised infants as a matter of standard practice, understanding the rite's efficacy to depend on the divinely instituted action rather than on the conscious faith of the recipient, while the more radical wing of the Reformation, the Anabaptists treated in Part VI, would insist that baptism required a believer's own conscious profession of faith and so rejected infant baptism as no true baptism at all — a position the mainstream Reformers, Luther and Calvin among them, rejected as sharply as Catholic authorities did, making this one of the few doctrinal questions on which sixteenth-century Catholic and mainstream Protestant authorities, otherwise sharply divided, substantially agreed against a shared third position.

## The Eucharist: Institution and Early Understanding

The Eucharist (from the Greek *eucharistia*, "thanksgiving"), also called Holy Communion, the Lord's Supper, or the Mass (this last term, from the Latin dismissal formula *Ite, missa est*, becoming the standard Western liturgical name for the entire rite of which the Eucharistic meal is the culmination), commemorates and, on the dominant historic understanding examined fully below, actually re-presents the Last Supper — the meal Jesus shared with his disciples on the night before his crucifixion, at which, according to the institution narratives preserved independently in the three Synoptic Gospels and, in close though not identical form, in Paul's own letter to the Corinthians (1 Corinthians 11, itself among the earliest written witnesses to the tradition, predating any of the Gospels), Jesus took bread and wine, identified them as his body and blood, and instructed his disciples to "do this in remembrance of me."

The conviction that something more than ordinary memorial commemoration was taking place in this rite is attested very early and very widely across the patristic record, well before any of the later technical philosophical vocabulary examined below had been developed to explain precisely how. Ignatius of Antioch, writing in the early second century and already encountered in Chapter 3 for his role in consolidating episcopal authority, refers to the Eucharist as "the medicine of immortality" and criticises docetic opponents (those who denied Christ's full physical humanity, a position touched on in Chapter 5's discussion of the Incarnation) precisely on the grounds of their corresponding denial that the Eucharistic bread is truly Christ's flesh. Justin Martyr, in the same mid-second-century *First Apology* discussed in Chapter 3 for its broader apologetic argument, describes the Eucharistic food, once consecrated by prayer, as being "the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh" rather than common bread and drink. This early and widespread conviction of a real, rather than merely symbolic or memorial, presence is important to register clearly, because it establishes that the later mediaeval technical vocabulary of transubstantiation, examined next, was not inventing a new and previously absent claim of real presence out of philosophical enthusiasm, but was rather attempting to give increasingly precise conceptual articulation to a conviction the church had held, in less technically specified form, from a very early date — even though, as Chapter 6's final section and Part VI's Reformation chapters will make clear, the question of precisely how that real presence was best conceptualised, and whether the mediaeval West's particular philosophical articulation of it was itself necessary or correct, would become one of the most fiercely contested points of the entire Reformation.

## Transubstantiation: The Aristotelian Framework

The doctrine of transubstantiation, given its first conciliar definition in the technical vocabulary it would retain at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 and its fullest scholastic elaboration in Thomas Aquinas's thirteenth-century work (treated in its proper chronological and intellectual context in Chapter 23), explains the Eucharistic real presence using a philosophical distinction borrowed from Aristotelian metaphysics, as that metaphysics had been recovered and absorbed into Western Christian thought across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries — a recovery and absorption itself part of the broader scholastic project examined in Part V. Aristotelian metaphysics distinguishes between a thing's substance (Latin *substantia*, the underlying reality or "what-it-fundamentally-is" of a thing, not perceptible directly by the senses) and its accidents (Latin *accidentia*, the perceptible qualities — colour, taste, texture, smell, weight — that inhere in and are normally reliable indicators of the underlying substance, but which are, in Aristotelian metaphysical theory, in principle conceptually distinguishable from it). In an ordinary object, substance and accidents change together: when bread goes stale, both its underlying substance (it ceases, on this framework, to be the substance "bread" in a meaningful culinary sense) and its accidents (texture, taste) change in tandem, and this is the normal, expected pattern of all natural change.

The doctrine of transubstantiation claims that at the moment of priestly consecration during the Mass, something metaphysically unprecedented occurs: the entire substance of the bread is converted into the entire substance of Christ's body, and the entire substance of the wine into the entire substance of Christ's blood, while the accidents of bread and wine — their appearance, taste, texture, and so forth — remain entirely unchanged, continuing to look, taste, and behave exactly as bread and wine do. What communicants receive, on this doctrine, is therefore not bread that has been spiritually charged or symbolically repurposed, nor a mixture or coexistence of bread-substance and Christ's-body-substance side by side, but the literal, complete substance of Christ's body and blood, present under (the technical term is "under the species of," *species* here meaning the outward appearing form) what continues, at the level of mere appearance, to present itself as bread and wine. This is, it should be said plainly, an intentionally precise and intentionally counter-intuitive philosophical claim, designed specifically to explain how the Eucharistic elements could be, with full metaphysical seriousness, both genuinely and completely Christ's body and blood and also continue to look, taste, and digest exactly like ordinary bread and wine — a tension the Aristotelian substance/accident distinction was deployed specifically to resolve, by locating the change at the level of substance alone while leaving the entire accidental, sensorily perceptible order untouched.

It bears emphasising, given how readily the doctrine is caricatured in both directions, that the mediaeval church did not teach that communicants were practising a disguised form of cannibalism by consuming literal flesh tissue in any biological or anatomical sense — the doctrine operates entirely at the level of Aristotelian substance, a category with no straightforward correspondence to anything modern biology or chemistry would recognise as "what something is really made of," and collapsing the mediaeval metaphysical claim into a crude literalism about flesh and blood, a collapse that both some hostile early modern polemic and some popular modern caricature have been prone to, misrepresents the doctrine's own internal philosophical logic, whatever one makes of that logic's ultimate persuasiveness. Equally, the doctrine's heavy dependence on a specific, historically contingent Aristotelian metaphysical apparatus — recovered into Western thought only in the twelfth century, and not available in anything like this technical form to the patristic authors, including Ignatius and Justin discussed above, whose earlier real-presence convictions long predate it — is precisely the point on which Reformation-era critics, examined in Part VI, would press hardest: not generally by denying any form of Eucharistic real presence (Luther himself argued fiercely, against more radical reformers, for a continued strong doctrine of real presence, articulated through a different conceptual framework usually labelled sacramental union or, polemically and somewhat imprecisely, consubstantiation), but by questioning whether this specific, philosophically contingent, and historically late mediaeval Aristotelian articulation of that presence was itself a necessary or even a coherent way of stating what the patristic church had believed in less technically specified form for a millennium beforehand. That dispute, irreducibly theological in its substance and irreducibly entangled with questions of papal authority and ecclesiastical power in its consequences, belongs properly to the Reformation chapters of Part VI; what matters for this chapter is that the reader carries forward a clear grasp of what transubstantiation actually claims, so that the sixteenth-century argument over it can be followed as a dispute about a precisely defined position rather than a vaguely gestured-at one.

With the two universally shared sacraments now explained, the following chapter turns to a cluster of related doctrines — sin, grace, and the fate of the soul after death — that govern how the tradition has understood both the human condition the sacraments address and the ultimate destiny toward which sacramental and moral life is oriented.
