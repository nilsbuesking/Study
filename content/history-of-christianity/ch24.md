# Chapter 24 — The Church and the Jews: The Origins of Institutional Christian Antisemitism

## Framing: Theology, History, and Moral Responsibility

The history of Christian antisemitism is one of the most consequential and most morally serious topics this overview addresses, and it requires a particular kind of historiographical care: enough precision to avoid flattening fifteen centuries of varied relationship into a single undifferentiated story of persecution, and enough moral clarity to avoid the opposite error of treating the trajectory from theological anti-Judaism to genocidal antisemitism as so complex and mediated that the church's institutional responsibility becomes effectively invisible. The position taken here, consistent with the best of current scholarship on the subject, is that Christian theological anti-Judaism — the systematic, institutionally transmitted set of negative claims about Jews and Judaism that the church developed from the patristic period onward — was a necessary though not sufficient cause of the violence and dispossession that Jews in Christian societies endured across the mediaeval and early modern periods, and that the connection between theological contempt and physical harm was not accidental or contingent but structural: the former repeatedly supplied the warrant, the vocabulary, and the emotional register within which the latter became possible and sometimes actively encouraged.

The distinction between antisemitism and anti-Judaism is worth establishing at the outset, since the two terms are sometimes used interchangeably and sometimes distinguished in ways that matter for the historical argument. Anti-Judaism, in the sense used by most scholars of the patristic and early mediaeval period, refers to a theologically grounded hostility toward Judaism as a religious system and toward Jewish practice as an inferior, superseded, or spiritually dangerous mode of engagement with the God of Abraham — a position whose primary concern is religious rather than racial or ethnic. Antisemitism, in its modern sense, adds to this theological contempt a set of racial, biological, or pseudo-scientific claims about the inherent characteristics of Jews as a people that are independent of their religious practice and that cannot be removed by conversion — a development most clearly attested in the later mediaeval period and consolidated in the nineteenth century. The distinction is real and historically significant: in the earlier period, Jewish conversion to Christianity was in principle the solution the church advocated, and converted Jews were, at least formally, no longer subject to the religious disabilities imposed on Jews. By the later mediaeval period and above all by the racialised antisemitism of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the distinction between religious and ethnic identity had collapsed in ways that made conversion irrelevant as a solution and that contributed directly to the specific form the Nazi genocide took. Both periods are addressed in this chapter, with the full treatment of the twentieth century reserved for Chapter 37.

## The Patristic Roots: Supersessionism and the Adversus Judaeos Tradition

The theological framework within which Christian anti-Judaism developed has a name in modern scholarship: supersessionism — the doctrine that the Christian church has superseded or replaced the Jewish people as the covenant people of God, that the Hebrew scriptures properly interpreted point to and find their fulfilment in Christ, and that post-Christian Judaism, in its continued adherence to the law and its rejection of Christ, represents a religious condition that is at best spiritually blind and at worst willfully obstinate. This doctrine, whose scriptural basis was found in Paul's letters (particularly the complex argument of Romans 9–11, where Paul discusses the fate of Israel with a theological nuance that the supersessionist tradition frequently simplified), in the Letter to the Hebrews (which presents the Levitical priesthood and sacrificial system as shadows superseded by Christ's definitive high priesthood), and in the Gospel of John's sharp opposition between Jesus and "the Jews," was present in recognisable form from an early date and provided the theological soil within which more explicitly hostile treatments of Judaism and Jews could grow.

The *Adversus Judaeos* ("Against the Jews") literary genre — a recognisable body of polemical writing addressing Christian-Jewish theological differences, refuting Jewish objections to Christian claims about Jesus, and arguing for the church's superiority to the synagogue — developed across the patristic period, with contributions from Tertullian, Origen, John Chrysostom, and Augustine, among many others. The tone and content of these texts varied considerably: some engaged seriously with Jewish scriptural interpretation and preserved a residue of respect for the biblical heritage they claimed to have inherited; others, particularly John Chrysostom's eight *Homilies Against the Jews* (delivered in Antioch around 386–388), descended to an invective so extreme — Jews characterised as deicides, the synagogue as a haunt of demons and a house of prostitution, Jewish religious practice as worse than that of pigs and goats — that subsequent generations of scholars have struggled to explain how a man of Chrysostom's evident theological sophistication in other respects could have produced them, with the most plausible answer involving the specific pastoral situation of a congregation in Antioch whose members were apparently attending Jewish synagogue services alongside Christian liturgy in ways that Chrysostom found theologically and institutionally threatening. The *homilies* were preached in a specific context, but they circulated far beyond it, and their imagery of demonic Jews and diabolical synagogues entered the repertoire of Christian anti-Jewish polemic with consequences that outlasted any specific Antiochene pastoral emergency by many centuries.

Augustine's position on the Jews, more complex and more theologically structured than Chrysostom's, proved equally consequential in the long run. Augustine developed what scholars have called the doctrine of Jewish "witness": Jews, in their very persistence as a recognisable community adhering to the scriptures that Christians claimed to have fulfilled, served an involuntary but providentially ordained function as living testimony to the Christian scriptures' authenticity — their continued existence was therefore not to be ended by extermination but preserved in a condition of humiliation and servitude that made their witness legible, as a conquered and subjected people whose defeat testified to Christianity's truth. This doctrine had the practical consequence of providing theological justification for the Jews' continued existence within Christian society while simultaneously mandating their degradation: they should be kept alive but kept low, as witnesses rather than as equals. The Augustinian witness doctrine thus constituted both a barrier against the logic of extermination and a theological warrant for perpetual humiliation — a combination whose internal tensions would be exposed in increasingly violent ways across the mediaeval period.

## The Charge of Deicide and Its Consequences

The single most consequential theological charge in the history of Christian antisemitism was the accusation of deicide — the killing of God — attributed collectively to the Jewish people understood as a whole and across time. The charge derived from readings of the Gospel passion narratives, particularly the notorious verse of Matthew 27:25 in which the Jerusalem crowd says "his blood be upon us and upon our children," which the tradition interpreted as a collective acceptance of guilt for Jesus's death transmitted across generations, such that all Jews everywhere and in every subsequent era bore co-responsibility for the crucifixion. The historical absurdity of this reading — which required ignoring that Jesus himself, his disciples, and the earliest Christian community were all Jewish, and which attributed to a first-century Jerusalem crowd a guilt transmissible to their descendants in fourteenth-century Germany or twentieth-century Poland — did not prevent it from operating as a powerful and destructive trope in mediaeval Christian culture, providing the emotional charge that energised anti-Jewish violence at its most intense.

It was this deicide charge, activated within the broader theological framework of Jewish guilt and supersession, that gave the Crusades their anti-Jewish dimension examined in Chapter 21: the logic that one should not fight God's enemies abroad while leaving his killers unpunished at home was not an aberration from crusading theology but a consistent extension of it. The same charge energised the recurrent accusations of ritual murder — the claim, first attested in England at Norwich in 1144 and subsequently proliferating across much of Europe, that Jews ritually murdered Christian children to use their blood in religious ceremonies — a fabrication without any basis in Jewish law or practice (which in fact prohibits the consumption of blood), whose emergence and rapid spread illustrates how the theological warrant of deicide guilt could generate specific and lethal accusations in local contexts. The blood libel, as these accusations are known, resulted in the execution of numerous innocent Jews, in several cases the establishment of Christian cults around the alleged victims (the cult of "Little Saint Hugh" of Lincoln being among the more notorious), and in a pattern of violence that repeated itself across different European communities over several centuries with a consistency that reflects the stability of the underlying theological framework more than any specific local circumstance.

## The Institutional Architecture of Mediaeval Jewish Subjugation

The mediaeval church's institutional measures against Jews were not limited to the violence that theological anti-Judaism licensed in popular culture but extended to a systematic legal architecture of degradation that the papacy and the councils constructed across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries with increasing precision. The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 — convened by Innocent III and in many respects the high-water mark of papal institutional authority — imposed on Jews living in Christian territories a set of disabilities that codified and in some respects intensified earlier practice: Jews were required to wear distinctive clothing or badges to mark their visible separation from Christian society; they were prohibited from appearing in public during Holy Week; they were forbidden from holding public office over Christians; and usury regulations were tightened in ways that pushed Jewish economic activity increasingly toward money-lending while simultaneously intensifying the resentment that money-lending generated.

The requirement of distinctive dress — a yellow badge in most territories, though the specific form varied by region — was not merely symbolic but functionally significant: it made Jews visibly identifiable in public, facilitating both the social exclusion the canon law mandated and the physical targeting that periodically followed. The prohibition on public office represented a formal exclusion from civic participation that reinforced at the institutional level the theological claims about Jewish subjugation that Augustine's witness doctrine had articulated. The usury regulations created a structural trap: Christian canon law prohibited Christians from lending money at interest, effectively reserving this economically indispensable function for Jews, whose subsequent identification with money-lending was then used to fuel the resentment and stereotype of Jewish greed that anti-Jewish polemic deployed against them — a perfect illustration of how institutional structures generated the social realities that theological prejudice then claimed to be merely describing.

Expulsions of Jewish communities from entire territories constituted the most extreme institutional expression of mediaeval anti-Judaism: England expelled its Jewish population in 1290, France repeatedly between 1306 and 1394, and the series of German and other expulsions across the later mediaeval period climaxed in the Spanish expulsion of 1492, one of the largest and most traumatic single episodes of forced displacement in mediaeval Jewish history, and the subject of Chapter 31's treatment of the late mediaeval and early modern context. These expulsions were not random or spontaneous but the products of deliberate royal and ecclesiastical policy, motivated by combinations of theological hostility, fiscal exploitation (Jewish property was typically confiscated at expulsion), and the political calculation that the Christian population's resentment of Jewish communities could be directed toward a defined enemy to the political advantage of the authority ordering the expulsion.

## The Black Death and the Accusations of Well-Poisoning

The Black Death of 1347–1351, which killed somewhere between a third and half of Europe's population in one of the most devastating demographic catastrophes in human history, generated in several regions a wave of anti-Jewish violence surpassing anything that had preceded it in scale and systematic character. The mechanism was the accusation, which spread rapidly across southern France, Switzerland, and Germany, that Jews had caused the plague by poisoning wells — a charge whose logic required ignoring the inconvenient fact that Jewish communities were dying of the plague alongside their Christian neighbours, a point that some contemporaries noted and that some authorities, including Pope Clement VI, explicitly used in attempts to protect Jewish communities from mob violence. These attempts were only partially effective: the massacres that accompanied the plague years destroyed dozens of Jewish communities in the Rhineland and elsewhere, with estimates of the dead running into the tens of thousands. The Strasbourg massacre of February 1349, in which approximately two thousand Jews were burned alive before the plague had even reached the city — making the preventive character of the killing explicit — was among the most systematic, preceded by the dispossession of Jewish property and conducted with the participation of civic authorities.

The well-poisoning accusation illustrates with particular clarity the dynamic whereby theological anti-Judaism generated a framework of suspicion and hostility within which specific accusations, however factually baseless, could achieve immediate credibility and motivate extreme violence. The theological claim that Jews were enemies of Christian society, guilty of deicide and engaged in ritual murder, did not by itself cause any specific massacre; but it provided the interpretive framework within which plague, understood as divine punishment, could be attributed to Jewish malice, and it supplied the emotional register of righteous vengeance within which killing Jews could be experienced as the performance of God's justice rather than as straightforward murder.

## Forward Threads: Luther and the Holocaust

The history traced in this chapter does not end in the mediaeval period, and two forward connections need to be flagged explicitly here, even though their full treatment belongs to later chapters.

Martin Luther's relationship to antisemitism underwent a trajectory that is particularly instructive and particularly disturbing. His early writing *That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew* (1523) argued, with apparent generosity, that Jews had been right to reject the corrupt papacy's caricature of Christianity, and that genuine Christian proclamation might now lead to Jewish conversion — a position whose apparent tolerance was instrumentally motivated by the hope of conversion rather than by any respect for Judaism on its own terms, but which represented a genuine departure from the more extreme mediaeval polemic. When Jewish conversion did not materialise, Luther's writing shifted dramatically: his late works, above all *On the Jews and Their Lies* (1543), produced toward the end of his life and explicitly disowned by some of his own contemporaries as an embarrassment, descended to recommendations of a savagery that went beyond most mediaeval precedent — the burning of synagogues, the destruction of Jewish homes, the confiscation of Jewish property, the prohibition of rabbinical teaching, and ultimately the expulsion or forced labour of Jews who refused conversion. These recommendations were cited explicitly by Nazi propagandists in the twentieth century, and Luther's late antisemitism cannot be treated as a peripheral aberration from his theological programme without ignoring how deeply the supersessionist framework that generated it was embedded in the Reformation's own reading of Paul and the Hebrew scriptures. The full treatment of this thread belongs to Chapter 27's discussion of Luther's theological programme; what this chapter establishes is the long tradition within which Luther's late writings operated, making their content less surprising, even as it makes them no less damning.

The churches' conduct during the Holocaust — the silence of the German Protestant establishment, the failures of the Catholic church under Pius XII, and the minority of individual Christians who actively resisted — belongs to Chapter 37's treatment of the twentieth century. What connects that history to the present chapter is the argument, made most powerfully by the historian Raul Hilberg and developed subsequently by many others, that the Nazi genocide drew on and required the prior existence of a centuries-long tradition of institutional anti-Judaism that had produced, across the Christian societies of Europe, a population habituated to regarding Jews as a separate, dangerous, and degraded category of human being, and that the specific measures of the Nazi state — the yellow badge, the prohibition on intermarriage, the exclusion from public office, the ghetto, the expulsion, and ultimately the extermination — each had precedents in the mediaeval Christian legal tradition, some of which Nazi ideologues were aware of and explicitly cited. This does not make the church responsible for the Holocaust in the direct sense of having ordered or desired it, and the Nazi genocide's racial rather than theological logic marked a genuine rupture from the Augustinian witness doctrine's insistence on Jewish survival. But it does mean that the ground on which the Holocaust occurred had been prepared across many centuries of Christian theological contempt and institutional discrimination, and that any honest account of the church's relationship to the Jews must reckon with this accumulation rather than treating the genocide as an event entirely external to the Christian tradition's own history.

The Second Vatican Council's declaration *Nostra Aetate* (1965), which formally repudiated the deicide charge, affirmed the spiritual bond between Christianity and Judaism, and condemned antisemitism as contrary to the spirit of Christ, constituted the most significant official Catholic reckoning with this history to date — a genuine and important departure, even if it came very late, even if its reception in Catholic practice and preaching has been uneven, and even if its theological repudiation of supersessionism remains incomplete in ways that subsequent ecumenical dialogue has continued to address. Its treatment belongs with the Vatican II chapter (Chapter 39); its mention here signals that the history traced in the present chapter is not one that the church has been entirely unwilling to confront, even as the confrontation has been insufficient and overdue.
