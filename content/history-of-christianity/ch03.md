# Chapter 3 — Persecution, Apologetics, and the Church Within the Roman Empire

## A Movement Without Legal Standing

By the end of the first century, Christianity occupied an anomalous and precarious legal position within the Roman world, one that needs to be understood structurally before the individual persecutions themselves can be properly assessed. Judaism, from which the movement had emerged and from which it was only gradually and unevenly distinguishing itself in the eyes of outside observers, enjoyed the status of a *religio licita* — not a formal technical category in Roman law so much as a long-settled practical accommodation, dating to the late Republic, under which Jewish communities were permitted exemption from the otherwise general expectation of participation in civic and imperial cult, on the grounds of antiquity and ancestral custom, qualities Roman administrative culture took seriously as grounds for toleration even of practices it found alien. Christianity possessed no comparable claim to antiquity, and as it became progressively more Gentile in composition and more clearly distinguished from the synagogue communities that had initially sheltered it — a separation usually associated with the later first century, accelerated by the trauma of the Jewish War and by synagogue-level measures, such as the *Birkat ha-Minim* curse against heretics incorporated into synagogue liturgy in the late first or early second century, that made continued dual affiliation increasingly untenable on both sides — it lost the benefit of that accommodation without acquiring any alternative legal protection of its own.

This left Roman authorities, when they did notice and act against Christians, working without a specific statute criminalising the new movement as such; what existing law did supply was the general expectation, woven into civic life at every level, of participation in sacrifice to the traditional gods and, increasingly from the early imperial period onward, to the genius of the emperor — not usually a demand for personal belief in the emperor's divinity, which most Romans did not straightforwardly hold either, but a demand for the outward, civic performance of loyalty and piety that sacrifice constituted. Christian refusal of this performance, rooted in a monotheism inherited from Judaism but now shorn of Judaism's protective antiquity, was experienced by Roman observers not primarily as theological deviance — a category with limited purchase in Roman religious thought generally, which was considerably more concerned with orthopraxy than orthodoxy — but as a kind of civic and cosmic disloyalty, liable to be popularly associated with the *odium humani generis*, "hatred of the human race," that Tacitus attributes to Christians in his account of the Neronian persecution, and with a cluster of related anxieties about secretive nocturnal gatherings, sexual impropriety, and cannibalism (the latter two almost certainly garbled popular distortions of the Eucharist's language of eating Christ's body and drinking his blood, and of the genuinely close, kiss-exchanging fellowship of the agape meal) that circulated widely enough to require direct rebuttal in the apologetic literature discussed below.

## The Pattern of Persecution: Local, Sporadic, and Later Systematic

The popular image of continuous, empire-wide persecution running in an unbroken line from Nero to Constantine considerably overstates and flattens what the evidence actually supports, and correcting this image is one of the more important tasks of this chapter, not in order to minimise the real suffering involved but in order to understand its actual mechanisms. For the first two and a half centuries of Christian history, persecution was characteristically local, sporadic, and dependent on the initiative of provincial governors, mob agitation, or specific local grievances, rather than systematic empire-wide policy emanating from Rome — a pattern only decisively broken in the mid-third century, for reasons examined below.

The earliest persecution of any documentary substance, under Nero following the great fire of Rome in 64 CE, is attested by Tacitus, writing half a century later, as a deliberate scapegoating exercise: Nero, facing rumours that he himself had ordered the fire, redirected suspicion onto Christians, already an object of popular dislike, and subjected a number of them in Rome to executions of conspicuous and theatrical cruelty. This episode, however, appears to have been confined to the city of Rome itself rather than constituting an empire-wide edict, and there is no clear evidence of a sustained anti-Christian legal policy issuing from it. The clearest window onto how the system actually operated at the local level comes considerably later, from the correspondence between Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia-Pontus, and the emperor Trajan, datable to around 112 CE: Pliny, uncertain how to handle accusations against Christians brought before him — a sign in itself that no settled imperial policy yet existed for him to consult — describes a procedure of interrogating the accused and executing those who persisted in confessing Christian identity after opportunity to recant and perform sacrifice, while expressing uncertainty about whether the name "Christian" alone constituted sufficient grounds for punishment independent of any other crime, and whether anonymous accusations should be entertained. Trajan's reply, equally significant for what it does not say, instructs Pliny to continue broadly as he had been doing but explicitly forbids both the active seeking-out of Christians and the entertaining of anonymous denunciations — establishing, in effect if not in formal statute, a pattern that would govern much of the next century and a half: Christianity remained nominally a capital offence (the *nomen ipsum*, "the name itself," as later apologists bitterly noted), but its enforcement depended on local accusation and governorial discretion rather than systematic state pursuit, producing a legal situation apologists such as Tertullian would later mock as self-evidently inconsistent — punishing the name without investigating the substance of any actual crime.

This locally contingent pattern continued through the second century — the martyrdom of Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, traditionally dated to the 150s or 160s, illustrates the mechanism well, arising from local mob agitation during a civic festival rather than central directive, with the Roman proconsul himself shown in the surviving martyrdom account as initially reluctant and offering Polycarp repeated opportunities to recant before yielding to crowd pressure — and persisted, with only partial exceptions, into the mid-third century. The first clearly empire-wide persecutions, breaking decisively from this pattern of local contingency, came under Decius (250–251) and, after a partial respite, Valerian (257–260), both issued against a backdrop of severe third-century military, fiscal, and political crisis in which the traditional gods' favour was widely held to be implicated in the empire's fortunes and universal sacrifice could plausibly be presented, by emperors themselves under acute existential pressure, as a unifying measure of restored cosmic and civic order rather than an anti-Christian measure specifically — Decius's edict required sacrifice of the entire population, with *libelli* (certificates) issued to those who complied, and Christians were simply the most conspicuous and theologically committed group of non-compliers caught within a measure not, in its original conception, aimed at them alone. The Decian persecution proved traumatic for the institutional church in a way earlier, more localised episodes had not, precisely because of its systematic reach, and it generated the first major internal Christian institutional crisis treated in Part III of this overview: the question of how to handle the *lapsi*, those who had complied with the sacrifice requirement, once the persecution ended and they sought reconciliation and readmission to the church — a controversy whose North African manifestation under Cyprian of Carthage would echo directly into the Donatist schism. The final and most severe persecution, under Diocletian and his colleagues beginning in 303 — the so-called Great Persecution — combined demands for sacrifice with the destruction of church buildings and scriptures and the removal of clergy from office, and was pursued with markedly variable severity across the empire's different regions depending on the dispositions of the particular co-emperor responsible (severely in the eastern territories under Galerius, considerably more mildly in the western territories under Constantius), before being formally ended by the Edict of Toleration issued by Galerius in 311 and definitively superseded by the settlement Constantine and Licinius reached at Milan in 313, the subject of the next Part of this overview.

The cumulative numerical toll of pre-Constantinian persecution, it should be said plainly, was very probably smaller than later Christian martyrological memory — itself a genre with its own literary conventions, theological agenda, and tendency toward retrospective elaboration, embellishment, and outright invention of martyr accounts in subsequent centuries — would suggest; the most severe episodes were geographically and temporally concentrated rather than continuous, and considerable stretches of the second and earlier third centuries saw Christian communities living in relative, if legally precarious, peace. This is a corrective to be stated carefully rather than triumphantly: it does not diminish the real suffering and real courage of those who were in fact martyred, nor does it license treating the apologetic and martyrological literature this experience generated as merely propagandistic, but it does mean the older image of near-continuous empire-wide bloodshed, still current in some popular and confessional historiography, requires correction against the actual documentary pattern.

## The Apologists: Defending the Faith to a Hostile World

The persecution and, more pervasively, the everyday social suspicion and misunderstanding Christians faced generated, from the mid-second century onward, a distinct literary genre — Christian apologetic — addressed, at least in form, to Roman emperors, governors, or educated pagan opinion generally, defending Christianity against the specific charges in circulation (atheism, for refusing the traditional gods; immorality, for the rumours of incestuous and cannibalistic gatherings noted above; civic disloyalty, for refusing sacrifice) and increasingly, as the genre matured, making a positive case for Christianity's superiority to pagan philosophy and religion on the latter's own intellectual terms.

Justin Martyr, a former pagan philosopher converted to Christianity who continued to wear the philosopher's cloak after his conversion and who was martyred in Rome around 165, represents the genre's most philosophically ambitious early phase: his *First Apology*, addressed nominally to the emperor Antoninus Pius, combines rebuttal of the standard charges with a striking theological move — the argument, developed through his concept of the *logos spermatikos* ("seminal word" or "seed of reason"), that the divine Logos who became incarnate in Christ had been partially and fragmentarily active in pre-Christian philosophy as well, such that figures like Socrates could be considered, in some sense, proto-Christians avant la lettre, reasoning by the same divine Logos later fully revealed in Christ. This move allowed Justin to claim continuity with, rather than wholesale rejection of, the Hellenistic philosophical culture his educated readers valued, establishing a pattern of selective appropriation rather than blanket rejection that would prove durably influential for the relationship between Christian theology and classical learning across the patristic period. Tertullian of Carthage, writing in Latin around the turn of the third century and considerably more pugnacious in temperament, took a sharper line in works like the *Apologeticum*, mounting the procedural critique of Trajan's rescript noted above while also, in other writings, expressing a more thoroughgoing suspicion of philosophy's value for Christian truth (his rhetorical question "what has Athens to do with Jerusalem?", though probably more rhetorical flourish than a considered systematic rejection of all philosophical reasoning, has nonetheless become proverbial as shorthand for a more separatist strand within early Christian intellectual culture, standing in real tension with Justin's more synthetic approach). Origen of Alexandria, active in the first half of the third century and treated more fully in Part III for his role in subsequent doctrinal controversy, represents the genre's most sophisticated late flowering in his *Contra Celsum*, a point-by-point rebuttal of the pagan philosopher Celsus's earlier critique of Christianity (itself surviving substantially only through Origen's extensive quotation) that engages seriously with Celsus's philosophical objections rather than merely asserting Christian superiority, and that illustrates how thoroughly the more intellectually ambitious wing of the apologetic tradition had absorbed and was prepared to argue within the conventions of contemporary Hellenistic philosophical debate.

## Institutional Consolidation Under Pressure

The same period that produced the apologetic literature also saw the church's internal institutional structures consolidate in ways that cannot be fully separated from the external pressures discussed above, even though the fuller treatment of how episcopal authority and apostolic succession came to be theorised belongs properly to Part II of this overview. The letters of Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, written while under guard en route to martyrdom in Rome around the early second century, already show a vigorous insistence on the authority of a single bishop (as against a more diffuse earlier pattern of plural, less differentiated communal leadership visible in the earliest sources) as the guarantor of unity against division — a structural development plausibly accelerated, rather than merely coincidental with, the experience of external pressure and persecution, since a clearly identified single leader, capable of providing both doctrinal coherence against the proliferating alternative Christianities (Gnostic and otherwise, treated in Part III) and a recognised point of contact and decision under conditions of legal precarity and occasional crisis, offered practical institutional advantages that a more diffuse leadership structure could not as readily supply. By the end of the period covered in this chapter, the church that would inherit the transformed legal status Constantine's settlement conferred in the early fourth century was already, however unevenly across different regions, organised around the episcopal structure, equipped with an articulate apologetic literature engaging seriously with the wider intellectual culture of its world, and possessed of a martyrological memory — whatever its precise scale — that would supply some of its most durable founding narratives.

## The Martyr's Grave and the Origins of the Cult of Saints

That martyrological memory was not merely literary. From a very early date — attested already in the account of Polycarp's death, where his community is described gathering the bishop's bones after his burning and venerating them as "more precious than costly gems" — the physical remains of martyrs, and the sites of their deaths and burial, became objects of intense and practically oriented veneration that constitutes the earliest stratum of what would develop into the fully elaborated Catholic and Orthodox cult of saints. Understanding this development historically requires stepping back from both the devotional tradition's own self-presentation and the Reformation's later polemical account of it, since both distort the picture in opposite directions: the former tends to present saint veneration as an obvious and unproblematic extension of Christian belief in the communion of the living and the dead, while the latter tends to present it as straightforward idolatry or pagan contamination — and neither account adequately captures what the historical evidence actually shows about why this form of devotion emerged when and how it did, and what needs it served.

The historian Peter Brown has provided what remains the most influential scholarly account, in his 1981 study *The Cult of the Saints*, and his argument deserves extended summary here because it illuminates a dynamic that runs through the entire subsequent history of popular Christian devotion. Brown's central claim is that the cult of the saints addressed a fundamental problem in the Christian theology of God that the intellectual, ecclesiastically educated tradition tended to underestimate or simply not see: the God of Christian theology is, by definition, utterly transcendent, infinite, and absolutely distinct from the created order — a being of such incomparable otherness that direct, personal, locally specific access to this God through prayer and supplication is, at the level of popular religious experience, not easy to sustain or feel confident in. The converts whom Christianity drew in increasing numbers across the second and third centuries had, in most cases, grown up within a religious world of extraordinary local and specific divine presence: the pagan religious landscape of the Roman empire was populated with divine patrons, local numina attached to particular springs, trees, crossroads, and cities, household gods with intimate daily relevance to family life, and above all divine intermediaries — heroised dead figures, divine men, and wonder-workers — who served as accessible, this-worldly channels through which the divine could be petitioned on specific, immediate, human concerns. The Christian God absorbed and superseded this entire world theologically while leaving, at the level of lived religious need, a very large vacancy.

The martyr's grave filled that vacancy in a way that was psychologically and functionally continuous with the divine presence the pagan religious landscape had provided, while being genuinely and distinctively Christian in its theological interpretation. The martyr was understood, from the earliest accounts, to be present at the place of their burial in a manner that differed from the ordinary Christian dead: the *dies natalis*, the anniversary of the martyr's death, was celebrated as a birthday into eternal life rather than mourned as a terminus, and Christians gathered at the tomb for prayer, the Eucharist, and the *refrigerium* — a commemorative meal at the graveside that had deep roots in Roman funerary custom and that the church accepted and gradually baptised into Christian form rather than simply prohibiting. The martyr at their grave was present, available, and capable of interceding with God on behalf of the living — a patron saint in the original, social sense of *patronus*, the powerful figure who mediates access to higher authority on behalf of those who cannot approach it directly, a role entirely intelligible within the Roman social world's own patron-client structures. To petition the martyr at their tomb was, within this framework, not to worship the saint but to enlist a powerful heavenly advocate; the distinction between the veneration due to the saint (*dulia*, in the later scholastic terminology) and the worship due to God alone (*latreia*) was the doctrinal expression of a real experiential distinction between the accessible, locally present holy dead and the transcendent God they had access to.

The geographical specificity of this devotion is important and connects directly to Brown's broader argument: saints were embedded in places, and places were embedded in communities. The shrine of a martyr was a point of contact between the earthly and heavenly worlds anchored in a particular city or region, and the relationship between a community and its patron saint was in many respects the Christian transformation of the relationship between a city and its divine patron — a transposition that made the transition from paganism to Christianity, for populations living through it, considerably less experientially disruptive than the intellectual content of the two traditions' respective theologies would suggest. When Augustine's mother Monica was found sharing a meal and wine at the shrine of the martyrs in Carthage, and when Ambrose of Milan asked her to stop — partly to distinguish Christian practice from the pagan funerary meals it resembled too closely — he was confronting precisely this zone of overlap between the new religion's devotional forms and the old religion's functional equivalents. That Monica's practice was widespread and deeply felt is as significant a historical datum as Ambrose's discomfort with it.

The post-Constantinian period saw the cult of saints expand dramatically in both institutional and geographical reach, as the church acquired the legal standing and the material resources to build permanent shrine churches over martyrs' tombs — the great basilicas of Rome over the tombs of Peter, Paul, and the other martyrs constituting the most prominent examples — and as the translation of relics (the transfer of martyr remains or their portions from original burial sites to new shrines) allowed the cult's geography to extend beyond the cities where martyrdoms had originally occurred. The relic trade that followed — with its attendant problems of fraud, competition, and the theological awkwardness of a market in holy body parts — was the inevitable consequence of a devotional system in which physical proximity to the saint's remains was understood to matter, and in which the demand for locally present sacred power far outran the supply of original martyrdom sites. By the time the period of persecution ended under Constantine, the cult of saints was already so deeply embedded in Christian devotional life that the Constantinian church did not so much introduce it as inherit and elaborate it — and the subsequent mediaeval development of canonisation processes, indulgences attached to shrine visits, and the elaborate hierarchies of intercession and patronage that Reformation critics would later identify as the most egregious departures from authentic Christianity were not inventions of the mediaeval church but developments of devotional forms already substantially in place in the third and fourth centuries.

This is the context the reader needs to carry forward into the Crusades chapter's discussion of the pilgrimage impulse that helped generate crusading enthusiasm, into the mediaeval popular religion that the Cathars rejected as corrupt, and into the Reformation's assault on what it characterised as the idolatrous apparatus of saint veneration — an assault the historical evidence suggests was directed at something more deeply and durably embedded in the Christian tradition than most Reformation polemic acknowledged. It is also the context in which the Saints Annex at the end of this overview's final file should be read: not as a catalogue of picturesque medieval folklore, but as the record of a devotional system with deep roots in the first centuries of Christian history and a continuous functional logic across nearly two millennia of practice.

It is to the doctrinal and scriptural foundations underlying the church's structure — the question of which texts counted as authoritative scripture, and what the church actually believed those texts to teach about God, Christ, and salvation — that Part II of this overview now turns.
