# Chapter 33 — The Wars of Religion and the Peace of Westphalia

## The Confessionalisation of Violence

The century between the beginning of the Reformation and the conclusion of the Peace of Westphalia in 1648 was the most violent in the history of Western Christianity since the barbarian invasions of the fifth century, and its violence was inextricable from the specific religious divisions examined in the preceding chapters in ways that defy both the older confessional narratives — which presented each side's violence as righteous defence of truth against diabolical error — and the more recent tendency to dissolve religious motivation entirely into geopolitical interest. The Wars of Religion were not simply wars in which religion provided ideological cover for conflicts whose real drivers were dynastic, territorial, and economic; nor were they crusades in which pure theological conviction generated violence independently of material interest. They were wars in which religious identity, political allegiance, territorial ambition, dynastic survival, and economic interest were so thoroughly fused that the question of which "really" caused the violence is malformed: the participants experienced the religious and the political as aspects of a single reality rather than as separable variables, and the historian who privileges one at the expense of the other distorts what the evidence shows.

The process that historians have called "confessionalisation" — the hardening of the three major confessions (Lutheran, Reformed, and Catholic) into distinct, institutionally defined communities with clear boundaries, precise doctrinal statements, disciplinary mechanisms for enforcing conformity, and political identities that made confessional allegiance and political loyalty structurally convergent — was itself a driver of conflict as much as a response to it. As each confession became more precisely defined and more institutionally coherent across the middle decades of the sixteenth century, the space for the ambiguous, doctrinally fluid Christianity that had characterised much of the earlier reform period contracted, and the communities thus defined increasingly experienced those outside their boundaries as not merely theologically incorrect but as existential threats to the divinely ordered society each confession understood itself to be building.

## The French Wars of Religion

France in the second half of the sixteenth century experienced the most sustained and most destructive confessional civil war in Western European history, the Wars of Religion (1562–1598) in which eight separate conflicts over thirty-six years killed perhaps three million people — through direct violence, famine, and disease associated with military campaigning — and brought the French state to the verge of collapse before the accidental resolution of the succession crisis by Henri of Navarre's conversion to Catholicism and promulgation of the Edict of Nantes produced an uneasy settlement.

The specific character of French confessional conflict reflected the specific social geography of French Calvinism. The Huguenots — the French Reformed community that had grown rapidly across the 1550s under the influence of Genevan missionaries and the protection of significant sections of the French nobility — represented at their height perhaps ten to fifteen percent of the French population but a considerably higher proportion of the nobility, including several of the greatest noble houses. This aristocratic strength gave the Huguenot movement military capacity that the French crown could not simply suppress, as it had suppressed earlier evangelical movements, but it also meant that the Wars of Religion were simultaneously wars of religion and wars of noble faction, in which the specific grievances of noble families against the Valois monarchy were expressed in confessional terms that were not merely cynical but were genuinely felt alongside the material interests they served.

The most notorious single episode of the French Wars of Religion was the St Bartholomew's Day Massacre of August 1572, in which the assassination of the Huguenot military leader Coligny, planned by the queen mother Catherine de Medici and the Guise faction, triggered a wave of popular Catholic violence against Huguenots assembled in Paris for the wedding of Henri of Navarre that killed several thousand in the capital and spread across France in the following weeks to kill perhaps ten to thirty thousand more across the country. The massacre was celebrated in Rome with a Te Deum and a commemorative medal struck by Pope Gregory XIII — a response whose moral character requires no editorial gloss — and it generated in Huguenot political theory the most sophisticated early modern arguments for the right of resistance to tyrannical rulers, the *monarchomach* tradition that fed directly into later political theory about the limits of sovereign authority.

The Edict of Nantes (1598), promulgated by Henri IV after his conversion from Protestantism to Catholicism — "Paris is worth a mass," the remark attributed to him capturing the pragmatic calculation behind the conversion, though he appears to have been a genuine if not especially doctrinally precise Christian — granted the Huguenots substantial rights of worship, civil equality, and the garrisoning of designated strongholds (*places de sûreté*), creating a state of confessional coexistence that was not toleration in any principled sense but a practical settlement of irresolvable conflict. Its revocation by Louis XIV in 1685 — the Edict of Fontainebleau, which abolished Huguenot rights and triggered the emigration of perhaps two hundred thousand French Protestants to England, the Dutch Republic, Prussia, and South Africa — illustrated both the fragility of settlement based on royal concession rather than principled rights and the persistence of the confessional logic of uniformity well into the era when its practical costs were in other contexts beginning to generate arguments for toleration.

## The Thirty Years War: Europe's Catastrophe

The Thirty Years War (1618–1648) was the most destructive conflict in European history before the twentieth century, killing perhaps a third of the population of the Holy Roman Empire through a combination of direct military violence, plague, and famine generated by the systematic destruction of agricultural infrastructure, and reducing some regions — Pomerania, parts of the Rhineland, much of Württemberg — to demographic conditions from which recovery took a century. It began as a conflict with unmistakably religious dimensions and ended as something considerably more complex, and the trajectory from the Defenestration of Prague in 1618 to the Peace of Westphalia in 1648 illustrates with unusual clarity how confessional conflict and dynastic-geopolitical interest interpenetrated across the period.

The war's origins lay in the specific constitutional and confessional tensions of the Holy Roman Empire that the Peace of Augsburg had papered over rather than resolved. The Augsburg settlement's exclusion of the Reformed (Calvinist) confession had created a permanently unstable situation in which the rapidly expanding Reformed community — which had grown through the conversion of several important territorial rulers, including the Elector of the Palatinate, from Lutheranism in the later sixteenth century — had no legal protection and whose very existence was contested. The *Ecclesiastical Reservation* clause of Augsburg, which required ecclesiastical princes who converted to Protestantism to resign their sees, had been unevenly enforced and was a permanent source of conflict over the secularisation of former church properties. And the specific crisis that triggered the war — the revolt of the Bohemian Protestant estates against the incoming emperor Ferdinand II's attempt to reimpose Catholicism, and the election of the Calvinist Elector Palatine Frederick V as king of Bohemia — combined religious, constitutional, and dynastic dimensions in proportions that were, even then, genuinely difficult to disentangle.

The war's phases — the Bohemian phase (1618–1625), the Danish phase (1625–1629), the Swedish phase (1630–1635), and the French phase (1635–1648) — each involved the entry of new powers whose motivations were increasingly geopolitical rather than confessional. France's entry in 1635, under the direction of Cardinal Richelieu, on the side of the Protestant powers against the Catholic Habsburgs, illustrated with particular clarity the limits of purely confessional analysis: France was fighting to prevent Habsburg hegemony over Europe, and the religious affiliations of its allies were strategically rather than theologically significant. Sweden's intervention under Gustavus Adolphus combined genuine Lutheran piety — the Swedish king understood himself as the champion of the Protestant cause with real personal conviction — with the strategic imperative of establishing Swedish dominance around the Baltic littoral. The emperor's armies, fighting under the banner of Catholic reconquest in the early phases, were increasingly Habsburg dynastic instruments as the war progressed.

The devastation that the war produced in German-speaking territories had no precedent in the historical memory of those who survived it and generated a cultural trauma — visible in the literature of the period, above all the picaresque novel *Simplicissimus* (1668) of Grimmelshausen, whose protagonist's odyssey through the war's violence constitutes one of the most powerful literary responses to mass atrocity in Western European literature — whose consequences shaped German political culture's relationship to war and state power for generations.

## The Peace of Westphalia and Its Mythology

The Peace of Westphalia — concluded in October 1648 through two simultaneously negotiated treaties, the Peace of Osnabrück (between the empire and Sweden) and the Peace of Münster (between the empire and France, and separately between Spain and the Dutch Republic) — has acquired in subsequent historiography and international relations theory a mythological status as the founding document of the modern international system of sovereign states, the origin of principles of state sovereignty and non-interference in internal affairs that are still invoked in twenty-first century international law and diplomacy. This myth requires careful qualification, both because it overstates what the treaties actually established and because it attributes to seventeenth-century diplomats conceptual innovations they did not clearly intend.

What the treaties actually settled, in their religious provisions, was an extension and modification of the Augsburg system: the Reformed confession was finally given legal recognition alongside Lutheranism and Catholicism, resolving the exclusion that had been a permanent source of tension since 1555; the normative year 1624 was established as the baseline for determining the confessional status of each territory, effectively freezing the confessional map at that point and preventing further territorial confessionalisation in either direction; and certain mixed-confession territories were given provisions for the coexistence of Catholic and Protestant communities rather than being required to adopt the confession of their ruler. These were significant modifications of the Augsburg settlement, but they were modifications of an existing imperial constitutional framework rather than the invention of a new international order.

The broader significance attributed to Westphalia — the establishment of state sovereignty and non-interference as general principles of international relations — is a retrospective construction that reflects the needs of later international lawyers and theorists more than the intentions of the Westphalian negotiators themselves. The treaties did not clearly articulate a principle of sovereign non-interference; they were deeply entangled with the imperial constitutional order and with the intervention rights that the major powers — France, Sweden — had specifically secured for themselves as guarantors of the settlement. The "Westphalian system" is a useful shorthand for a certain model of international order, but its attribution to 1648 as a definitive founding moment requires the qualification that it describes a trajectory more than a single event.

What Westphalia did represent, in its religious dimensions, was not the triumph of toleration but the exhaustion of confessional violence. The settlement did not embody a principled commitment to religious liberty — individuals within territories were still subject to the confession of their ruler with only limited protections, and the settlement explicitly excluded certain Christian minorities from its provisions — but it did represent the recognition, hard-won through three decades of catastrophic violence, that confessional uniformity imposed by force was beyond the means of any party to the conflict to achieve and that some form of regulated coexistence was the only alternative to permanent war. This was pragmatic settlement rather than principled toleration, and the distinction matters: the road from Westphalia to genuine religious liberty was long, and the arguments for toleration that would eventually travel it came not from the diplomats of 1648 but from philosophers, theologians, and political thinkers — John Locke, Pierre Bayle, Baruch Spinoza — whose arguments the following century would begin to institutionalise.

## The Legacy for Christianity

The Wars of Religion's legacy for Western Christianity was profound and in some respects irreversible. The spectacle of Christian communities engaged in sustained, catastrophic violence against one another — legitimised by theological argument, prosecuted with pastoral sanction, and producing suffering on a scale that no subsequent account of Christian civilisation could simply ignore — generated in educated European opinion a scepticism about the church's claims to moral authority that the subsequent Enlightenment critique would systematise into a comprehensive challenge. If Christianity produced the Wars of Religion, the argument ran, then Christianity was not the guarantor of civil peace and moral order it claimed to be but a generator of conflict requiring external, secular management — an argument whose force the churches could not entirely rebut and whose institutional consequences included the gradual transfer of moral and intellectual authority from ecclesiastical to secular institutions that characterises the subsequent history of European culture.

The specific Westphalian settlement's contribution to this trajectory was to establish, in constitutional and legal terms, the principle that the management of religious diversity was a problem for political rather than ecclesiastical institutions — that the coexistence of multiple confessions was a political fact to be regulated by treaty and law rather than a theological error to be resolved by force or conversion. This was not yet secularism in the modern sense, but it was a significant step toward the institutional separation of religious authority from political management of religious diversity that the Enlightenment would push considerably further. The trajectory from Westphalia to Locke's *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) to the American First Amendment (1791) is not a straight line, but it is a genuine trajectory, and the exhaustion of the confessional violence that Westphalia registered was one of its necessary conditions.
