# Chapter 31 — The Catholic Counter-Reformation: Trent, the Jesuits, and Discipline Restored

## Naming the Movement

The term "Counter-Reformation" carries an implicit historiographical argument: it suggests that the Catholic reform of the sixteenth century was primarily reactive, a response to the Protestant challenge rather than an independent religious movement with its own internal dynamics and its own roots in the pre-Reformation church's traditions of self-criticism and renewal. The rival term "Catholic Reformation," preferred by many twentieth-century historians, emphasises the continuity between sixteenth-century Catholic reform and the reforming energies already present in the late mediaeval church — the conciliarist tradition, the Devotio Moderna, the Observant movements within the mendicant orders, the Erasmian humanist critique — and resists the implication that everything the Catholic church did in this period was simply a response to Protestant pressure. Both terms capture something real. The Catholic reform was genuinely and independently motivated: the Council of Trent was called before the Protestant threat had reached its full dimensions, the Jesuits were founded in a spiritual climate that owed more to late mediaeval mysticism and Ignatian prayer than to any anti-Lutheran agenda, and the renewal of Catholic pastoral and devotional life that the period produced had its own authenticity independent of its polemical context. And the movement was also, genuinely, counter-reformatory: Trent's definitions of doctrine were shaped by the need to respond to specific Protestant challenges, the Index of Forbidden Books was directed primarily at Protestant literature, and the Inquisitions of the period were deployed with heightened energy against Protestant-influenced currents within Catholic territories. Holding both characterisations simultaneously is the accurate historical position.

## The Council of Trent: Reform and Definition

The Council of Trent (1545–1563), convened by Pope Paul III after decades of pressure from within and without the church for a reforming general council, was the most significant Catholic ecclesiastical gathering since the Council of Constance and the longest-running council in the church's history, its sessions interrupted by plague, political crisis, and papal-imperial conflict and spread across eighteen years in three distinct phases. Its achievement was twofold and in some respects internally contradictory: it produced genuine institutional reform of the abuses that reformers had identified across centuries, and it defined Catholic doctrine in response to Protestant challenges with a precision and a rigidity that made subsequent dialogue between the confessions considerably more difficult.

On the reform side, Trent addressed with real seriousness the structural abuses the reformers had identified: episcopal residence was required (absent bishops unable to supervise their dioceses had been a standard complaint for centuries); plural benefices were restricted; seminary education for the clergy was mandated, creating the institutional basis for the long-term improvement of clerical standards; the administration of the sacraments was regulated; and the supervision of religious orders was tightened. These reforms were genuine and had real long-term consequences for the quality and coherence of Catholic institutional life — the Catholic clergy of the seventeenth century, formed in the Tridentine seminaries, was on the whole considerably better educated and more pastorally effective than its pre-Reformation predecessors, a transformation whose significance for the survival and recovery of Catholicism in contested territories is difficult to overstate.

On the doctrinal side, Trent's definitions were constructed in deliberate opposition to specific Protestant positions, and the resulting formulations hardened positions that the pre-Tridentine Catholic tradition had left more fluid. On scripture and tradition, the council affirmed the equal authority of both against the Protestant *sola scriptura* principle, and defined the Latin Vulgate as the authoritative biblical text for the Western church — a decision whose scholarly problems were immediately apparent to the humanist-trained theologians in attendance but whose institutional logic was clear: the vernacular Bible translations associated with Protestant evangelism were implicitly countered by the assertion of the Latin text's authority. On justification, the most theologically complex and most hotly contested of the council's doctrinal decrees, Trent produced a sophisticated statement that affirmed the necessity of grace, the reality of faith's role in justification, and the genuine interior transformation that justification effects, while explicitly rejecting the Lutheran doctrines of imputed righteousness and the bondage of the will — a decree that some modern ecumenical theologians have argued is less incompatible with Lutheran positions than the sixteenth-century polemical context made it appear, but that in its historical context functioned as a direct refutation of what Luther and his successors had taught. On the sacraments, Trent affirmed the full seven-sacrament system, defined transubstantiation with explicit reference to the Aristotelian substance-accident distinction (using the term in the *Summa Theologiae*'s precise sense), and reaffirmed the sacrificial character of the Mass — the claim that the Eucharistic celebration was a genuine sacrifice repeating or re-presenting the sacrifice of the cross, against the Protestant insistence that the cross was a once-for-all event that no subsequent ritual could add to or repeat. On purgatory, indulgences, the veneration of saints and images, and the authority of tradition alongside scripture, Trent affirmed Catholic positions against their Protestant alternatives with a directness that left little room for subsequent ambiguity.

The council's closure under Pope Pius IV. in 1563 and the promulgation of its decrees by Pius IV and subsequently implemented systematically under Pius V. and Gregory XIII produced what has been called the "Tridentine church" — a Catholicism of considerable institutional coherence, doctrinal precision, and pastoral organisation, but also one considerably more uniform, more centralised, and more resistant to the kind of internal diversity that had characterised pre-Reformation Catholicism. The liturgy was standardised in the Roman Rite of 1570 (the "Tridentine Mass"), which suppressed the regional liturgical variations that had existed across the mediaeval Western church; the catechism was standardised in the Roman Catechism of 1566; and the Index of Forbidden Books, maintained with increasing rigour, defined the boundaries of permissible Catholic intellectual life in ways that would have significant long-term consequences for the relationship between Catholic culture and the emerging scientific and philosophical movements of the seventeenth century.

## Ignatius of Loyola and the Society of Jesus

The most significant new institutional development of the Catholic Reformation was not a council decree but a religious order: the Society of Jesus, founded by Ignatius of Loyola and formally approved by Pope Paul III in 1540, which became within a generation the most dynamic, most intellectually ambitious, and most controversially effective instrument of Catholic renewal and missionary expansion in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Ignatius (1491–1556) was a Basque nobleman whose early career was that of a soldier and courtier, whose life was redirected by a severe leg wound sustained at the siege of Pamplona in 1521 during which he underwent a prolonged religious conversion of the type — dramatic, experiential, centred on a felt encounter with divine presence — that mystical traditions across the history of Christianity have produced. The *Spiritual Exercises* that he developed from his own conversion experience and subsequent years of prayer and austerity — a structured four-week programme of meditative and contemplative prayer designed to bring the practitioner to a profound orientation of their entire life toward God's will, and still used in its essential form across the global Catholic church today — were the spiritual foundation on which the Society was built, and they give the Jesuits their characteristic spirituality: discernment of spirits, the *magis* (always seeking the greater glory of God, *ad maiorem Dei gloriam*, the order's motto), and a contemplation-in-action that sought God in all things rather than in withdrawal from the world.

The Society of Jesus differed from earlier religious orders in several respects that proved institutionally revolutionary. It abandoned the communal choral recitation of the Divine Office that the Benedictine and Dominican traditions had maintained — freeing its members for more flexible deployment in the active apostolate rather than tying them to a monastery's liturgical schedule. It placed extraordinary emphasis on the formation and education of its members, with a novitiate and a period of studies more demanding and more prolonged than any previous religious order had required, producing men of considerable intellectual accomplishment capable of engaging the best Protestant and humanist scholarship on its own terms. It required an additional vow of special obedience to the pope, placing its members at the direct disposal of the papacy for deployment wherever the church's need was greatest — a provision that made the Jesuits the papacy's most flexible and most trusted instrument for missionary expansion and for the recovery of Protestant-influenced territories. And it elected a superior general with considerable central authority over a geographically dispersed network of provinces, creating an organisational structure capable of coordinating activity across the globe in a way that the territorially anchored traditional orders could not match.

The Jesuits' engagement with the intellectual culture of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was extensive and sophisticated. Their network of colleges — free secondary schools offering humanist education of high quality, established across Europe and eventually worldwide — became one of the most significant educational systems in Western history, educating the Catholic elite across several generations and establishing a model of humanist Catholic education that engaged seriously with classical and contemporary learning rather than retreating behind defensive walls. Their engagement with Protestant theology was equally serious: figures like Peter Canisius, whose catechisms were the Catholic counterpart to Luther's in the German territories, and Robert Bellarmine, whose systematic *Controversiae* engaged Protestant theological positions with a scholarly thoroughness that the polemical tradition had rarely achieved, represented a Catholic intellectual response to the Reformation that went beyond denunciation to genuine engagement.

The Jesuit missions outside Europe, examined further in Chapter 34's treatment of Christianity and empire, brought the order into contact with civilisations — China, Japan, India, the Americas — where the question of how Christianity related to non-Christian cultures was posed with a sharpness and a practical urgency it had never previously faced in Europe, and where the Jesuits' characteristic intellectual flexibility led them to adaptations — Matteo Ricci's accommodation of Chinese ritual practice, the toleration of caste distinctions in India — that generated bitter controversy within the Catholic church and eventually led, in the Chinese Rites Controversy (condemned by Pope Clement XI in 1715), to a papal decision whose consequences for the development of Catholicism in China were both far-reaching and, by most subsequent assessments, disastrous.

The Society's extraordinary success and influence also generated the intense hostility and suspicion that surrounded it from an early date. "Jesuit" became, in Protestant polemical usage, a byword for intellectual duplicity and political manipulation — the doctrine of mental reservation (which permitted the withholding of the full truth in specified circumstances), the casuistical approach to moral theology (the application of general principles to specific cases with considerable flexibility), and the order's deployment in the courts and confessional boxes of Catholic rulers generated charges of hypocrisy, manipulation, and a willingness to use any means for ostensibly good ends. Blaise Pascal's *Lettres Provinciales* (1656–1657), the most brilliant literary attack on Jesuit casuistry, embedded the association of "Jesuit" with moral slipperiness in French cultural memory in a way from which the order has never entirely recovered. The eventual suppression of the Jesuits by Pope Clement XIV in 1773 — under pressure from the Bourbon crowns of France, Spain, and Portugal, which had found the order's independence from national ecclesiastical control increasingly intolerable — was the most dramatic institutional collapse in the Catholic church's modern history, and the order's subsequent restoration in 1814 has not entirely erased its memory.

## The Spanish Inquisition

The Spanish Inquisition — the institution properly called the *Tribunal of the Holy Office of the Inquisition* as established in Spain in 1478 by Ferdinand and Isabella and operating with distinctive characteristics that distinguished it from the mediaeval Inquisition examined in Chapter 22 — requires separate treatment both because of its importance and because of the substantial mythology that surrounds it in both directions: the Protestant "Black Legend" that made it the supreme symbol of Catholic tyranny and bigotry, and the revisionist overcorrection that has sometimes, in reacting against the legend, understated the genuine scale and genuine harm of what the institution did.

The Spanish Inquisition was, unlike the mediaeval Inquisition, a royal rather than primarily papal institution: it was established under royal authority, its inquisitors were royal appointments, and the proceeds of its confiscations went to the crown rather than to the church — a character that made it an instrument of royal policy as well as religious conformity, directed above all in its early decades against the *conversos* (Jewish converts to Christianity whose sincerity of conversion was suspected) and *moriscos* (Muslim converts), and subsequently against Protestant influences, humanist intellectuals, and the various currents of *alumbrado* (illuminist) spirituality that the sixteenth-century Catholic reform generated alongside its more institutionally conventional expressions.

The scale of the Spanish Inquisition's activity has been substantially revised downward by modern historical scholarship, above all by the work of Henry Charles Lea and, more recently, the detailed archival study of Henry Kamen and others working from the inquisitorial records themselves. The total number of executions over the Inquisition's three centuries of active operation has been estimated by careful modern scholarship at between three thousand and five thousand — a figure that is neither negligible nor commensurate with the popular image of an institution whose principal activity was the mass burning of heretics. The majority of those who appeared before the Inquisition received penances, public shaming, or imprisonment rather than death; torture was used but subject to regulations that restricted its application more than popular accounts have generally acknowledged; and the Inquisition's procedures, while deeply unjust by modern standards, were in some respects more regular and more subject to documented appeal than the secular criminal justice of the same period. None of this changes the fundamental moral character of an institution that prosecuted people for their religious beliefs and practices, used torture to extract confession, and burned a significant number of human beings for the crime of heterodoxy. It does mean that the honest assessment requires accuracy rather than either the Black Legend's lurid exaggeration or the revisionist impulse to treat the matter as essentially trivial.

## The Tridentine Settlement and Its Long Shadow

The Catholic Reformation's achievement, taken as a whole, was the consolidation of a Catholicism that was institutionally more disciplined, doctrinally more precise, and pastorally more effective than its pre-Reformation predecessor, at the cost of a rigidity and a uniformity that represented a genuine narrowing of the tradition's earlier breadth. The Tridentine church that emerged from the mid-sixteenth century — with its standardised liturgy, its mandatory clerical celibacy more effectively enforced than ever before, its seminary-trained clergy, its Jesuit intellectuals, its reinvigorated Inquisitions, and its Index of Forbidden Books — was a formidable institution capable of holding its ground in much of southern Europe, recovering significant territories in central Europe through the Catholic Reformation's missionary and educational energy, and extending itself across the Americas and Asia through the great missionary orders.

What it was not was a church capable of the kind of internal reform and intellectual flexibility that might have addressed the deeper structural problems — the relationship between faith and emerging science, the question of political authority and individual conscience, the connection between institutional Catholicism and European intellectual culture — that would define the church's struggles in the following three centuries. The condemnation of Galileo in 1633, the Index's suppression of the Copernican system, and the church's rearguard action against Enlightenment thought examined in Chapter 36, all reflect the Tridentine settlement's characteristic tendency to respond to intellectual challenge with institutional discipline rather than intellectual engagement — a tendency that the Jesuits at their best had resisted but that the institutional church as a whole increasingly reinforced. The long shadow of Trent lay across Catholic intellectual life until the Second Vatican Council of the 1960s began, cautiously and incompletely, to lift it.
