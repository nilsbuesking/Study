# Chapter 36 — Faith Under Siege: Enlightenment Critique, Vatican I, and the Church Against Modernity

## The Enlightenment Challenge: What It Actually Was

The Enlightenment's challenge to Christianity was not simply anti-religious prejudice dressed in philosophical clothing, and treating it as such — as a strand of Catholic apologetic has persistently done, and as some Protestant historiography of "secularisation" has also implied — prevents both an honest assessment of the intellectual challenge's force and an honest reckoning with the churches' often inadequate responses to it. The Enlightenment critique of Christianity was, at its most serious, a sustained philosophical and historical challenge to the specific claims on which the institutional authority of the Christian churches rested: the authority of revelation against the claims of natural reason, the historical reliability of scripture against the emerging methods of historical criticism, the compatibility of traditional Christian cosmology with the results of natural science, and the political authority of the churches over conscience and civic life against the emerging claims of individual rational autonomy and toleration.

Each of these challenges had genuine intellectual substance and could not be answered by repetition of the received tradition's own claims. The development of what is now called biblical higher criticism — the application of the same historical and philological methods that humanist scholars had developed for classical texts to the biblical text itself, asking not what the text meant theologically but what it meant historically, in its original context, to its original authors and audiences — produced results that sat uncomfortably with traditional claims of scriptural inerrancy and divine authorship in their simple forms. The geological and biological sciences developed across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries a picture of an ancient, slowly changing earth and a gradually evolving biosphere that was incompatible with a literal reading of the Genesis creation narratives, and that required either a reinterpretation of those narratives or a defence of literal reading against the weight of accumulating scientific evidence. The historical method applied to the church's own institutional history — its record on slavery, on persecution, on the Galileo affair, on the crusades — produced an evidential base for critique that could not be dismissed as external hostility alone.

The most influential Enlightenment critiques of religion drew on these accumulating challenges to build cases of varying severity. David Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (published posthumously in 1779) subjected the arguments for God's existence, particularly the design argument, to a sceptical analysis of lasting philosophical rigour, and his essay *Of Miracles* argued that no testimony could establish a miracle's credibility against the uniform experience of natural law that constituted the evidence on the other side. Immanuel Kant's critical philosophy demonstrated, in his own formulation, that theoretical reason could neither prove nor disprove God's existence, confining religion to the domain of practical reason and moral necessity rather than theoretical knowledge — a limitation whose implications for traditional metaphysical theology were substantial even though Kant himself retained a commitment to the moral God of rational religion. The French philosophes — Voltaire above all, whose *Candide* and *Dictionnaire philosophique* subjected institutional Christianity's historical record to withering satirical treatment — popularised a critique of the church's political power, its persecution of dissenters, its support for despotism, and its opposition to scientific inquiry that embedded the association of ecclesiastical authority with tyranny and obscurantism in the educated European imagination with consequences that outlasted the specific arguments.

The problem of evil — why an omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good God would permit the enormous suffering that human experience contains — received new philosophical urgency in the Enlightenment period, particularly in the aftermath of the Lisbon earthquake of 1755, which killed tens of thousands of people in one of the most devout Catholic cities in Europe on All Saints' Day while they were in church — an event whose theological implications generated an immediate, intense, and revealing public controversy that Voltaire exploited with devastating rhetorical effect. The theodicy question, already present in patristic and scholastic theology, became in the post-Lisbon intellectual climate something more urgent and more culturally central: a challenge not merely to the philosophical consistency of theism but to the moral credibility of a God who permitted mass suffering of the innocent, and to the institutions that claimed to speak in that God's name.

## The Church's Responses: Reform, Retreat, and Reaction

The Christian churches' responses to the Enlightenment challenge ranged from serious intellectual engagement to defensive withdrawal to political reaction, and the distribution across this spectrum was not uniform across denominations or across the century. Protestant liberalism — the theological movement associated above all with Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768–1834) in Germany, whose *On Religion: Speeches to Its Cultured Despisers* (1799) represented the most sophisticated early Protestant engagement with the Enlightenment's challenge — attempted to reground Christianity not in metaphysical claims or historical evidence that could be disputed by philosophy and science, but in the irreducible experience of religious feeling (*Gefühl*), the sense of absolute dependence on something beyond the self that Schleiermacher argued was a fundamental and universal dimension of human consciousness. This move — relocating religion's validity from the domain of theoretical reason, where Kant had shown it could not be securely established, to the domain of human experience and feeling — proved enormously influential in the subsequent liberal Protestant theological tradition, generating a century of theological development that sought to reconcile Christianity with modern historical scholarship, evolutionary biology, and political liberalism by distinguishing the historically conditioned forms in which Christian truth had been expressed from the underlying experience and moral vision they conveyed.

The Catholic Church's dominant institutional response to the Enlightenment was considerably less accommodating. The French Revolution's assault on the institutional church — the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, the dechristianisation campaigns, the execution of clergy and the closing of churches — had produced a reaction that fused Catholic identity with counter-revolutionary politics in ways that shaped the nineteenth-century papacy's self-understanding as a besieged institution defending revealed truth against the corrosive acids of modern philosophy, liberal politics, and historical criticism. The Syllabus of Errors issued by Pius IX. in 1864 — a list of eighty propositions condemned as errors, including the propositions that "the Roman Pontiff can and should reconcile himself with progress, liberalism, and modern civilisation" — represented the most explicit expression of this posture, and it generated embarrassment even among loyal Catholics who recognised that a formal rejection of accommodation to "progress" and "liberalism" was a public relations catastrophe as well as a theological overreach, condemning categories too broad and too varied to be usefully anathematised.

The papacy's response to the Galileo affair's long shadow — the 1633 condemnation of Galileo for defending the Copernican heliocentric system had become, by the nineteenth century, the single most cited evidence for the institutional church's hostility to scientific inquiry — was to attempt a rehabilitation of natural science within a Thomistic framework while maintaining that the magisterium retained authority over the proper interpretation of biblical texts that appeared to conflict with scientific findings. Leo XIII's encyclical *Providentissimus Deus* (1893) attempted to square this circle by distinguishing between the inspired and inerrant religious and moral content of scripture and the incidental, non-authoritative character of its statements about natural phenomena — a distinction that rational but that left unresolved the question of who determined which statements fell in which category. The same Leo XIII's encyclical *Aeterni Patris* (1879), mandating the revival of Thomistic philosophy as the framework for Catholic intellectual engagement with modernity, represented a more positive if also more retrospective response: rather than simply condemning modern philosophy, it proposed to engage it through the resources of the scholastic tradition, updated and applied to contemporary questions.

## Darwin and the Churches

Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) and *The Descent of Man* (1871) presented the churches with a scientific challenge considerably more fundamental than Galileo's heliocentrism, since evolution by natural selection not only displaced humanity from the centre of a purposively designed cosmos but eliminated the need for a designer in the explanation of the biological world's apparent purposiveness — the argument from design that had been one of the most widely used arguments for God's existence across the previous century lost its force if natural selection could account for the adaptation of organisms to their environments without any superintending intelligence. The popular image of a uniform church reaction of horrified rejection against a uniform scientific community of enlightened acceptance considerably distorts what actually happened. Several senior Anglican clergy were among the earliest and most enthusiastic proponents of evolutionary theory; the confrontation between Bishop Samuel Wilberforce and T.H. Huxley at the 1860 Oxford debate, subsequently legendary in secularist history as a decisive moment of science defeating religion, was in its actual course considerably more ambiguous than the later retrospective construction; and prominent Catholic theologians across the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries found various ways of accommodating evolutionary biology within a theistic framework, including the position — never formally condemned by the magisterium — that evolution could be understood as the means by which God had created the biological world, with the special creation of the human soul preserved as the point of divine intervention within an otherwise evolutionary process.

The more theologically serious long-term challenge of evolutionary biology was not the specific origin of species question but the deeper problem of theodicy that natural selection's mechanisms posed: a process driven by variation, competition, suffering, and death on an enormous scale across hundreds of millions of years was an odd vehicle for a benevolent creator's purposes, and the sheer profligacy of extinction — the vast majority of species that had ever lived had been eliminated — was difficult to square with traditional accounts of providential design. These deeper questions were not resolved in the Victorian confrontation between Darwinians and clerics, and they remain genuinely open in contemporary theology.

## Vatican I: Papal Infallibility and Its Context

The First Vatican Council (1869–1870), convened by Pius IX and interrupted by the Italian army's occupation of Rome that ended the papal temporal states in September 1870, produced two major doctrinal definitions: the constitution *Dei Filius*, which affirmed the compatibility of faith and reason and the reality of natural knowledge of God against the fideist and rationalist extremes, and the constitution *Pastor Aeternus*, which defined the papal primacy and — most controversially — the doctrine of papal infallibility.

The infallibility definition — that the Roman pontiff, when speaking *ex cathedra* (from the chair, in his official capacity as universal shepherd and teacher) on matters of faith and morals, with the intention of binding the whole church, is preserved by divine assistance from error — was considerably more qualified and narrower in its stated form than its critics, then and since, have sometimes treated it. The conditions specified — the formal *ex cathedra* exercise, the specific subject matter of faith and morals, the explicit intention to bind universally — were intended to distinguish this specific form of definitive papal teaching from ordinary papal statements, allocutions, and encyclicals, which were not covered by the definition. The two infallible definitions subsequently issued under the definition's authority — the Immaculate Conception of Mary (1854, technically before the formal definition but subsequently understood as a retroactive exercise of the authority it would codify) and the Assumption of Mary (1950) — illustrate both the narrow practical scope of the definition in use and the specific Marian theology that the nineteenth-century papacy was especially committed to promoting.

The definition's political context was as important as its theological content. Pius IX, whose temporal sovereignty over the Papal States was being systematically dismantled by Italian nationalism and whose relationship with the modern liberal political order was one of uncompromising hostility, was seeking in the definition of infallibility a spiritual authority capable of compensating for the political power being stripped away — a dynamic that the minority of bishops at the Council who opposed the definition's timing, including several of the most capable Catholic theologians of the period, recognised and articulated with considerable frankness. The dissenting minority argued not necessarily against the doctrine's theological content but against the wisdom of defining it at that moment, in those terms, without the broad collegial consultation that a definition of such magnitude required — objections that the subsequent century's experience of the definition's pastoral and ecumenical complications has not rendered obviously wrong.

## Catholic Modernism and Its Condemnation

The early twentieth century saw within Catholicism the most ambitious attempt to engage constructively with historical criticism, evolutionary biology, and modern philosophy that the tradition had yet produced — the movement known as Catholic Modernism, associated with thinkers including Alfred Loisy in France, George Tyrrell in England, and Friedrich von Hügel as its most irenic representative. The Modernists applied the methods of historical criticism to the New Testament and the development of church doctrine with results that disturbed the received understanding of both: Loisy's *L'Évangile et l'Église* (1902), challenging the Protestant liberal position represented by Adolf von Harnack while simultaneously demonstrating, through historical analysis, that the institutional church was a historical development rather than the simple unfolding of Christ's explicit intentions, generated the famous (if perhaps slightly misattributed) formulation that "Jesus announced the Kingdom and it was the Church that came."

Pope Pius X's condemnation of Modernism in the encyclical *Pascendi Dominici Gregis* (1907) and the decree *Lamentabili Sane* (1907) — which condemned sixty-five propositions associated with Modernist scholarship — was followed by the imposition of an anti-modernist oath on all Catholic clergy and seminary teachers that remained in force until 1967, and by a systematic suppression of Modernist scholarship within Catholic institutions that drove several of its leading figures out of the church and created within Catholic intellectual life a climate of suspicion and conformity hostile to genuine scholarly engagement. The condemnation of Modernism was not without genuine theological substance — some Modernist positions did involve a dissolution of Christian truth claims into mere historical processes in ways that compromised the tradition's cognitive content — but the sledgehammer with which those positions were addressed destroyed much that was valuable alongside what was genuinely problematic, and the subsequent history of Catholic theology before Vatican II was shaped by this enforced intellectual caution in ways that the Council itself would eventually have to address.

The story that closes this chapter — of a church confronting modernity largely through defensive reaction, occasional creative engagement, and systematic institutional suppression of the most creative engagement — sets up the dramatic reversal, partial and incomplete as it proved to be, that the following chapter's examination of Vatican II will trace. Between the Syllabus of Errors (1864) and the opening of Vatican II (1962) lay a century of ecclesiastical engagement with the modern world that was, by any honest assessment, more defensive than creative — even as individual thinkers within the tradition, including many whose work was officially discouraged or condemned, were developing the theological resources that the Council would eventually deploy.
