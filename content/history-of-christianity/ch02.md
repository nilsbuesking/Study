# Chapter 2 — Paul, the Gentile Mission, and the Earliest Communities

## The Earliest Jerusalem Community

Before turning to Paul, the movement he joined needs brief characterisation in its own right, since later Pauline dominance of the New Testament canon has a tendency to obscure how the earliest post-Easter community actually organised itself. The book of Acts, written by the same author as the Gospel of Luke and probably in the 80s or early 90s CE, presents a Jerusalem-centred community under the leadership of Peter and, increasingly as the narrative progresses, James "the brother of the Lord" — a figure whose prominence is independently corroborated by Paul's own letters, where James appears as one of the "pillars" of the Jerusalem church alongside Peter (called Cephas in Paul's Aramaic-derived usage) and John the son of Zebedee. Acts' account needs to be read with some caution as a retrospective and theologically shaped narrative — composed, like the Gospels, well after the events it describes, by an author with clear interests in presenting the movement's expansion as providentially guided and in smoothing over tensions that Paul's own letters suggest were considerably sharper in practice — but its broad outline of a Jerusalem-based leadership in the movement's first years is not seriously disputed.

This earliest community remained, by its own self-understanding, thoroughly within Second Temple Judaism rather than a separate religion: its members continued to worship at the Temple, observed Jewish law including circumcision and dietary regulation, and understood themselves as the nucleus of restored, eschatological Israel rather than as founders of a new faith superseding the old one — a self-understanding crucial to bear in mind given how thoroughly later Christian theology would reverse this relationship. The community held some form of communal property arrangement, according to Acts' early chapters, though the precise extent and permanence of this arrangement is debated and may reflect an idealised retrospective gloss as much as sustained practice. Internal tension is visible even within Acts' generally harmonising account: the dispute over food distribution to "Hellenist" (Greek-speaking, probably diaspora-background) widows as against "Hebrew" (Aramaic-speaking, Judaean) widows in Acts 6 points to a Jerusalem community already divided along linguistic and cultural lines, and the subsequent martyrdom of Stephen, a Hellenist leader, at the hands of a Jerusalem crowd — followed, on Acts' account, by a wave of persecution that scattered the Hellenist wing of the movement out of Jerusalem while leaving the apostles themselves apparently undisturbed — suggests that the more Hellenised, diaspora-oriented strand of the early movement was perceived as considerably more threatening to Jewish religious sensibilities than its more conservative, Temple-observant core, plausibly because the Hellenists' looser relationship to Temple and Torah more readily incurred charges of blasphemy of the kind Acts attributes to Stephen's trial.

## Saul of Tarsus

Paul's own letters are the earliest surviving Christian documents of any kind, predating all four canonical Gospels, and they are also, unusually for ancient figures of his importance, substantially autobiographical in patches, which gives the historian a degree of direct access to Paul's self-presentation not available for Jesus. Born Saul, a Jew of the tribe of Benjamin holding Roman citizenship — itself indicative of some standing, since citizenship was not universal among provincial Jews — and raised, on his own testimony in Philippians and Galatians, as a Pharisee zealous for ancestral tradition, Paul states plainly that he had persecuted the early church before his apostolic call, a detail multiply attested across his letters and one no later hagiographer would have had reason to invent, satisfying the embarrassment criterion discussed in the previous chapter about as cleanly as any datum concerning Paul exists. Acts locates this persecuting activity and the subsequent conversion experience on the road to Damascus, with Paul travelling to extend the persecution beyond Jerusalem; Paul's own account in Galatians is considerably more terse, describing simply that God "was pleased to reveal his Son" to him and called him to proclaim Christ among the Gentiles, without Acts' vivid narrative details of blinding light and voice, which appear in three separate, not perfectly consistent retellings within Acts itself, a divergence form critics have long taken as evidence of Luke's own literary shaping of an underlying tradition rather than verbatim historical reportage.

The traditional designation of this event as a "conversion" has been increasingly questioned in recent scholarship, particularly since the 1976 publication of Krister Stendahl's influential essay reframing it instead as a prophetic "call" — Paul did not, on this reading, abandon Judaism for a different religion, since no such distinct religious category yet existed in his conceptual world, but rather received a commission, analogous to the calls of Hebrew prophets such as Jeremiah and Isaiah, to a specific task: proclaiming the crucified and risen Messiah to the Gentile nations. This reframing has become broadly influential within the academic field generally associated with the label "Paul within Judaism," which emphasises Paul's continued self-identification as a Jew throughout his subsequent career against an older historiographical tradition — itself entangled, not coincidentally, with the longer history of Christian supersessionism examined in later chapters of this overview — that read Paul as having decisively broken with and superseded Jewish particularism in favour of a universal gospel. The dating of the call itself is anchored with unusual precision for ancient biography by Paul's own chronological references in Galatians, cross-referenced against external synchronisms (notably a reference to King Aretas IV of Nabataea in 2 Corinthians, and the Gallio inscription from Delphi fixing Paul's appearance before the Achaean proconsul Gallio to around 51–52 CE), allowing scholars to place the Damascus event in the early-to-mid 30s CE — remarkably close, on most reckonings, to the crucifixion itself, underscoring how early and rapidly the movement's claims about the risen Christ had spread beyond the Jerusalem nucleus.

## The Gentile Mission and Its Theological Logic

The central and most historically consequential feature of Paul's apostolic career was his conviction, which he presents as integral to his original call rather than a later development, that the gospel concerning the crucified and risen Messiah was to be proclaimed to Gentiles (non-Jews) without requiring them first to become Jews — that is, without requiring circumcision for men or observance of the full Mosaic law, including dietary restrictions and Sabbath observance, as a precondition of full membership in the community of the saved. This was not the only possible position available within the early movement, and Paul's letters, particularly Galatians, make clear that it was sharply contested by other Jewish-Christian figures (Paul calls them, polemically, "false brothers" and elsewhere associates a rival position with "the circumcision faction") who held that Gentile converts needed to be circumcised and observe Torah to be included in the Abrahamic covenant that, on their reading, Jesus's messiahship had not abolished but rather extended.

Paul's theological case against this position, developed most fully in Galatians and Romans, rested on a reading of Abraham as justified by faith prior to and independent of his circumcision (Genesis 15 preceding Genesis 17 in the narrative sequence Paul exploits), and on a broader argument that the Mosaic law, while holy and given by God, was never intended as the means of justification before God, and that its temporary custodial function (Paul's image in Galatians is of a *paidagōgos*, a slave entrusted with supervising children) had reached its intended terminus with the coming of the Messiah, in whom Jew and Gentile alike were now justified by faith and incorporated into a single body without ethnic distinction. This argument needs to be read, as with the doctrinal controversies treated later in this overview, with both its theological substance and its practical institutional stakes held together rather than separated: the question of whether Gentile converts required circumcision was not an abstract point of theology but a determinant of whether the rapidly growing Gentile wing of the movement — by the time of Paul's later letters, plausibly already outnumbering the Jewish-Christian core — would be admitted on equal terms or treated as second-class adherents, and of whether the movement's centre of institutional gravity would remain anchored in Jerusalem under James's leadership or shift toward the diaspora communities Paul was establishing across Asia Minor, Macedonia, and Achaea.

The dispute came to a formal, though imperfectly resolved, head at what is conventionally called the Jerusalem Council or Apostolic Conference, attested both in Acts 15 and, in Paul's own first-person and arguably more historically primary account, in Galatians 2 — accounts that diverge in detail (Acts presents a more formal, council-like proceeding issuing a written decree with specific dietary stipulations for Gentile converts, while Paul's account in Galatians describes a more informal meeting with the Jerusalem "pillars" resulting in an agreement to divide the mission field, Paul to the Gentiles, Peter to the circumcised, with no mention of Acts' specific decree) in ways that have generated extensive scholarly discussion about which account better reflects what actually transpired, with many scholars inclined to weight Paul's contemporary, first-person testimony over Luke's later and more harmonising narrative where the two conflict. Whatever the precise proceedings, the outcome, on Paul's own account, was an agreement, however provisional and however differently each side subsequently understood its terms, that circumcision would not be required of Gentile converts — a victory for Paul's position, though one whose practical fragility is immediately illustrated by the incident Paul narrates directly afterward in the same chapter of Galatians, the confrontation at Antioch, where Paul publicly rebuked Peter for withdrawing from table fellowship with Gentile believers under pressure from "men from James," suggesting that the Jerusalem agreement, whatever its formal terms, did not settle the practical question of mixed Jewish-Gentile table fellowship to either side's full satisfaction and that the issue continued to generate friction well after any putative council resolution.

## The Shape of the Pauline Mission

Paul's missionary activity, reconstructed from the undisputed letters (the seven letters — Romans, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon — whose Pauline authorship commands near-universal scholarly assent, as against a further six letters traditionally attributed to Paul whose authorship is disputed to varying degrees, from the moderately contested Colossians and 2 Thessalonians to the widely doubted Pastoral Epistles of 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus, generally regarded by critical scholarship as later compositions written in Paul's name by disciples or admirers extending his authority to address later institutional concerns) and cross-referenced cautiously against Acts' narrative framework, took him across a wide arc of the eastern Mediterranean: through Asia Minor, into Macedonia (Philippi, Thessalonica) and Achaea (Corinth, and a brief, apparently unsuccessful stop in Athens), establishing small house-church communities typically meeting in the homes of relatively well-off patrons able to provide space for the group, and maintaining contact with these communities afterward through the letters that constitute the bulk of his surviving corpus.

These letters are not systematic theological treatises composed at leisure but occasional documents responding to specific crises and questions within particular communities — Corinthian factionalism and sexual ethics, Galatian susceptibility to rival "Judaising" missionaries, Thessalonian anxiety about the fate of believers who had died before an apparently imminently expected return of Christ — and this occasional character needs to be borne in mind when extracting from them anything resembling systematic doctrine, a task subsequent centuries of theological reflection, treated in Part II of this overview, would undertake far more deliberately than Paul himself did. What does emerge consistently across the genuinely Pauline corpus is a christology centred on the cross and resurrection as the decisive eschatological event inaugurating a new age, a soteriology of justification by grace through faith rather than by works of the law (a formulation whose precise meaning, particularly the contested phrase "works of the law" — whether referring to the Mosaic law as a whole or, as the "New Perspective on Paul" associated particularly with E. P. Sanders and N. T. Wright from the late 1970s onward has influentially argued, more specifically to boundary-marking observances like circumcision, dietary law, and Sabbath that distinguished Jew from Gentile — remains one of the most actively contested questions in contemporary Pauline scholarship, with significant implications for how later Reformation-era readings of Paul, treated in Part VI of this overview, relate to Paul's own first-century concerns), and an ecclesiology of the community as the "body of Christ," united across ethnic, social, and — in his most striking formulations, even if imperfectly realised in practice within his own communities — gendered lines.

Paul's eventual fate is not narrated within the New Testament itself; Acts ends with Paul under house arrest in Rome around 60–62 CE, and later, non-canonical Christian tradition (1 Clement, writing from Rome around 96 CE, and later patristic testimony) places his martyrdom in Rome, conventionally associated with the Neronian persecution following the fire of 64 CE, though the precise date and circumstances cannot be established with the same documentary confidence as the earlier phases of his career. By the time of his death, however the exact circumstances are reconstructed, the movement he had done more than any other individual to shape had already established itself as a network of Gentile-inclusive communities stretching from Judaea to Rome itself — a geographical and demographic transformation whose consequences, both for the movement's relationship to the Roman state and for its internal self-understanding, form the subject of the chapter that follows.
