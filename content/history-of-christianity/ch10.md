# Chapter 10 — Constantine, Nicaea, and the Arian Controversy

## Constantine: The Conversion and Its Historiographical Problems

Constantine I, Roman emperor from 306 to 337 CE, is one of the most consequential figures in Christian history and one about whose inner life and actual motivations the surviving evidence permits less certainty than the confidence of subsequent Christian tradition might suggest. The broad outline of his relationship to Christianity is not seriously disputed: after his victory at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome in October 312, which secured his control of the western empire against his rival Maxentius, Constantine attributed his victory to the Christian God — the famous vision of a Chi-Rho symbol (the first two letters of *Christos* in Greek) before the battle, narrated in varying and not entirely consistent forms by Eusebius of Caesarea and Lactantius, the two near-contemporary sources for this event, constitutes the evidential basis for what later tradition crystallised into the canonical conversion narrative. He and his eastern co-emperor Licinius subsequently issued what is conventionally called the Edict of Milan in 313, a letter restoring to Christians full legal toleration and the right to worship freely, and returning confiscated church property — a document whose precise legal status and novelty have been debated by scholars (it was less an edict than a rescript following an earlier, partially overlapping toleration measure, and Licinius's subsequent relationship to Christianity became considerably more hostile before Constantine defeated and killed him in 324), but whose practical consequences for the church's legal position were profound and durable.

What cannot be established with anything like the same confidence is the nature of Constantine's personal religious conviction, and the older historiographical tradition of treating his conversion as a straightforward, sincere, and decisive personal religious experience needs to be qualified against the evidence rather than simply accepted or simply debunked. Constantine delayed baptism until his deathbed in 337 — a practice not without contemporary parallel, since baptism was understood to wash away all sin and some Christians deliberately deferred it to maximise its efficacy, but also a practice consistent with someone maintaining maximum flexibility in a still-religiously-plural political environment for as long as possible. He continued to use traditional Roman religious imagery and solar monotheist language on his coinage for some years after 312, a practice that can be interpreted as political gradualism, genuine religious syncretism, or evidence that his theological commitments were for some time less precisely Christian than later tradition represented. He presided over the Council of Nicaea not as a theologian but as a political manager, intervening to push for a resolution without demonstrating sophisticated personal investment in the doctrinal substance — behaviour more consistent with a ruler who needed the church unified and pacific than with one who had strong prior theological convictions about the specific questions at issue. The most historically responsible position is probably that Constantine's relationship to Christianity was genuine, in the sense that he evidently believed the Christian God had intervened on his behalf and felt real obligations of gratitude and allegiance in consequence, but also deeply shaped by the political and administrative imperatives of a ruler who needed a workable religious settlement for an empire in which Christianity was now politically advantaged but still numerically a minority of the population — a combination of conviction and pragmatism that resists both the hagiographical reduction to a saint and the cynical reduction to a mere political calculator, since neither position does justice to the complexity the evidence actually supports.

## The Church After Toleration: A New Set of Problems

The Constantinian settlement transformed the church's situation with extraordinary speed and in ways that created as many problems as it solved. Within a generation, a movement that had spent most of its history as a persecuted or legally precarious minority found itself the object of imperial favour, the recipient of state funding for building programmes that produced the great basilica churches of Rome and the Holy Land, and gradually possessed of a legal status that would eventually shade into something approaching state religion — a trajectory formalised under Theodosius I in 380, who by the Edict of Thessalonica made Nicene Christianity the sole legitimate religion of the empire and set in motion the legal marginalisation of both paganism and non-Nicene Christianity. The institutional consequences of this transformation were immense: bishops became figures of considerable civic as well as religious authority, recipients of legal powers (including jurisdiction over certain categories of civil dispute), social prestige, and access to state resources that their predecessors had not possessed; the church acquired property, building infrastructure, and administrative apparatus at a scale previously inconceivable; and the relationship between ecclesiastical and imperial authority, which Chapter 8 established had always been in some degree contested even in the adversarial environment of the persecution era, became newly and acutely complex now that the emperor was a Christian patron with interests in the church's internal affairs and the church was an institution with interests in imperial policy, a relationship whose long-term dynamics would generate the conflicts examined in Chapters 18 through 20.

More immediately, the church's new legal visibility and the emperor's personal investment in its affairs meant that theological disputes that might previously have festered in regional obscurity now became imperial problems requiring imperial solutions — and none more urgently than the controversy that had already been generating serious disruption in the Eastern churches before Constantine had even consolidated his control of the East in 324.

## Arius and the Controversy Before Nicaea

Arius, a priest in Alexandria probably of Libyan origin, began articulating the position that would bear his name around 318, in the context of a dispute with his bishop, Alexander of Alexandria, that started as a local Alexandrian controversy before spreading with remarkable speed across the Eastern church. Arius's central claim, reconstructed primarily from the fragments of his verse composition the *Thalia* and from his letters (since the heresiological tradition, with its usual thoroughness, ensured that relatively little of his direct writing survived intact), was that the Son of God, the divine Logos through whom all things were created, was himself a creature — the first and greatest of God's creations, existing before all else, pre-eminent above all other beings, but nonetheless brought into existence by the Father's will from a prior state of non-existence rather than coeternal and coequal with the Father. The formula most closely associated with the Arian position, even if its precise textual attribution to Arius himself admits some scholarly qualification, was that "there was a time when he was not" (*ēn pote hote ouk ēn*) — a formulation that, whatever its exact origins, captures the core of what was at stake: the Son's existence, on this account, has a beginning, even if that beginning precedes all created time as human beings experience it, and the Son therefore occupies a different ontological order from the Father, whose existence is absolute, underived, and without beginning.

Arius's position was neither arbitrary nor obviously self-evidently wrong from within the theological resources available in the early fourth century. It had substantial precedent in earlier theological trajectories — the "subordinationist" tendency visible in some earlier theological writers, including Origen, who had distinguished the Father and Son in ways that could be read as implying the Son's ontological inferiority (though Origen's complex legacy on precisely this point was itself vigorously contested), and a broader philosophical tradition in which the supreme divine principle was understood as absolutely simple, self-sufficient, and incapable of direct contact with the material world, requiring intermediary beings to function as cosmological bridges — and it had a certain intuitive appeal to monotheist sensibilities concerned that any full identification of Christ with the divine being would compromise the oneness and simplicity of God that Jewish and Christian theology alike required. Arius also enjoyed initial support from a significant number of Eastern bishops, including the influential Eusebius of Nicomedia (not to be confused with Eusebius of Caesarea, the church historian), who shared some of his theological commitments and who had sufficient political standing at the imperial court to ensure that the controversy could not simply be managed at the local Alexandrian level.

Alexander of Alexandria, and after him his deacon and eventual successor Athanasius, argued against Arius's position with a clarity that the subsequent tradition judged definitive: if the Son is a creature, then in worshipping Christ the church is worshipping a creature, which is simply idolatry; if the Son is not truly and fully God, then salvation — understood, in the Greek theological tradition above all, as genuine participation in the divine nature, *theōsis*, a real sharing in what God is rather than a merely external forgiveness of sins — cannot be accomplished through him, since only God can give what God is, and a created intermediary, however exalted, remains on the creature's side of the unbridgeable divide between Creator and creation. This soteriological argument — connected directly to the logic of Gregory of Nazianzus's "what is not assumed is not healed," discussed in Chapter 5 — was not merely rhetorical; it identified a genuine internal inconsistency in any position that simultaneously affirmed Christ as saviour in the full soteriological sense the tradition required and denied him full divine being.

## The Council of Nicaea, 325

Constantine, having defeated Licinius and assumed control of the Eastern empire in 324, found the Arian controversy already in full, disruptive swing — bishops in open conflict with one another, communities divided, and correspondence crossing the empire in volumes that threatened the administrative and political coherence he had just achieved at considerable military cost. His response was to convene, for the first time in the church's history, a council representative of bishops from across the entire Christian world, meeting at Nicaea in Bithynia in the summer of 325. The numbers who attended are variously reported in ancient sources, with the traditional figure of 318 bishops almost certainly a symbolic number derived from the 318 servants of Abraham in Genesis 14 rather than a literal headcount; modern estimates generally place attendance at somewhere between 250 and 300, drawn predominantly from the Eastern provinces with relatively thin Western representation, reflecting both the controversy's geographical concentration and the practical difficulties of travel across a still-vast empire.

Constantine presided at the opening sessions in person, an act of striking symbolic significance — a Roman emperor chairing a theological gathering — and the political register of his involvement was transparent throughout: his primary concern, repeatedly expressed in his surviving correspondence about the controversy both before and after Nicaea, was unity and concord rather than the resolution of any specific doctrinal point on its own merits, and he had initially hoped that Alexander and Arius might simply be persuaded to cease their public dispute rather than that one position would be formally condemned. When it became clear that the theological substance could not simply be wished away, Constantine threw his support behind the anti-Arian position, and specifically behind the adoption of the term *homoousios* — "of the same substance" — to describe the Son's relationship to the Father, a term that, as Chapter 5 noted, carried considerable theological weight but also a contested pre-history (it had been used by the heterodox Paul of Samosata and formally condemned at the Synod of Antioch in 268, a history that gave some theologically conservative bishops pause about adopting it even when they were not inclined toward Arius) and that appears to have been pressed by the Western bishop Hosius of Cordoba, Constantine's ecclesiastical adviser, as a deliberately unambiguous formula that Arius himself would find impossible to accept while subscribing to it in good conscience.

The council duly affirmed a creed — not the Nicene Creed as it appears in later liturgical use, which is the revised and expanded version adopted at the Council of Constantinople in 381, but the shorter Nicaean formula — that included the *homoousios* and anathematised, with explicit and specific force, the core Arian propositions, including the "there was a time when he was not" formula and the claim that the Son was created from nothing or from a different substance than the Father. Arius himself and the small number of bishops who refused to subscribe were exiled. The emperor had his unity; the church had its definition; and neither turned out to be as settled as either party might have hoped.

## The Long Aftermath: Arianism After Nicaea

What followed Nicaea was not the clean resolution the council's formal outcome might suggest but one of the most turbulent and prolonged doctrinal aftershocks in the church's history, extending across the better part of four decades and illustrating with unusual clarity the degree to which the theological and the political were inextricable in this period. The *homoousios* formula, precisely because of its unambiguity, proved divisive among bishops who were not Arian but who had theological reservations about a term absent from scripture, with its contested pre-history, and who were also uncomfortable with the philosophical implications of asserting identity of substance between Father and Son — a position critics associated with the older heresy of Sabellianism or modalism (the doctrine that Father, Son, and Spirit are not genuinely distinct but merely three modes of presentation of a single divine subject, which the *homoousios*'s unqualified use seemed to some to risk implying).

This discomfort generated, in the decades after Nicaea, a proliferating series of alternative theological formulae associated with what scholars have called the "Homoian," "Homoiousian," and "Anomoian" parties — distinguished by whether they wished to say the Son was "like" the Father (*homoios*), "of like substance" (*homoiousios*, differing from the Nicene *homoousios* by only a single iota, a distinction whose historical significance prompted the remark, attributed to Edward Gibbon and probably apocryphal but thematically apt, that the Christian world was convulsed over a diphthong), or simply "unlike" (*anomoios*) — parties whose shifting compositions and sometimes bewildering succession of synods, counter-synods, and competing creedal formulae across the 340s, 350s, and 360s were further complicated by the dramatically varying theological sympathies of the emperors who succeeded Constantine, since imperial favour still functioned as a major determinant of which theological position enjoyed official support at any given moment. Constantius II, Constantine's son and eventual sole emperor, leaned toward Homoian positions broadly sympathetic to Arian concerns, and it was under Constantius that the great champion of Nicene orthodoxy, Athanasius of Alexandria, endured the first of his five exiles — driven from his see by imperial pressure on five separate occasions across a career of extraordinary tenacity and combativeness, generating the biographical epithet *Athanasius contra mundum*, "Athanasius against the world," that subsequent tradition bestowed on him as tribute to his refusal to yield to emperors, councils, and majorities when he judged Nicene orthodoxy at stake.

The eventual triumph of the Nicene position owed as much to the theological work of the Cappadocian Fathers — Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus, whose contribution to clarifying the relationship between *ousia* and *hypostasis* and thereby dissolving the confusion between *homoousios* and Sabellianism was discussed in Chapter 5 — as to any direct suppression of Arianism within the empire, though the accession of Theodosius I in 379 and his decisive commitment to Nicene orthodoxy, formalised in the Edict of Thessalonica of 380 and confirmed by the Council of Constantinople in 381, which reaffirmed the Nicene formula in its now-standard expanded form, did supply the imperial authority that definitively ended the period of doctrinal oscillation within the Roman empire's boundaries. But Arianism did not thereby simply disappear: the Gothic, Vandal, Burgundian, and other Germanic peoples who entered and eventually dismembered the Western Roman Empire had been converted to Christianity in its Arian form by the missionary work of Wulfila in the mid-fourth century, during the period of Arian imperial favour, and carried this tradition with them into their new kingdoms, creating a situation in which the Catholic, Nicene populations of the post-Roman West found themselves governed by Arian rulers for much of the fifth and sixth centuries — a political-theological tension whose resolution, generally through the eventual conversion of the ruling groups to Nicene Christianity (the Frankish conversion under Clovis in 496/508 and the Visigothic conversion under Reccared in 589 being the most important cases), belongs to the narrative of Chapter 17.

The Arian controversy, read in full, is a case study in the impossibility of separating doctrinal and political history in the post-Constantinian church: the questions at issue were theologically serious and the participants in the debate, on both sides, believed them to matter enormously for the right understanding of God and salvation; and the outcomes at each stage — Nicaea's verdict, the decades of post-Nicene turbulence, the eventual Theodosian settlement — were shaped by imperial politics, episcopal alliance networks, and the personal religious sympathies of emperors in ways that cannot be reduced to either a purely theological history of ideas progressively clarifying themselves or a purely cynical story of power politics dressed in theological clothing. Both dimensions were real, and the controversy cannot be honestly narrated while ignoring either.
