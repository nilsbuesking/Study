# Chapter 18 — Iconoclasm and the Byzantine Crisis

## Images and Their Contested Place in Christian Devotion

The veneration of images — painted icons of Christ, the Virgin Mary, and the saints, along with sculptural representations in various media — had become, by the early eighth century, one of the most widespread and deeply embedded features of Christian devotional life across both the Eastern and Western churches, yet also one whose theological legitimacy had never been formally settled by any ecumenical council and whose critics could point to a genuine and serious scriptural and theological argument. The second of the Ten Commandments explicitly prohibits the making of graven images for worship — a prohibition that early Christianity had inherited from Judaism and had initially observed with some rigour, producing a largely aniconic tradition in the first two or three centuries that left relatively little representational art and whose traces survive in the discomfort about images expressed by several patristic writers. The gradual elaboration of Christian visual culture across the fourth and fifth centuries, accelerated by the church's new imperial status and access to Roman artistic traditions, and the parallel development of theological reflection on the icon that would eventually produce the sophisticated theological defences mounted by figures like John of Damascus, had not resolved the underlying tension but had rather raised the stakes of it considerably by the time the Byzantine imperial court moved against images in the 720s.

The theological question about images is more precise and more interesting than the simple "should Christians have pictures?" that a superficial account might suggest, and it deserves careful formulation before the political history is examined. The charge of idolatry — worship of a material object in place of God — was the most serious accusation brought against icon veneration, and the defenders of icons were clear that the charge needed to be answered theologically rather than merely dismissed as a misunderstanding: the distinction they drew, and that the council of Nicaea in 787 would eventually enshrine as dogma, was between *latreia* (the worship of adoration properly owed to God alone) and *proskynesis* or *timē* (the veneration or honour appropriate to a holy person or sacred object, such as a king or a holy relic, which falls short of the worship owed to God and does not compromise monotheism). Venerating an icon, the defenders argued, no more constituted idolatry than bowing before a king constituted treating the king as God; the honour was directed through the material image to the holy person it represented, in the same way that a letter addressed to a person is directed through the material paper to the person named, not to the paper itself.

The positive theological case for the icon went further and connected directly to the Christological controversies of the previous century: if the Incarnation was genuine — if the eternal Son of God had truly and fully become material, embodied, and visually perceptible in Jesus of Nazareth — then the depiction of Christ in material form was not a violation of the divine transcendence but a consequence and celebration of the Incarnation's reality. An iconoclasm that refused to depict Christ on the grounds that divinity could not be represented in material form was, on this argument, implicitly docetic — implying that Christ's human, perceptible nature was not genuinely material or that his divine and human natures could be separated in such a way that the perceptible human could not be depicted without claiming to depict the impassible divine. John of Damascus, writing from the relative safety of Muslim-ruled Palestine where Byzantine imperial edicts had no force, and who constitutes the most theologically sophisticated defender of icon veneration in the pre-conciliar phase of the controversy, pressed this Christological argument with particular precision: the Incarnation had changed the conditions of representation irreversibly, and a Christianity that refused material depiction of Christ was, in refusing it, implicitly denying the completeness of what the Incarnation claimed.

## The Origins of Imperial Iconoclasm

The specific trigger of the iconoclast crisis as an imperial policy matter remains to some degree debated, and the older historiographical tradition's tendency to attribute it primarily to Islamic and Jewish influence on the Byzantine emperor Leo III — the argument being that Islamic monotheism's fierce aniconism and Jewish anti-image polemic had shamed Christians into recognising the idolatrous character of their own practice — has been substantially qualified by more recent scholarship, which while not dismissing these influences as irrelevant locates the crisis more firmly in internal Byzantine religious and political dynamics. Significant volcanic activity in the Aegean (the catastrophic eruption of Thera around 726), epidemic disease, and a string of military reverses against Islamic forces that had taken Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and North Africa from Byzantine control in the previous century all contributed to a climate of anxiety about divine favour that made the question of whether the Christian empire's devotional practices were pleasing or offensive to God a pressing political as much as theological question.

Leo III, emperor from 717 to 741, began his iconoclast campaign with a series of measures in the late 720s — the removal or whitewashing of prominent icons in Constantinople, culminating in the destruction of a large and beloved image of Christ above the Chalke Gate of the imperial palace in 730 that provoked significant popular and clerical resistance — and issued a formal edict against icon veneration in 730, which he followed by attempting to enforce in the Western territories of the empire, including Italy, where Pope Gregory II and his successor Gregory III mounted fierce opposition that included a Roman synod condemning iconoclasm in 731. Leo's response to papal opposition was to attempt to arrest Gregory III (unsuccessfully) and to strip the papacy of its jurisdictions in southern Italy, Sicily, and Illyricum, transferring them to the patriarchate of Constantinople — a punitive ecclesiastical measure with lasting consequences for the territorial and institutional balance between Rome and Constantinople that would contribute directly to the eventual Schism examined in Chapter 19.

The theological arguments Leo and the iconoclasts advanced were not merely cynical rationalisations for a politically motivated policy, even if political motivations were also real: the iconoclast position had genuine theological content, rooted in the scriptural prohibition of images, in a theology of divine transcendence that emphasised God's absolute otherness from the material world, and in a Christological argument of their own — the "iconoclast dilemma" — which held that any attempt to depict Christ was necessarily either a Nestorian error (if the artist claimed to depict only his human nature, separating it from his divine nature in a way Chalcedon's single-person formula ruled out) or a monophysite error (if the artist claimed to depict his whole person, thereby claiming to circumscribe the infinite divine in a finite material image). This argument was theologically serious enough that its refutation required the sustained Christological analysis that John of Damascus and later the council of Nicaea provided, rather than merely the assertion of traditional practice.

## The Course of the Controversy

The iconoclast controversy unfolded across more than a century in two major phases, with an intervening period of icon restoration, and its course was shaped throughout by the intersection of genuine theological disagreement with the politics of imperial succession, military crisis, and the shifting relationships between the emperor, the patriarch of Constantinople, the monastic communities, and the Roman papacy.

The first phase, under Leo III and his son and successor Constantine V (741–775), was the more severe and more theologically elaborate of the two. Constantine V — an iconoclast of considerably more systematic theological conviction than his father, who developed the iconoclast position in a series of treatises (*Peuseis*) that survive only in the extensive quotations made by his opponents in the act of refuting them, a familiar irony of patristic controversy — convened an iconoclast council at Hieria in 754, attended by 338 bishops and claiming ecumenical authority (though neither the patriarchates of Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, nor Jerusalem were represented, a lacuna that the subsequent Nicene counter-council would exploit in denying Hieria the status of a genuine ecumenical council), which condemned the veneration of images in precise theological terms and began a programme of active persecution of icon defenders, directed above all at the monastic communities that had proved the most tenacious institutional resistance to iconoclasm. Monks were publicly mocked, forced to participate in humiliating processions, exiled, and in some cases executed; monasteries were dissolved or converted to secular uses; and the patriarch of Constantinople Niketas I was forced to sign iconoclast declarations that his predecessors had refused.

The restoration of icons came about through a combination of political change and the energy of the empress Irene, who served as regent for her son Constantine VI from 780 and whose personal commitment to icon veneration drove the convening of the Seventh Ecumenical Council at Nicaea in 787 — the Second Council of Nicaea, as it is distinguished from the First of 325. The council, attended by over 350 bishops and presided over by the patriarch Tarasius of Constantinople, developed the definitive theological framework for distinguishing veneration from worship, drew on the Christological arguments John of Damascus had developed, and restored the veneration of icons as legitimate and indeed obligatory Christian practice, anathematising the iconoclasts' position and reversing the decisions of the 754 Hieria council. The council's decisions were accepted in the East and in Rome — whose legates attended and subscribed — though Charlemagne's theologians at the Frankish court, working from a Latin translation of the council's acts that was in places genuinely misleading and that in other places reflected a genuinely different Western theological sensibility about images (the Frankish *Libri Carolini*, composed in response, rejected both iconoclasm and what they read as the council's excessive elevation of image veneration, in a position that reflected a Western theological culture less invested in the icon theology the Greek East had developed), declined to endorse it — a reminder that "East" and "West" were already operating with sufficiently different theological frameworks that even a formally ecumenical council's reception was uneven across the two traditions.

A second, briefer phase of iconoclasm (814–842), associated with the emperors Leo V, Michael II, and Theophilos, was ended definitively by the empress Theodora (wife of Theophilos and regent for her son Michael III), who restored icon veneration in 843 in what the Orthodox church commemorates annually as the "Triumph of Orthodoxy," celebrated on the first Sunday of Lent in the Orthodox liturgical calendar to the present day — a commemoration that has made the iconoclast crisis one of the most liturgically vivid episodes in Orthodox historical memory.

## Monasticism, Resistance, and the Reconfiguration of Authority

One of the most significant and least adequately recognised consequences of the iconoclast crisis for the subsequent institutional history of Byzantine Christianity was the reconfiguration of the relationship between monastic communities, the imperial church, and the patriarchate of Constantinople that the crisis produced. The monks — whose communities had been the primary institutional site of resistance to iconoclasm in both its phases, and who had suffered the most severe persecution as a consequence — emerged from the controversy with their moral authority enormously enhanced and with a self-understanding of the monastic vocation as guardians of true orthodoxy against imperial and even patriarchal betrayal that positioned them as a structural check on both episcopal and imperial authority that they had not previously exercised. The Stoudite monastery of Constantinople, whose abbot Theodore of Stoudios had been among the most vocal and courageous opponents of iconoclasm in the second phase, became in the ninth century one of the most influential institutions in Byzantine Christianity, and Theodore's writings on monastic independence from imperial and patriarchal interference set a precedent for monastic claims to a sphere of authority and conscience independent of secular and hierarchical control that would recur as a theme across subsequent Byzantine history.

## Iconoclasm and the Estrangement of Rome

The iconoclast crisis accelerated the estrangement between Rome and Constantinople in ways whose significance for the eventual Schism of 1054 cannot be overstated, even if iconoclasm itself was not the precipitating cause of that Schism. Leo III's punitive response to papal opposition — the transfer of ecclesiastical jurisdictions in southern Italy and Illyricum to Constantinople — had direct and lasting institutional consequences: the Illyrian churches, which had been under Roman jurisdiction and provided Rome with a significant source of income and influence in the Balkans, were removed from the Roman orbit and placed under Constantinople, a reorganisation that Rome never formally accepted and that contributed to the simmering jurisdictional disputes that the ninth-century Bulgarian mission would further inflame. The papacy's experience of imperial iconoclast pressure reinforced its existing and growing inclination to seek alternative secular protectors in the West — a logic that drove Gregory III's appeal to the Lombards and, eventually, the fateful alliance between the papacy and the Carolingian Franks that produced Charlemagne's coronation of 800 examined in Chapter 17, an event that from the Byzantine perspective constituted an intolerable usurpation of the imperial title that belonged to the emperor in Constantinople and that deepened the estrangement between the two Christian centres in ways from which, as the following chapter will show, the relationship never fully recovered.

The iconoclast controversy thus illustrates, with unusual clarity, the thesis this overview has been pressing throughout Part III and Part IV: that the great crises of early Christian history cannot be understood either as purely theological disputes that happen to have political consequences or as purely political conflicts dressed in theological language, but as events in which the theological and the political dimensions were genuinely and inextricably interwoven, each generating the other in ways that require both to be taken seriously if either is to be understood at all. With iconoclasm resolved and its resolution embedded in the liturgical life of Eastern Orthodoxy, the following chapter turns to the event that definitively separated what had been, despite all tensions and divergences, a single Christian communion into the two great traditions — Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic — that remain distinct to this day.
