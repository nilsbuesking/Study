# Chapter 29 — Calvin, Zwingli, and the Reformed Tradition

## Zwingli: The Swiss Reformation and Its Distinct Character

The Reformation in the Swiss Confederation developed independently of and in some respects prior to Luther's German movement, and its leading figure, Huldrych Zwingli (1484–1531), deserves treatment on his own terms rather than merely as a precursor to Calvin. Zwingli was a humanist-trained parish priest who had arrived at his reforming positions through a combination of Erasmian biblical humanism, his own lectio continua preaching through the New Testament at the Grossmünster in Zürich from 1519, and a personal theology that diverged from Luther's in characteristic and consequential ways even where it arrived at many of the same institutional conclusions about the need to reform the church.

Zwingli's reforming programme in Zürich, conducted from 1519 onward with the active support of the Zürich city council in a model of civic-ecclesiastical partnership that would prove more typical of Swiss and south German urban Reformations than of the Lutheran territorial model, moved rapidly through the abolition of the mass, the removal of images and organs from churches, the dissolution of monasteries, and the establishment of a reformed church under close civic oversight. The speed and thoroughness of the Zürich reformation reflected both Zwingli's personal decisiveness and the specific political character of Swiss civic life, in which the city council's authority over religious as well as civic affairs was well established and in which the absence of a powerful territorial prince meant that reform could be pursued through civic rather than princely machinery.

Zwingli's theology differed from Luther's in ways that were partly temperamental and partly substantive. Where Luther retained a strong sense of the continuity between law and gospel, old covenant and new, and insisted on a genuine though differently understood real presence in the Eucharist, Zwingli's theology was characterised by a sharper spiritualism — a tendency to emphasise the radical distinction between the spiritual and the material, between the inner reality and the outer sign — that had roots both in his Erasmian humanist formation and in a theological instinct quite different from Luther's own. This spiritualist tendency had practical consequences for worship — Zwingli's Zürich stripped churches of images, music, and ceremony to a degree that Luther found excessive and theologically mistaken — and decisive consequences for the Eucharistic controversy that would permanently divide the Lutheran and Reformed traditions.

## The Marburg Colloquy and the Eucharistic Division

The Colloquy of Marburg in October 1529 — arranged by the Landgrave Philip of Hesse, who had political and strategic reasons for wanting the German and Swiss Protestant movements to present a united front — brought Luther and Zwingli together with their respective theological allies for the most important intra-Protestant theological debate of the Reformation century. The encounter produced agreement on fourteen of the fifteen articles under discussion and permanent, irreconcilable disagreement on the fifteenth: the presence of Christ in the Eucharist.

Luther's position, as examined in Chapter 6, was a strong real-presence doctrine — sacramental union, in which the body and blood of Christ were genuinely and bodily present in and with the bread and wine, "in, with, and under" the eucharistic elements, even if he rejected transubstantiation's specific Aristotelian philosophical account of how. The words of institution — "this is my body" — meant what they said, and Luther's insistence on the literal force of *est* ("is") was uncompromising: Christ's body was genuinely, corporally, sacramentally present at the altar wherever the words were spoken and the sacrament rightly administered.

Zwingli's position was diametrically opposed. His reading of John 6:63 — "the flesh profiteth nothing; it is the spirit that giveth life" — convinced him that any doctrine of bodily, material presence in the Eucharist was a category error, confusing the spiritual reality that the sacrament signified with the material elements that served as its sign. The eucharistic words of institution were, on his reading, figurative: "this is my body" meant "this signifies my body," the bread serving as a memorial sign of the body given in the Incarnation and on the cross, the rite as a whole functioning as a communal act of commemoration and confession of faith rather than as a vehicle of Christ's real, bodily presence. The Eucharist was, for Zwingli, a pledge, a memorial, a sign — spiritually powerful in its capacity to evoke faith and unite the community, but not a vehicle of objective, presence-independent sacramental grace conveyed through the physical elements.

Luther's response to Zwingli's position was contemptuous and uncompromising. At Marburg, after Zwingli had developed his symbolic interpretation at length, Luther — who had spent the meeting writing *Hoc est corpus meum* ("this is my body") in chalk on the table and periodically returning to it as his non-negotiable position — declared that Zwingli was of a different spirit and that agreement was impossible. The conference ended with a statement of the areas of agreement and a mutual acknowledgement, in carefully diplomatic language, of the disagreement on the Eucharist: the two movements had been confirmed as theologically distinct rather than merely organisationally separate, and the division proved lasting. Lutheran and Reformed Protestantism would remain separate confessional traditions, unable to achieve sacramental communion with each other, a division that persists in some forms to the present day and that had immediate practical consequences for the political-military alliance Philip of Hesse had hoped to forge.

Zwingli's own career ended in 1531 on the battlefield of Kappel am Albis, where he was killed fighting alongside the Zürich forces against the Catholic cantons of the Swiss Confederation in the second Kappel War — a death that illustrates both the civic character of the Swiss Reformation (Zwingli went to battle as a civic chaplain serving his city's military force) and the degree to which Swiss confessional conflict had become military conflict within a generation of the Reformation's beginning. He was succeeded in Zürich by Heinrich Bullinger, whose long tenure and extensive correspondence with Reformed churches across Europe made Zürich a significant second centre of Reformed influence alongside Geneva.

## John Calvin: Theology and Institution

John Calvin (1509–1564) was a French humanist-trained lawyer who underwent a conversion to evangelical Christianity at some point in the early 1530s, fled France following the persecution of evangelicals that began in 1534, and arrived in Geneva in 1536 essentially by accident — Guillaume Farel, the reformer who had already begun the transformation of Geneva toward Protestantism, accosted him as he passed through and, deploying what Calvin later described as a terrible imprecation, demanded that he stay and help build the Genevan church. Calvin's Geneva, the institutional experiment that would prove the most influential single model for the Reformed tradition internationally, was the product of this accidental beginning worked out over nearly three decades of intense and frequently contentious labour.

Calvin's theological programme was at once more systematic and more pastorally focused than Zwingli's, and the *Institutio Christianae Religionis* (Institutes of the Christian Religion), first published in 1536 and substantially expanded through several editions culminating in the definitive 1559 version, was the most comprehensive and most rigorously organised statement of Reformed theology produced in the sixteenth century — a work that combined biblical exegesis, polemical engagement with both Catholic and Anabaptist opponents, and systematic dogmatic theology in a single, architecturally impressive structure. Calvin's theological starting point was the sovereignty of God — the absolute priority of divine glory over every other consideration in the ordering of theology, ethics, and institutional life — from which followed, with the logical consistency that was Calvin's characteristic intellectual virtue, the doctrines of grace, predestination, and vocation that gave Calvinist culture its distinctive moral seriousness and its tendency toward the transformation of social and political life in the light of divine command.

Calvin's doctrine of predestination — the conviction, shared with Luther's *De Servo Arbitrio* but developed with more systematic rigour and considerably less existential discomfort — held that God from eternity elects some human beings to salvation and others to damnation, entirely according to divine will and entirely independently of any foreseen human merit or response, and that this double predestination (the term, though associated with Calvin, was in fact used sparingly in the *Institutes* themselves and pressed further by subsequent Reformed orthodoxy than Calvin himself always pushed it) was not an embarrassing implication to be softened but a doctrine to be proclaimed as a manifestation of divine sovereignty and a pastoral comfort to the elect, who could be assured that their salvation rested on God's immutable decree rather than on the fragility of their own faith and works. The pastoral comfort argument was, as Calvin recognised, in tension with the obvious question of how anyone could know whether they were among the elect — a question subsequent Reformed theology addressed through the doctrine of the "marks of election" and the "third use of the law," and that in its social expression generated the intense self-examination and disciplined moral culture that sociologists from Max Weber onward have associated with the "Protestant ethic."

Calvin's Eucharistic position occupied a middle ground between Luther and Zwingli that each of them found in different ways unsatisfactory but that ultimately defined the mainstream Reformed position: Christ was genuinely present in the Eucharist, truly given and truly received by faith — not merely commemorated — but his presence was spiritual rather than bodily, received through the action of the Holy Spirit who lifts the believing communicant to where the glorified, ascended, and therefore bodily absent Christ is, rather than through any local, material presence of Christ's body in the eucharistic elements. This "virtualist" or "pneumatic" real-presence doctrine was genuinely distinct from both Zwinglian memorialism and Lutheran bodily presence, and it remained the characteristic Reformed position even as subsequent Calvinist churches developed varying emphases within it.

## Geneva as Laboratory: Discipline, Consistory, and Christian Society

The institutional experiment of Calvin's Geneva is as important for understanding the Reformed tradition as his theology, and the two cannot be separated: the *Institutes* and the Geneva consistory were, for Calvin, expressions of the same theological vision of a community ordered according to God's word and God's law.

Geneva in the 1540s and 1550s was a city of some thirteen thousand inhabitants that Calvin attempted to make into a visible realisation of the Reformed Christian community — not a theocracy in the sense of clerical rule (Calvin always maintained the formal distinction between the church's spiritual authority and the city council's civil authority, and was frequently in conflict with the council over who had the final word), but a Christian city in which civil and ecclesiastical discipline reinforced each other in the pursuit of a society visibly ordered according to the Word of God. The consistory — a body of pastors and lay elders whose establishment Calvin regarded as essential to the Reformed church's integrity — exercised a supervisory jurisdiction over the moral and religious life of the city's inhabitants, summoning those accused of doctrinal error, immorality, absence from church, dancing, card-playing, or blasphemy, and imposing ecclesiastical censures ranging from admonition through suspension from communion to excommunication, the last being the point of maximum tension with the city council over who had the authority to impose it.

The case of Michael Servetus, a Spanish physician and heterodox theologian who had challenged Trinitarian doctrine in writings that had made him a target of the Inquisition in Catholic territories as well as of Reformed authorities, and who was arrested in Geneva in 1553 and burned at the stake in October of that year, constitutes the most morally uncomfortable episode of Calvin's Geneva and the one most frequently cited by critics of his regime. Servetus had been condemned as a heretic before his arrival in Geneva, and the Genevan council's decision to execute him — which Calvin supported, though he preferred beheading to burning and his advocacy on this point was unsuccessful — reflected both the theological seriousness with which anti-Trinitarian heresy was regarded and the degree to which the compelle intrare logic examined in Chapters 11 and 22 ran as a continuous thread through the Christian tradition's handling of doctrinal dissent, not — as some Protestant apologists have argued — a specifically Catholic failing from which the Reformation was immune. Sebastian Castellio's *Concerning Heretics* (1554), published in direct response to the Servetus execution and arguing for a principled toleration of heretical opinion on the grounds that doctrinal questions were too uncertain to warrant killing over them, represents the most significant early Protestant challenge to this tradition, and deserves recognition as a landmark in the longer history of the case for religious liberty.

## The International Spread of Calvinism

Calvin's Geneva was not merely a local experiment but a training centre and a model for Reformed churches across Europe, and the international spread of Calvinism from the 1550s onward — into France (the Huguenot movement), the Netherlands, Scotland (where John Knox, who had spent time in Geneva, implemented a Presbyterian settlement), England (where Calvinist theology shaped the theology of the Elizabethan church even where Presbyterian polity was resisted), Hungary, Poland, and eventually the American colonies — constituted the most significant geographical extension of the Reformation beyond the German-speaking heartland.

The characteristic institutional form that Calvinism developed — Presbyterian church governance, in which the authority of individual bishops was replaced by the collective authority of a graduated system of assemblies from local sessions through presbyteries to national synods, with lay elders participating alongside ordained ministers at every level — was not without precedent in Calvin's own Geneva (which retained a modified episcopal-style structure under the authority of the pastors' company), and its systematic development as a confessional polity owed as much to John à Lasco and the refugee churches of the mid-century as to Calvin directly. But it represented a distinctly different ecclesiological vision from both Lutheran territorial church governance and Catholic hierarchy, one in which the church's internal governance was ordered by the community of the faithful operating through elected representatives rather than by either episcopal hierarchy or territorial prince — a vision with political implications that extended well beyond the ecclesiastical sphere, and that contributed, across the seventeenth century, to the development of the constitutional and republican political theory that would prove consequential in both the English and the American revolutions.

The differences between the Lutheran and Reformed traditions — on the Eucharist, on predestination, on church governance, on the relationship between church and civil authority, and on the proper use of the arts and material culture in worship — were deep enough and institutionally established enough by the end of the sixteenth century to constitute, in effect, two distinct Reformation traditions rather than variants of a single one. The further diversification of Protestantism through the Anabaptist movements examined in the preceding chapter and through the specific circumstances of the English Reformation examined in the following chapter would add further streams to what had become, within half a century of Luther's challenge, an irreversible religious pluralism whose political management would require the catastrophe of the Thirty Years War before any stable settlement was achieved.
