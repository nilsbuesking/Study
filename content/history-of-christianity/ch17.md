# Chapter 17 — Conversion of the North: Franks, Anglo-Saxons, and the Carolingian Church

## Conversion as Political Event

The conversion of the Germanic peoples of northern and western Europe to Christianity is a process that resists reduction to either a purely religious or a purely political account, since in the societies and circumstances involved the two dimensions were not meaningfully separable. For the rulers who converted, Christianity was simultaneously a genuine encounter with a new religious tradition and a political resource of considerable value: access to the literacy and administrative culture of the Roman church, alliance with an institution that commanded loyalty across ethnic boundaries, a framework of kingship rooted in divine sanction rather than merely military dominance, and in the specific case of Nicene Catholic Christianity, a basis for alliance with the Gallo-Roman episcopate and the Roman population of their territories that conversion to the rival Arian tradition could not supply. For the church, conversion of the Germanic kings was simultaneously a genuine religious mission and an institutional opportunity: the extension of episcopal networks, the acquisition of patronage and protection, and the absorption of new and militarily formidable populations into a Christian order that the church increasingly understood itself to be building and defending. Neither the sincerity of the religious commitment nor the reality of the political calculation should be denied; they were typically simultaneous and interwoven rather than in tension, in a world where the boundaries between the sacred and the political that modern secular thought takes for granted had no meaningful existence.

## Clovis and the Frankish Conversion

The conversion of Clovis I, king of the Franks, to Nicene Catholic Christianity around 496–508 (the dating remains debated, with 496 the traditional figure and more recent scholarship tending toward 498 or 508 for the baptism proper) was the single most consequential act of conversion in the entire history of early mediaeval Western Christianity, and its consequences ramified across the following three centuries in ways that shaped the political and religious map of Europe more profoundly than any other single event in the period. The Franks were at the time of Clovis's conversion the most militarily powerful Germanic group in the former Roman West, controlling a territory centred on modern Belgium and northern France and expanding rapidly; Clovis himself was a ruthless and extraordinarily effective military commander whose career Gregory of Tours, writing in the late sixth century in his *Historia Francorum*, narrates with a mixture of admiration for Clovis's political and military accomplishments and theological satisfaction at the providential pattern he discerns in them — a narrative whose reliability as biography is qualified by Gregory's evident ideological interests but which remains the primary source for the conversion's circumstances.

The specific circumstances Gregory describes — a battle against the Alamanni in which Clovis, hard-pressed, appealed to the Christian God whom his wife Clotilda, already a Nicene Christian, had urged him to acknowledge, and subsequently attributed his victory to that God's intervention in a manner strikingly parallel to Constantine's Milvian Bridge narrative — are almost certainly stylised by the time they reach Gregory, and the precise theological understanding Clovis brought to his baptism should not be romanticised. What is not in doubt is the strategic significance of his choice of Nicene rather than Arian Christianity, since the other major Germanic rulers of the period — the Visigoths in Spain, the Burgundians, the Ostrogoths in Italy — had been converted to Arian Christianity in the period of Arian imperial favour in the fourth century, as noted in Chapter 10. By converting to Nicene Catholicism, Clovis aligned himself with the Roman population of his territories and with the Gallo-Roman episcopal aristocracy that provided the administrative backbone of what remained of Roman institutional culture in the West, against the Arian Germanic rulers who were perceived by that same Gallo-Roman episcopate as theologically alien and politically unreliable protectors. Avitus of Vienne's congratulatory letter to Clovis on his baptism articulates this alignment with striking explicitness, presenting Clovis's conversion as a providential event that would bring the whole Western world into Nicene orthodoxy under Frankish leadership — a prophecy that, within a generation, proved substantially accurate, as Clovis's military campaigns against the Visigoths and Burgundians were framed and experienced on both sides as partly confessional conflicts between Nicene and Arian Christianity.

The Frankish church that developed under Clovis and his Merovingian successors was from the beginning a church of intimate royal-episcopal interdependence, in which bishops were drawn from the Gallo-Roman aristocracy and appointed substantially under royal influence, held significant judicial and administrative functions in their cities, and provided the Frankish kingdom with administrative services — literacy, record-keeping, diplomatic correspondence — that the royal court could not supply from its own resources. This interdependence had consequences for ecclesiastical independence that later reformers, examined in Chapter 20, would regard as deeply corrupting: a church whose bishops were effectively royal appointees, whose property was frequently subject to royal exploitation, and whose canonical discipline was enforced only insofar as it suited royal interest was a church considerably more subordinated to secular power than its own developing theory of spiritual authority required, and the tension between this practical royal dominance and the church's theoretical claims to independence from secular control would generate the investiture controversy that Chapter 20 examines in detail.

## Ireland and the Peregrini: Monasticism as Mission

A distinct and in several respects contrasting model of Christianisation was provided by Ireland, which had received Christianity in the fifth century — traditionally associated with the mission of Patrick, a Romano-British Christian who had been enslaved in Ireland as a young man and returned as a missionary, probably in the second quarter of the fifth century — without the imperial or royal-conversion mechanism that characterised the continental Germanic conversions, and which consequently developed a form of Christianity with a distinctive institutional character rooted primarily in the monastic community rather than the episcopal diocese.

Irish Christianity organised itself around great monastic communities — Clonmacnoise, Iona, Bangor — whose abbots rather than bishops held the primary institutional authority, reflecting both the tribal rather than urban character of Irish society (the Roman episcopal model was organised around cities, of which Ireland had none in the Roman sense) and the enormous prestige that monasticism had acquired as the primary vehicle of Christian seriousness in the tradition the Irish had received. Irish monastic culture developed, across the fifth and sixth centuries, an extraordinary intensity of Latin scholarship, scriptural study, and manuscript production — the Book of Kells and the Lindisfarne Gospels are the most celebrated surviving products of a scriptoria tradition of remarkable artistic and technical achievement — alongside a penitential tradition that produced the earliest Irish penitential handbooks (*libri poenitentiales*), systematic tariffs of penances for specific sins that would profoundly influence the practice of confession across the whole Western church in ways examined in Chapter 22's treatment of the Inquisition's background.

The distinctively Irish contribution to the broader Christianisation of the North was the missionary movement of the *peregrini* — wandering monks who understood voluntary exile from their homeland as itself a form of ascetic offering, the "white martyrdom" of permanent displacement analogous to the "red martyrdom" of physical death and the "green martyrdom" of the desert — whose journeys brought Irish monastic Christianity to Britain, Scotland, and significant parts of the continental Frankish and Germanic world in the late sixth and seventh centuries. Columba, who founded the monastery of Iona off the Scottish coast around 563 and conducted extensive missionary work among the Pictish people of Scotland, and Columbanus, who crossed to the continent around 591 and established monasteries at Luxeuil in Burgundy and eventually Bobbio in northern Italy, were the most influential of these wandering missionaries, whose foundations became centres of literary and theological culture as well as religious life in the territories they entered.

## The Anglo-Saxon Mission and the Roman Model

The Christianisation of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms of England followed a different model and generated a different institutional outcome from the Irish mission, reflecting its different origins and its different relationship to the Roman episcopal tradition. Gregory I, bishop of Rome from 590 to 604 and a figure of extraordinary energy and administrative capability whose contributions to the papacy's institutional development extended well beyond the specific mission he initiated, sent the monk Augustine with a group of companions to the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Kent in 597 — a mission whose immediate success (the conversion of the Kentish king Æthelbert and the establishment of an episcopal see at Canterbury) was followed by a complex, decades-long process of extending Christianity across the other Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, marked by reversals, re-paganisations, and the competing influence of the Irish mission working southward from Iona and Lindisfarne.

The competition between the Roman and Irish missionary traditions in Northumbria came to a formal, if in practice less than fully decisive, resolution at the Synod of Whitby in 664, which addressed the immediately practical question of the correct method for calculating the date of Easter — a question whose apparent triviality conceals its genuine symbolic weight, since a kingdom whose king followed Roman practice and whose bishop followed Irish practice literally celebrated Easter on different days, a visible and socially disruptive expression of two incompatible ecclesiastical orientations — and found in favour of the Roman tradition. Whitby's decision aligned the Anglo-Saxon church firmly with Rome and with the developing Gregorian model of Christian organisation under papal authority, with consequences for the Anglo-Saxon church's subsequent development and, through its extraordinarily productive missionary activity on the continent in the eighth century, for the shape of Frankish and Germanic Christianity as well.

The Anglo-Saxon church that emerged from this process was in several respects the most vigorous and intellectually productive expression of Christianity in the seventh and eighth-century West: Bede's *Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum*, written at the monastery of Jarrow in the 720s and constituting the most sophisticated work of historical scholarship produced in the West between Eusebius in the fourth century and the twelfth-century Renaissance, is the most celebrated product of a culture of learning that also produced the Lindisfarne Gospels, the poetry of Cædmon, and a tradition of biblical commentary of genuine scholarly quality. The Anglo-Saxon missionary movement of the eighth century — above all the work of Boniface of Crediton, who evangelised large parts of Frisia, Thuringia, and Bavaria, reorganised the Frankish church under more direct papal authority, and was martyred by pagan Frisians in 754 — carried this Romanised, papally oriented Christianity into the Germanic heartland in a form that would prove decisive for the shape of Charlemagne's church a generation later.

## Charlemagne and the Carolingian Church

Charlemagne (ruled 768–814), king of the Franks and from Christmas Day 800 emperor of what subsequent historiography would call, with some anachronism, the Holy Roman Empire — the imperial coronation performed by Pope Leo III. in St Peter's in Rome, in circumstances whose precise orchestration and whose implications for the relationship between papal and imperial authority would be disputed almost immediately and for centuries afterward — presided over the most ambitious and consequential project of Christian institutional formation in the early mediaeval West. The Carolingian church that emerged from his reign was not simply the result of organic Christian expansion but the product of deliberate, administratively managed Christianisation conducted under royal authority and in close coordination with the papal church — a model of top-down religious reform and Christian civilisation-building that set the template for the relationship between church and state in the Latin West for the following centuries.

Charlemagne's religious programme had several interconnected dimensions. Missionary activity was conducted on the military frontier, most violently in the decades-long Saxon wars (772–804), in which the conquest and forced baptism of the Saxon peoples of northern Germany was pursued with a combination of military violence, legislative compulsion (the Capitulary of Paderborn of 785 made apostasy and refusal of baptism capital offences among the conquered Saxons, a measure of coercive Christianisation that shocked some contemporaries, including Alcuin of York, who protested that "faith is a matter of the will, not of compulsion"), and the subsequent establishment of episcopal sees and monastic foundations to consolidate the Christian presence institutionally. The Saxon experience represents an extreme case of the conversion-by-conquest model whose moral complexity the hagiographical tradition has not always confronted honestly — the Saxons were not invited to receive Christianity but compelled to accept it on pain of death, a method whose short-term institutional success was not accompanied by any very deep initial Christianisation, though the subsequent development of the Saxon church into one of the most vigorous in the medieval West suggests that institutional imposition, whatever its moral status, was not without long-term cultural effect.

Within the Frankish heartland, Charlemagne's religious programme focused on institutional reform and standardisation: the imposition of the Benedictine Rule as the standard for all monasteries, the reform of the clergy's education and moral conduct through the network of cathedral and monastic schools his *Admonitio Generalis* of 789 mandated, the standardisation of the liturgy on Roman rather than local Gallican forms, and the development of the Carolingian minuscule script — a clear, legible hand developed at the scriptorium of his court school and subsequently adopted across the Frankish church — which transformed the legibility and reproducibility of manuscript culture and, by making classical Latin texts more widely copyable and accessible, played a significant role in the transmission of classical learning that Chapter 16 identified as a structural feature of monastic culture.

The political theology of the Carolingian regime was articulated most fully by figures in Charlemagne's court circle — above all Alcuin of York, the Anglo-Saxon scholar who served as the leading intellectual of the court school — in terms that presented the Frankish king as a new David, a Christian ruler responsible before God not merely for the political governance but for the religious welfare of his people, whose obligation to enforce correct belief and practice was as much a royal duty as military defence. This Carolingian political theology constituted an extraordinarily high estimate of royal religious authority — the king as divinely appointed guardian of the church's reform and purity — that simultaneously elevated royal religious responsibility to an almost sacerdotal level and subordinated episcopal and ultimately papal authority to the royal reform project, in a tension whose implications would only fully emerge in the conflicts of the eleventh century, examined in Chapter 20, when papal reformers turned the logic of Carolingian Christian kingship against the specific practice of royal appointment of bishops that Carolingian practice had made central.

Charlemagne's imperial coronation of 800, whatever its precise orchestration — whether, as Charlemagne himself later claimed to Einhard's reportage, he was taken by surprise by the pope's action and would not have entered St Peter's had he known what was planned (a claim most historians regard as at minimum a considerable overstatement designed to preserve his political room for manoeuvre in relation to the eastern emperor at Constantinople), or whether the coronation was a product of prior negotiation — established a symbolic and institutional fact of enormous subsequent consequence: a Western emperor, legitimised by papal coronation, ruling a Christian empire in explicit succession to Rome, in the territories where no emperor had operated since 476. The tension between the imperial and papal authority this act simultaneously created and entwined — who owed what to whom, who had the right to crown emperors, what the church owed a Christian emperor who claimed to rule in God's name — would generate the most convulsive institutional conflict of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, in which the Carolingian model of intimate royal-ecclesiastical partnership would be challenged, reformed, and ultimately transformed into something its Carolingian architects had not anticipated and would not have recognised as the outcome of their project.
