# Annex C, part one — Christian Holidays in England, Ireland, and Germany

## Introduction

The liturgical calendar of Western Christianity is one of the most durable cultural inheritances of the faith — a cycle of feasts and fasts that has shaped the rhythm of daily and seasonal life across Europe for more than a millennium, and whose observance, even in increasingly secular societies, continues to structure the calendar in ways whose Christian origins are often no longer consciously recognised. This annex covers the principal Christian holidays observed in England, Ireland, and Germany, the three countries whose traditions are most directly relevant to readers of this overview, noting where observance differs between the predominantly Protestant traditions of England and northern Germany, the predominantly Catholic tradition of Ireland and southern Germany, and the mixed confessional landscape of Germany as a whole. Both the English and German names are given for each holiday; dates follow the Western (Gregorian) calendar used by Catholic, Protestant, and Anglican traditions alike since its gradual adoption across Europe from the late sixteenth century onward, noting where the date varies between fixed celebrations and those dependent on the moveable calculation of Easter.

The moveable feasts — those whose dates shift each year in relation to Easter — deserve a preliminary note. Easter itself, and all the feasts calculated from it (Ash Wednesday, Palm Sunday, Good Friday, Easter Sunday, Ascension, Pentecost, Trinity Sunday, and Corpus Christi), move each year within a fixed range, following a complex calculation whose origins lie in the early church's effort to align the resurrection commemoration with the Jewish Passover (which itself follows a lunar calendar) while keeping it on a Sunday. The Council of Nicaea in 325 established the principle that Easter should fall on the first Sunday after the first full moon following the spring equinox, with refinements that have produced a calculation giving Easter a range from 22 March to 25 April. All the moveable feasts ripple forward from that anchor.

## The Moveable Feasts: The Easter Cycle

## Shrove Tuesday / Fastnacht / Faschingsdienstag

**When:** The Tuesday before Ash Wednesday, seven weeks before Easter. Falls between 3 February and 9 March.

**Background:** Shrove Tuesday takes its English name from the practice of "shriving" — confession and absolution — in preparation for the Lenten fast that begins the following day. The day marked the end of the pre-Lenten carnival season (from Latin *carne vale*, "farewell to meat"), during which rich foods — eggs, butter, fat — that would be prohibited or forsworn during Lent were consumed in festive excess. In England, Shrove Tuesday is commonly called Pancake Day, the pancake being the traditional vehicle for using up eggs and butter before the fast; the tradition of pancake races, most famously at Olney in Buckinghamshire, is attested since the fifteenth century. In Ireland the day carries the same character, with pancakes standard and the evening frequently observed as the last occasion for festivity before Lent. In Germany the equivalent tradition, Fastnacht or Faschingsdienstag, is the culmination of the extended Karneval or Fasching season whose full intensity is concentrated in the days around this date — above all in the Catholic Rhineland cities of Cologne, Düsseldorf, and Mainz, where street processions, costume wearing, and the suspension of normal social hierarchies produce the most elaborate expression of the pre-Lenten carnival in the German-speaking world. The Alemannic tradition of the southwest, centred on Freiburg and Basel, observes the equivalent as Fasnacht with its own distinctive masked processions. In Lutheran northern Germany the carnival tradition is considerably more muted, consistent with the Reformation's general suspicion of pre-Lenten excess as an institutional indulgence, though the secular carnival culture of Cologne and the Rhineland has spread beyond strictly Catholic communities.

## Ash Wednesday / Aschermittwoch

**When:** Forty-six days before Easter Sunday (counting the Sundays, which are excluded from the formal Lenten fast). Falls between 4 February and 10 March.

**Background:** Ash Wednesday marks the beginning of Lent, the forty-day penitential season of fasting and self-examination leading to Holy Week and Easter. The imposition of ashes on the forehead — traditionally made from the burned palm branches of the previous year's Palm Sunday — is the defining ritual of the day, accompanied by the words "Remember that you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:19) or "Repent and believe the Gospel." The practice of public penance marked by ashes has roots in the Old Testament and was formalised in the Western church across the early mediaeval period; it is now observed not only in Catholic and Anglican churches but, since the later twentieth century, increasingly in Lutheran and other Protestant congregations that had previously abandoned it. Ash Wednesday is not a bank holiday in any of the three countries, but it is observed liturgically across Catholic, Anglican, and many Protestant churches, and in Ireland and southern Germany the attendance at Ash Wednesday Mass or service is among the more widely maintained of the Lenten observances. The forty days of Lent themselves, excluding Sundays, recall both the forty years of Israel's desert wandering and the forty days of Jesus's fast in the wilderness before his public ministry.

## Palm Sunday / Palmsonntag

**When:** The Sunday before Easter, one week before Easter Sunday. Falls between 15 March and 18 April.

**Background:** Palm Sunday commemorates the triumphal entry of Jesus into Jerusalem, recounted in all four Gospels, during which the crowd spread palm branches and garments in his path — a gesture of royal welcome rooted in Jewish tradition (2 Kings 9:13; 1 Maccabees 13:51). The day opens Holy Week, the most intensively observed week in the Christian liturgical year. The liturgical celebration involves the blessing and distribution of palm branches (or, in northern climates where palms are unavailable, pussy willow, yew, or box — the latter two being standard in England, the former common in Germany as Palmbuschen in Catholic regions), followed by a procession either outside the church building or within it, recalling the Jerusalem procession. In Germany's Catholic south the Palmsonntag processions in rural communities can be substantial communal events, with elaborately decorated "palm branches" — sometimes large constructions of woven greenery — carried to church for blessing. The Gospel reading at the Palm Sunday Mass shifts dramatically from the triumphal entry narrative to the full Passion narrative of Christ's arrest, trial, and crucifixion, giving the day its complex double character of royal welcome and approaching death.

## Maundy Thursday / Gründonnerstag

**When:** The Thursday of Holy Week, three days before Easter Sunday. Falls between 19 March and 22 April.

**Background:** Maundy Thursday commemorates the Last Supper — the final meal shared by Jesus with his disciples, during which the Gospels record the institution of the Eucharist and, in John's Gospel (not shared by the Synoptics), the washing of the disciples' feet. "Maundy" derives from the Latin *mandatum* ("commandment"), from Jesus's words at the Last Supper: "A new commandment I give you, that you love one another as I have loved you" (John 13:34). The evening Eucharist — often called the Mass of the Lord's Supper in Catholic tradition — is one of the most theologically rich liturgical celebrations of the year, ending in the stripping of the altar, the removal of all decoration, and (in Catholic and some Anglican churches) the reposition of the Blessed Sacrament in a place of honour for night-long adoration. A distinctive English tradition, preserved from the mediaeval practice of monarchs washing the feet of the poor, is the Royal Maundy — the distribution by the monarch of specially minted Maundy money to a number of elderly recipients equal to the monarch's age, now conducted each year at a different cathedral. In Germany, Gründonnerstag (literally "Green Thursday," the etymology of *Grün* disputed — perhaps from *greinen*, an old word for mourning, or from the traditional consumption of green vegetables) follows the same liturgical pattern. The Catholic tradition of the Rorate Mass (a Mass in the dark, with candles) observed in some German communities on this and other days of Advent is distinct from Maundy Thursday proper.

## Good Friday / Karfreitag

**When:** The Friday of Holy Week, two days before Easter Sunday. Falls between 20 March and 23 April. A bank holiday in England, Ireland, and Germany.

**Background:** Good Friday commemorates the crucifixion and death of Jesus, and is the most solemn day in the Christian liturgical year — the only day on which, in the Catholic and Anglican traditions, the Eucharist is not celebrated (though previously consecrated bread may be received at a Communion service). The "good" of Good Friday is generally understood as deriving from "God's Friday" (as in "goodbye" from "God be with ye"), or from an older sense of "good" meaning "holy" or "observed as a holy day." The Catholic and Anglican liturgical observance centres on the Veneration of the Cross — a simple but powerful rite in which a cross or crucifix is unveiled and members of the congregation approach individually to bow, kneel, or kiss it — alongside the reading of the Passion narrative from John's Gospel and a series of solemn prayers for the church and world. In Germany, Karfreitag ("Sorrowful Friday") is one of the most strictly observed public holidays: by law in most German Länder, dancing, amplified music, and theatrical performances are prohibited or restricted, a survival of the day's character as one of extreme solemnity. Ireland likewise observes the day with considerable solemnity, with the tradition of abstaining from meat (eating fish instead) widespread. In England, hot cross buns — spiced buns marked with a cross, whose sale was historically restricted to Good Friday and the surrounding period — are the most widely retained folk custom of the day, now commercially available well before Easter but still culturally associated with it.

## Holy Saturday / Karsamstag / Easter Vigil

**When:** The Saturday between Good Friday and Easter Sunday. Falls between 21 March and 24 April.

**Background:** Holy Saturday is the day of waiting — the day on which, in the tradition's theological imagination, Christ lies in the tomb while his disciples are scattered and grieving, a day of apparent divine absence between the death of Good Friday and the resurrection of Easter. It has no liturgical celebration of its own during daylight hours; the great celebration of the day is the Easter Vigil, held after dark on Saturday night (or in the early hours of Sunday morning), which is the most ancient and in many respects the most theologically layered liturgy in the Western Christian tradition. The Vigil begins in darkness with the lighting of a new fire and the blessing and lighting of the Paschal candle — a large candle symbolising the risen Christ as the light of the world — followed by the singing of the Exsultet, a long and extraordinarily beautiful Latin hymn of praise for the redemption accomplished in the resurrection. A series of Old Testament readings follows (traditionally up to twelve, though in practice often fewer), tracing the history of salvation from creation through the Exodus, the prophets, and the restoration of Israel. The Vigil is also the traditional occasion for adult baptism — the initiation of those who have been preparing throughout Lent for entry into the church, following a tradition continuous since the early centuries. In Catholic communities across Ireland and Germany the Easter Vigil remains among the most attended services of the year. Anglican churches have increasingly restored the Vigil to prominence following its virtual disappearance in the sixteenth-century Reformation, with many Church of England parishes now observing it as the centrepiece of their Easter celebration.

## Easter Sunday / Ostersonntag

**When:** The first Sunday after the first full moon following the spring equinox. Falls between 22 March and 25 April. A bank holiday (along with Easter Monday) in England, Ireland, and Germany.

**Background:** Easter is the central and foundational celebration of the Christian year, the commemoration of the resurrection of Jesus from the dead on the third day after his crucifixion. The entire Christian theological edifice — the Trinity, the Incarnation, the atonement, the sacraments, the church, and the hope of eternal life — is grounded, in Christian self-understanding, on this event, as Paul states with unambiguous directness in 1 Corinthians 15: "If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile." The English word "Easter" derives, via the Old English *Ēastre*, from the name of an Anglo-Saxon spring festival — a etymology first proposed by Bede in the eighth century and not universally accepted by modern scholars, since the evidence for a pagan goddess Ēastre beyond Bede's single mention is thin — while the German *Ostern* shares the same probable Germanic root. Most other European languages use a word derived from the Hebrew *Pesach* (Passover) — the French *Pâques*, Italian *Pasqua*, Spanish *Pascua* — reflecting the holiday's origins in the Christian Passover, the original Easter celebration being understood as the Christian completion of the Jewish festival of liberation. The Easter celebration in all three countries combines the solemn midnight or dawn liturgy with more festive customs: the Easter egg (whose symbolism of new life emerging from an apparently sealed tomb is ancient, though elaborated in many directions across different cultures), the Easter hare (the antecedent of the Easter bunny, originating in German folk tradition and brought to America by German immigrants), decorated eggs (*Ostereier*) hung on twigs and branches across Germany, and in Ireland the traditional Easter foods and the family gatherings that the end of the Lenten fast enables.

## Easter Monday / Ostermontag

**When:** The Monday after Easter Sunday. A bank holiday in England, Ireland, and Germany.

**Background:** Easter Monday extends the Easter celebration into the following day, reflecting the octave tradition — the ancient practice of celebrating major feasts across eight days — and commemorates, in the liturgical calendar, the appearance of the risen Christ to the two disciples on the road to Emmaus (Luke 24:13–35), a narrative of unrecognised encounter, shared table fellowship, and sudden recognition at the breaking of bread that has generated rich theological and artistic reflection across the tradition. In English folk tradition, Easter Monday was the occasion for various customs including egg-rolling (rolling hard-boiled eggs down hillsides, most famously at Preston in Lancashire and, in the most widely known modern survival, at the White House in Washington DC — a tradition introduced by American families of British and Irish descent). In Germany, Ostermontag is a full public holiday with the character of a family and spring celebration; in many Catholic regions the Emmaus processions (*Emmausgänge*) — walks in the countryside recalling the Emmaus road — are a traditional observance. In Ireland, Easter Monday carries the additional weight of the 1916 Easter Rising, in which Irish republican volunteers launched an insurrection against British rule on Easter Monday 1916, the choice of date deliberate in its evocation of resurrection and rebirth — a layering of political onto religious symbolism that has given the holiday a distinctive national-historical resonance in Ireland not shared by England or Germany.

## Ascension Thursday / Christi Himmelfahrt

**When:** Forty days after Easter Sunday, always a Thursday. Falls between 30 April and 3 June. A bank holiday in Germany; not a bank holiday in England or Ireland, though observed liturgically.

**Background:** The Ascension commemorates the departure of the risen Christ from the company of his disciples into heaven, as described in Luke 24:50–51 and Acts 1:9–11, forty days after the resurrection. The forty days echo the forty days of Lent and the forty years of Israel's desert journey — forty being the biblical number of period of testing, waiting, and transition. The Acts narrative describes the disciples watching as Jesus is "taken up" and a cloud receives him out of their sight, and two figures in white appearing to reassure them that "this same Jesus... will come back in the same way you have seen him go." Theologically, the Ascension marks the completion of Christ's earthly ministry and the beginning of his heavenly intercession at "the right hand of the Father" — the phrase from the Nicene Creed that describes his position as the exalted Lord and mediator between humanity and God. In Germany, Christi Himmelfahrt is a public holiday on which a tradition of outdoor walks and excursions (*Herrenpartien*, or "men's outings," a folk custom of uncertain origin sometimes interpreted as a displaced May Day celebration) has grown up alongside the liturgical observance — a characteristic example of the way German public holiday culture combines residual Christian observance with thoroughly secular recreation. In England the day is marked liturgically but is not a bank holiday, with Sunday the following weekend serving as the observed celebration in many Church of England parishes. In Ireland, Ascension Thursday is observed liturgically in Catholic churches.

## Whit Sunday / Pentecost / Pfingstsonntag

**When:** Fifty days after Easter Sunday, always a Sunday. Falls between 10 May and 13 June. Whit Monday is a bank holiday in Germany; the bank holiday was abolished in England and Ireland in 1971, replaced by the late May bank holiday.

**Background:** Pentecost (from the Greek *pentekostē*, "fiftieth") commemorates the descent of the Holy Spirit on the disciples assembled in Jerusalem, as described in Acts 2, ten days after the Ascension — an event understood across the tradition as the birth of the church and the inauguration of its public, apostolic mission. The Acts narrative describes the Spirit's coming as a sound of rushing wind filling the house, tongues of fire resting on each of those present, and the disciples speaking in languages understood by the cosmopolitan crowd of Jewish pilgrims assembled in Jerusalem from across the Roman world for the Jewish festival of Shavuot — which fell fifty days after Passover and which the Christian Pentecost deliberately echoes in its timing. Peter's subsequent sermon addresses the crowd, citing the prophet Joel's promise that God would "pour out my Spirit on all flesh," and three thousand are reported to have been baptised. "Whit Sunday" (White Sunday) derives from the white garments worn by those baptised at the Pentecost vigil — another ancient tradition, paralleling the Easter Vigil baptism. In Germany, Pfingstsonntag and Pfingstmontag (Whit Sunday and Monday) are major celebrations; the Monday remains a public holiday, and in many regions the Pfingstwoche (Whit week) was traditionally the occasion for outdoor festivals, markets, and the blessing of animals and fields in a combination of Christian observance and folk spring celebration. In Catholic communities the colour red is worn by clergy and often by congregations on Pentecost, representing the fire of the Holy Spirit.

## Trinity Sunday / Trinitatisfest / Dreifaltigkeitssonntag

**When:** The Sunday after Pentecost, fifty-six days after Easter. Falls between 17 May and 20 June.

**Background:** Trinity Sunday is the only Sunday in the Western liturgical year dedicated not to a specific event in the life of Christ or the church but to a doctrine — the doctrine of the Trinity, examined at length in Chapter 5 of this overview. The feast was introduced relatively late in the Western liturgical calendar, given formal universal observance only by Pope John XXII in 1334, though regional observance predates this; the Eastern Orthodox churches do not observe a separate Trinity Sunday, regarding every Sunday as implicitly a celebration of the Triune God. In the Church of England, Trinity Sunday was historically so significant as a calendrical marker that the Sundays following it through the rest of the liturgical year were numbered "Sundays after Trinity" — a practice still used in some Anglican and Lutheran traditions, though the modern Roman Catholic calendar uses "Sundays of Ordinary Time" instead. The feast is observed liturgically across Catholic, Anglican, and Lutheran churches in all three countries, without the public holiday status or distinctive folk customs that attach to the major feasts of the Easter cycle.

## Corpus Christi / Fronleichnam

**When:** The Thursday after Trinity Sunday, sixty days after Easter. Falls between 21 May and 24 June. A bank holiday in Ireland and in the predominantly Catholic German Länder (Bavaria, Baden-Württemberg, Hesse, North Rhine-Westphalia, Rhineland-Palatinate, Saarland); not a bank holiday in England or in the predominantly Protestant German Länder.

**Background:** Corpus Christi ("Body of Christ") is the feast celebrating the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist — the doctrine of transubstantiation examined in Chapter 6 — instituted as a universal feast by Pope Urban IV in 1264, at the instigation of Thomas Aquinas, who composed the feast's magnificent Latin hymns (*Pange Lingua*, *Tantum Ergo*, *Panis Angelicus*). The feast developed in the context of the high mediaeval theological elaboration of Eucharistic theology and the growing culture of Eucharistic adoration — the worship of Christ present in the consecrated host outside the Mass — and its characteristic observance is the outdoor procession in which the consecrated host, enclosed in a monstrance (an ornate transparent vessel), is carried through the streets accompanied by clergy, religious, and lay faithful, with outdoor altars decorated with flowers and greenery at which the procession pauses for prayer. In the Catholic German Länder — above all Bavaria — the Fronleichnam procession remains one of the most visually spectacular public expressions of Catholic identity in the calendar, with elaborately decorated streets, traditional costumes (*Trachten*), and the participation of civic as well as ecclesiastical authorities. In Ireland the Corpus Christi procession was until relatively recently a major public event, though its scale has diminished significantly with the decline of institutional Catholic practice since the 1990s. As a distinctively Catholic feast celebrating a doctrine explicitly rejected at the Reformation, Corpus Christi has no observance in Anglican or Protestant churches in England.
